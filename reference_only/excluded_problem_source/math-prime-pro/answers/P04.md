## 0. The mechanism I would add to the theory

The useful positive mechanism is **construction of additional arithmetic markers from a lattice of local jets and a real smallness certificate**. It is stronger than adding redundant equations to a cover. The new marker is forced to vanish at integer points in a specified real region because it is divisible by M and has absolute value less than M. It need not vanish on the positive-dimensional p-adic germ. That is exactly how it can provide information that the old marker lacks.

There are two substantial obstructions and one substantial improvement in the answer.

1. PROVED: two fixed linear observables of a primitive unit ray cannot reach kappa<d−1, the strict-exponent threshold for the same-degree Thue height bound requested in Q1. Their approximation exponent satisfies kappa>=d−1, and in the totally real cubic case it is strictly greater than 2. This is a spectral/product-formula barrier for a precise class of constructions, not the ordinary Liouville inequality and not a prohibition on every nonlinear use of an orbit.
2. PROVED: the obvious unit/Galois transformations of Thomas's marker plane are a finite symmetry group. Their action preserves an explicit quadratic height in the ghost exponents. There is a genuine cubic covariant quotient, but the quotient does not by itself decrease height or preserve all lifting conditions.
3. PROVED: the proposed square-root determinant width is correct for the crude balanced monomial basis. It is a poor estimate for the best basis. Once a Runge residual has been isolated, Hankel determinants of its Laurent coefficients give exact changes of basis with much smaller real determinants. For the S_63 evaluation, the required initial Hankel determinants are nonzero, and the resulting scale estimates are far below the raw billion-interval estimate.

The last improvement is particularly relevant to the programme. Hankel minors simultaneously describe Padé cancellation, the first nonzero real jets, and useful tau-type quantities. Their arithmetic must still be tracked, but a determinant-one change of basis does not incur a new denominator penalty when the original integer coefficient lattice is retained. The determinant method then supplies additional markers; the existing lifting cards can isolate their intersections.

PROVED below means that a proof is supplied. A finite exact-arithmetic certificate is explicitly identified and accompanied by reproducible code. TARGET and UNPROVED mark what remains. BELIEF marks the practical recommendation. Classical sources identify the surrounding methods; their deep global theorems are not used as unexplained inputs.

One transport issue remains essential for the method cards mentioned in the question. The doubled-state observable h(t)h(−t)−h(0)^2 is even, but a condition h(t)=h(0) alone does not imply its vanishing. It can be used for an original solution condition only after proving the additional transport implication, or when the condition itself requires the product. For example, h(t)=1+p*t*(t−1) has h(1)=h(0)=1 but h(1)h(−1)!=1. This does not challenge a certified lifting calculation for the product observable; it distinguishes that calculation from coverage of a different arithmetic property. The auxiliaries constructed below have their required transport implication built into their smallness/divisibility proof.

## 1. Q1: the precise barrier for self-supplied unit approximants

### 1.1. The first correction: a unit power is not normally a two-coordinate element

PROVED. Let K=Q(theta) have degree d>2 and let epsilon be an element of K of degree d. The powers epsilon^j do not all lie in the rational plane V=Q+Q theta. In fact no three consecutive powers can lie in V when d>=3 and epsilon has degree at least 3.

Proof. If epsilon^j, epsilon^(j+1), epsilon^(j+2) all belong to the two-dimensional Q-space V, some rational linear combination is zero. Divide by the nonzero epsilon^j. This gives a polynomial relation of degree at most two for epsilon, a contradiction.

The same proof applies to a fixed arithmetic progression epsilon^(c+Lj) when epsilon^L has degree greater than two. In a cubic field, a non-torsion unit has this property for every L>=1: if epsilon^L had degree less than 3, it would be rational; a rational algebraic-integer unit is ±1; then epsilon is torsion. A real torsion unit is ±1.

Consequently, “powers of epsilon give x_j−y_j theta with a small conjugate” is generally false. They give d integral coordinates. Requiring the other d−2 coordinates to vanish is already the original thin-orbit problem. It cannot be assumed when constructing a supposedly external sequence of approximants.

### 1.2. The honest intrinsic construction: two trace observables

Let K=Q(theta) be a number field of degree d>=2, with theta a real primitive element at a chosen real embedding sigma_0. Let epsilon be an algebraic-integer unit generating K. Write theta_i=sigma_i(theta), epsilon_i=sigma_i(epsilon) for all embeddings, counting complex conjugates separately.

Assume epsilon_0 is strictly dominant in modulus:

    Q_0=|epsilon_0| > |epsilon_i|  for i!=0,
    Q_0>1.

For B in K*, choose a fixed positive integer D clearing the denominators needed to make

    q_j=D Tr_K/Q(B epsilon^j),
    p_j=D Tr_K/Q(theta B epsilon^j)

integers for j>=0. Such D exists because epsilon is integral: first choose D with DB and D theta B algebraic integers.

Put B_i=sigma_i(B). Then exactly

    q_j=D sum_i B_i epsilon_i^j,

    theta_0 q_j−p_j
       =D sum_(i!=0) (theta_0−theta_i) B_i epsilon_i^j.

Every displayed residual coefficient is nonzero: B_i!=0 and theta_i!=theta_0 because theta is primitive and the field has characteristic zero.

This construction includes every pair of fixed rational linear observables on the coordinate vector of epsilon^j whose ratio tends to theta_0. Indeed, nondegeneracy of the trace pairing represents the observables as Tr(A epsilon^j) and Tr(B epsilon^j). Dominance makes their limiting ratio A_0/B_0. If this equals theta_0, injectivity of sigma_0 gives A=theta B. Fixed integer scale factors can be absorbed into A and B.

For completeness, trace-pairing nondegeneracy here has an elementary matrix proof. In the power basis 1,theta,...,theta^(d−1), the embedding matrix is the Vandermonde matrix V=(theta_i^j). Its determinant is a product of nonzero root differences. The trace-pairing matrix is V^T V, whose determinant is det(V)^2!=0. Therefore every Q-linear functional is a trace against some B in K.

An especially explicit coordinate version avoids choosing B or D. Suppose epsilon belongs to Z[theta] and theta is a root of a monic irreducible f of degree d. Write

    epsilon^j=sum_(r=0)^(d−1) c_(r,j) theta^r,
    s_1=Tr(theta),
    q_j=c_(d−1,j),
    p_j=c_(d−2,j)+s_1 c_(d−1,j).

Lagrange interpolation gives

    q_j=sum_i epsilon_i^j/f'(theta_i),
    p_j=sum_i theta_i epsilon_i^j/f'(theta_i).

Thus these are the trace observables above with B=1/f'(theta), and p_j,q_j are integers by their coordinate definition. The approximants come directly from the multiplication recurrence of epsilon.

### 1.3. Spectral rates cannot beat the field-degree threshold

PROVED THEOREM (unit-ray approximation barrier).

Under the preceding hypotheses, put

    rho=max_(i!=0)|epsilon_i|.

If rho<1, the construction gives explicit bounds

    |q_j|<=C_Q Q_0^j,
    |theta_0 q_j−p_j|<=C_E rho^j,

with, for instance, C_Q=D sum_i |B_i| and C_E=D sum_(i!=0)|(theta_0−theta_i)B_i|. The denominator is nonzero for every sufficiently large j, because q_j/(D B_0 epsilon_0^j) tends to 1.

Conversely, any uniform exponential bounds for this same sequence

    |q_j|<=C Q^j,
    |theta_0 q_j−p_j|<=C' E^(−j)

valid for all sufficiently large j, with E>1, necessarily satisfy

    Q>=Q_0,
    E<=1/rho.

In particular, the existence of such E>1 requires rho<1. Since epsilon is a unit,

    Q_0 product_(i!=0)|epsilon_i|=1.

Therefore

    Q_0 >= rho^(−(d−1)),

and hence the determinant-lemma exponent necessarily satisfies

    kappa=log(Q)/log(E) >= d−1.

Thus the P03 determinant lemma gives an irrationality exponent 1+kappa>=d and cannot by itself beat the degree-d approximation supplied by a degree-d Thue equation.

Precisely, this rules out the strict-exponent height comparison in P03 Corollary 6.5. At the boundary kappa=d−1, a separate favorable comparison of constants could still exclude a particular equation; the exponent theorem alone neither supplies nor rules out that additional gap. For the totally real cubic rays considered below the inequality is strict, so this boundary qualification does not rescue their large-denominator comparison.

Proof of the lower rate assertions. Dominance gives the lower denominator rate Q>=Q_0 immediately. For the error, write a_i=D(theta_0−theta_i)B_i for i!=0, and e_j=sum_(i!=0) a_i epsilon_i^j. The epsilon_i are distinct because epsilon generates K.

For every j, the vector of d−1 consecutive errors e_j,...,e_(j+d−2) is the product of the fixed invertible Vandermonde matrix (epsilon_i^r)_(0<=r<=d−2,i!=0) with the vector (a_i epsilon_i^j)_(i!=0). Inverting that fixed matrix proves

    |a_i epsilon_i^j| <= C'' max_(0<=r<=d−2)|e_(j+r)|
                      <= C''' E^(−j)

for each i. Taking j-th roots gives |epsilon_i|<=1/E. This proof allows complex phases and shows why accidental cancellation at isolated indices cannot improve an exponential estimate valid at every index.

Finally product_(i!=0)|epsilon_i|<=rho^(d−1), so 1/Q_0<=rho^(d−1). Taking logarithms gives the stated barrier.

Equality in the optimal rate bound occurs only when all non-dominant conjugates have the same modulus. In a nonreal cubic field the two conjugates form a complex-conjugate pair, so equality is possible. In a totally real cubic field a non-rational unit cannot have its two non-dominant conjugates of equal modulus: equality would make them equal or opposite. Equality contradicts separability. If they were opposite, the third conjugate would be their integer trace, a rational root of the irreducible cubic. Therefore the inequality kappa>2 is strict for a totally real cubic unit ray.

The theorem does not require the field to be Galois. Changing from a cyclic cubic to a non-Galois cubic does not remove this obstruction.

### 1.4. Fixed filters, other unit directions, and subsequences

PROVED. Replacing epsilon by any fixed product of known units, or by epsilon^L, does not remove the theorem whenever the resulting element still generates K. The product formula and the same residual modes apply anew.

Likewise, a fixed finite rational linear combination of shifted trace sequences is just

    sum_l b_l Tr(B epsilon^(j+l))
       =Tr(B P(epsilon) epsilon^j),  P(T)=sum_l b_l T^l.

If P(epsilon)!=0, none of its conjugates is zero, so the same theorem applies. A fixed rational polynomial cannot kill just the unwanted conjugates: if P(epsilon_i)=0 for one conjugate, the irreducible minimal polynomial of epsilon divides P, killing every conjugate.

A fixed arithmetic progression is another unit ray. It can change constants and remove signs but cannot improve the exponent ratio. For general degree, one must check that the power still generates K. For real cubic units this was proved in Section 1.1.

A unit from a proper subfield does not secretly supply a high-degree target through this construction. If E=Q(epsilon) is a proper subfield of K, transitivity of trace rewrites p_j and q_j as traces over E of C epsilon^j and D epsilon^j. The limit of their dominant-mode ratio belongs to an embedding of E. It therefore cannot equal a primitive degree-[K:Q] element of K. The relevant spectral dimension is the degree of the actual target field, not the size of an artificially enlarged ambient basis.

### 1.5. Thomas's family: the near-equality is explicit

Take a>=2 and theta the positive root in P03 Section 8. Write

    L=log(theta), M=log(theta+1), delta=M−L>0.

The unit eta=theta(theta+1) has conjugates

    eta_0=theta(theta+1),
    eta_1=−theta/(theta+1)^2,
    eta_2=(theta+1)/theta^2.

Here eta_0>1 and 0<|eta_1|<eta_2<1. The last inequality follows from theta>2 when a>=2. Thus the coordinate construction above produces honest rational approximants to theta with the exact optimal rates

    Q=theta(theta+1),
    E=theta^2/(theta+1).

Consequently

    kappa=(L+M)/(2L−M)
          =(2L+delta)/(L−delta)
          =2+3delta/(L−delta)>2.

As a increases this approaches 2 from above, since theta>a, delta=log(1+1/theta)<1/theta, and L>log a. Parameter separation can bring the construction arbitrarily close to the required cubic threshold, but it never crosses it.

The other simple unit theta+1 is worse: its conjugates have moduli theta+1, theta/(theta+1), 1/theta, so its exponent ratio is log(theta+1)/log(1+1/theta), of order theta log theta. Merely selecting the largest unit is not the right criterion. One must balance all contracting conjugates.

For the eta ray the secondary moduli are unequal, so the consecutive determinant p_j q_(j+1)−p_(j+1)q_j is nonzero for all sufficiently large j. This can be proved by expanding it into the three pairwise exponential modes. The pair involving eta_0 and eta_2 has strictly largest modulus and a nonzero coefficient; elementary real dominance then gives an explicit cutoff. Removing finitely many initial terms changes constants, not kappa. Thus the defect is the exponent itself, not failure to construct legitimate independent approximants.

More explicitly, for b_i=1/f'(theta_i), the determinant is a sum over i<l of

    b_i b_l (theta_i−theta_l)(eta_l−eta_i)(eta_i eta_l)^j.

Every coefficient is nonzero, and |eta_0 eta_2|>|eta_0 eta_1|>|eta_1 eta_2|. Factoring the first term and bounding the other two gives a fully effective nonvanishing estimate by the same cone-dominance argument as P03 Section 6.1.

### 1.6. This is not the bare Liouville inequality

PROVED DISTINCTION. Let theta be a real algebraic integer of degree d with other conjugates theta_i. For a rational p/q with q>=1 and |p/q−theta|<=1, the integer norm is nonzero and gives

    1<=|Norm(p−q theta)|
      =q^d |p/q−theta| product_(i!=0)|p/q−theta_i|
      <=q^d |p/q−theta| product_(i!=0)(1+|theta−theta_i|).

Therefore |p/q−theta|>=c(theta) q^(−d).

If q_j grows like Q^j and |q_j theta−p_j| is bounded above by C E^(−j), this norm argument only forces E<=Q^(d−1), equivalently kappa>=1/(d−1). It does NOT force kappa>=d−1.

The spectral unit-ray theorem forces the much stronger bound kappa>=d−1 because the same integer recurrence must retain all d−1 unwanted conjugate modes and their product is constrained by the unit norm. This distinction prevents reversing the exponent ratio by mistake.

There is also a universal determinant obstruction: if the consecutive cross determinants are nonzero and the P03 bounds hold, then kappa>=1. Indeed

    1<=|p_j q_(j+1)−p_(j+1)q_j|
      <=C_Q C_E (Q/E)^j (Q+E^(−1)).

If Q<E the right-hand side tends to zero. Thus a successful degree-d determinant proof needs the possible range

    1<=kappa<d−1.

It is the raw unit-ray trace construction that cannot enter this range for d>=3. Arbitrary rational approximants are not ruled out.

### 1.7. What extra structure could break the natural-class obstruction?

PROVED / CONDITIONAL DISTINCTIONS.

1. A large parameter, a different signature or a non-Galois field does not by itself help. For each fixed parameter specialization the theorem still applies to every primitive fixed unit ray and its fixed linear observables.

2. Hypergeometric or Padé approximants vary their auxiliary polynomial with j. They are not two fixed trace observables of one fixed unimodular matrix orbit. Their remainder identities and denominator estimates can therefore suppress the unwanted modes in a way excluded by the fixed-filter theorem. The existence of a parameterized field alone is not this structure; the required finite-sum identity, denominator control and determinant identity are the extra arithmetic input.

3. Index-dependent gcd removal is another logically possible escape, and should not be ruled out by the theorem. Suppose the raw integer p_j,q_j have an explicitly proved common divisor g_j with g_j>=c G^j, and after division retain nonzero consecutive determinants. Then the reduced approximants satisfy rates

       Q'=Q/G, E'=E G,

   provided Q/G>1, with adjusted constants. Their ratio is

       kappa'=(log Q−log G)/(log E+log G).

   This is below d−1 precisely when

       d log G > log Q−(d−1)log E.

   Establishing this persistent exponential divisibility is a substantial new arithmetic statement. The fact that the full multiplication matrix is unimodular preserves the gcd of the entire state vector, but does not automatically bound the gcd of two selected coordinates. Therefore do not assert such gcd growth is impossible without a separate argument. It is a concrete form that an intrinsic self-separation theorem could take.

4. Several changing unit directions or integer linear combinations whose coefficients depend on j may achieve cancellations not available to a fixed ray. But their heights, integrality and determinant nonvanishing must be proved. Searching for a direction where two contracting conjugates cancel merely transfers the original cancellation-wall problem unless an independent identity makes the cancellation controlled.

5. A new arithmetic property can force an approximation stronger than q^(−d). If a separate equation gives |theta−p/q|<=C q^(−D) with D>1+kappa, the intrinsic unit-ray approximants already give a height bound by P03. The barrier is relative to a bare degree-d norm equation. Additional independent restrictions can change the comparison exponent.

TARGET (intrinsic divisibility certificate). For a specific unit orbit and the explicit coordinate approximants above, prove a uniform lower bound for a common divisor g_j large enough to satisfy item 3, together with a closed formula for the reduced cross determinant. This would be a genuine orbit-supplied separator. It is not proved here; it is a precise alternative to the Padé route, not a consequence of having units.

BELIEF. For the Thomas evaluation the already documented Padé identities are the more credible short-term way to cross the threshold. The unit ray eta=theta(theta+1) is useful as a diagnostic: it identifies exactly how little exponential improvement is needed, but its limiting approach to kappa=2 cannot be mistaken for the strict inequality kappa<2.

## 2. Q2: descent, finite symmetries, and a genuine cubic quotient

### 2.1. Scope and verdict

PROVED. For Thomas's cubic, the natural transformations obtained by fixed unit multiplication, Galois conjugation, and invertible rational linear changes preserving the norm equation do not give an unbounded height descent on the marker locus. Their exact behavior can be classified. There is also a genuine explicit degree-three quotient to another genus-one equation, but its displayed height grows quadratically; calling the quotient a descent would omit the decisive height and coverage lemmas.

UNPROVED HERE. No claim is made that every rational correspondence, parameter-changing cover, or arithmetic branch-dependent descent is impossible. The no-go hypotheses below are explicit. They leave precisely those broader mechanisms available.

Throughout this note, t>=1 is an integer, a=t+1, and

    F(x,y)=x^3-t*x^2*y-(t+3)*x*y^2-y^3,
    f(z)=F(z,1).

Let theta be the positive root. The rational-root test gives irreducibility: f(1)=-2t-3 and f(-1)=1. The identity

    (z+1)^3 f(-1/(z+1))=-f(z)

shows that the other roots are sigma(theta)=-1/(theta+1) and sigma^2(theta)=-(theta+1)/theta. Thus K=Q(theta) is cyclic cubic, with sigma of order three. Put R=Z[theta] and L=Q+Q theta. The Thomas unit-coverage theorem of P03 applies for a>=16; the algebraic identities and no-go assertions below only need a>=2.

### 2.2. Fixed unit translations: a precise obstruction

PROVED THEOREM. Let theta have degree at least three over Q, and set L=Q+Q theta. If u is a nonzero element of Q(theta) with uL=L, then u belongs to Q. If u is a unit of an order containing theta, then u=±1.

Proof. Since 1 lies in L, u=r+s theta with r,s rational. Since u theta lies in L,

    r theta+s theta^2 belongs to Q+Q theta.

Linear independence of 1,theta,theta^2 gives s=0. A rational unit of an order is a rational algebraic integer whose reciprocal is also an algebraic integer, hence is ±1. In the cubic norm-one setting one can instead use Norm(u)=u^3=±1 directly.

PROVED FINITE-INTERSECTION REFINEMENT. In a cubic field, fix u in K* with u not rational. Then

    {alpha in L : u alpha in L, |Norm(alpha)|=1}

has at most two elements.

Proof. L and u^(-1)L are two-dimensional Q-subspaces of the three-dimensional space K. Their intersection has dimension at least one. They cannot coincide by the preceding theorem, so the intersection is a line Q v. Norm(qv)=q^3 Norm(v), so each of the equations Norm(qv)=1 and Norm(qv)=-1 has at most one rational q.

For Thomas's basis this is completely explicit. Write

    u=u_0+u_1 theta+u_2 theta^2.

Since theta^3=1+(t+3)theta+t theta^2, the theta^2 coefficient of u(x-y theta) is

    u_2*x-(u_1+t*u_2)*y.

Thus a fixed nontrivial unit multiplier can preserve the marker at a solution only on the rational line

    u_2*x=(u_1+t*u_2)*y.

Clear denominators and compute its primitive integer direction (r,s). Any integer solution of |F(x,y)|=1 is primitive, because a common divisor would have its cube dividing F(x,y). Consequently the only candidates on this line are (r,s) and (-r,-s), and direct substitution decides them.

If u_2=0 and u_1!=0, the line is y=0 and the only candidates are (±1,0). If u_2=u_1=0, the multiplier is rational and the preceding trivial case applies.

IMPLICATION. A finite list of fixed unit translations can be an excellent finite exceptional-point certificate. It cannot supply a nontrivial transformation of the whole marker curve: every nontrivial entry applies to at most two points. Normalizing a general unit by arbitrary powers of the two generators, as in P03, leaves the marker plane in general; that operation proves unit coverage and does not descend the Thue solutions.

### 2.3. All semilinear marker-preserving maps in the cyclic cubic

PROVED THEOREM. Consider maps on K of the form

    alpha -> u sigma^j(alpha),  j in {0,1,2},

with u in K*, |Norm(u)|=1. Suppose the map carries L to L. On the pair (x,y) defined by alpha=x-y theta, the map is one of

    ±I, ±R, ±R^2,    R(x,y)=(y,-x-y).

Proof. Because sigma^j(1)=1, the condition implies u=r+s theta.

For j=0, Section 2.2 gives u=±1.

For j=1, use

    u sigma(theta)=-(r+s theta)/(theta+1)
                   =-s-(r-s)/(theta+1),
    (theta+1)^(-1)=-theta^2+(t+1)theta+2.

The theta^2 coefficient is r-s. Hence r=s and u=r(theta+1). Since Norm(theta+1)=-1 and |Norm(u)|=1, |r|^3=1, so r=±1. Direct substitution gives

    (theta+1)sigma(x-y theta)=(x+y)+x theta,

which corresponds to (x+y,-x)=-R^2(x,y).

For j=2, use sigma^2(theta)=-1-theta^(-1), with

    theta^(-1)=theta^2-t theta-(t+3).

The theta^2 coefficient of (r+s theta)sigma^2(theta) is -r. Thus r=0 and u=s theta. Norm(theta)=1 gives s=±1. Now

    theta sigma^2(x-y theta)=y+(x+y)theta,

which corresponds to R(x,y). This exhausts the cases.

These maps satisfy R^3=I and F(R(x,y))=F(x,y). Simultaneous negation changes F to -F. Their finite orbits were used in P03 to choose a convenient real cone, but that normalization is not an indefinitely iterable contraction.

#### 2.3.1. Exact action on the two ghost exponents

Suppose alpha=s theta^A(theta+1)^B, with s=±1 and A,B integers. For a>=16 this is the exhaustive and unique parametrization from P03. The identities

    sigma^2(theta)=-(theta+1)/theta,
    sigma^2(theta+1)=-1/theta

give

    theta sigma^2(alpha)
      =s*(-1)^(A+B)*theta^(1-A-B)*(theta+1)^A.

Thus R acts on exponents by

    rho(A,B)=(1-A-B,A).

Set C=1-A-B. The map rho cyclically permutes the triple (A,B,C). Therefore

    H(A,B)=A^2+B^2+(1-A-B)^2

is exactly invariant. This is an explicit positive definite quadratic height, centered at (1/3,1/3). Also

    H_pair(x,y)=max(|x|,|y|,|x+y|)

is invariant under all six pair transformations. Hence the Galois symmetry cannot decrease either of these natural heights, even on a cancellation wall.

Any other height can be minimized on each finite orbit, but that gives only a finite symmetry reduction. It does not remove large minimal representatives of other orbits.

### 2.4. This covers all rational linear norm-form symmetries

PROVED THEOREM. For Thomas's F, every M in GL_2(Q) satisfying the polynomial identity

    F(M(x,y))=±F(x,y)

is one of ±R^j, j=0,1,2.

Proof. Over K, F factors into the three distinct linear forms x-theta_i y. Unique factorization in the polynomial ring K[x,y] shows that the projective linear transformation induced by M permutes their three projective zeros. Since M has rational coefficients, that permutation commutes with the cyclic Galois action on the three roots. The centralizer of a three-cycle in S_3 is its cyclic subgroup: the image of one root determines a commuting permutation on all three. Thus the induced permutation is one of the three powers realized by R.

Two projective linear transformations agreeing on three distinct points of P^1 agree everywhere. Hence M=lambda R^j for some nonzero rational lambda. Homogeneity gives F(Mv)=lambda^3 F(v). The prescribed multiplier ±1 forces lambda=±1.

The elementary claim about three points can be checked directly: after composing one transformation with the inverse of the other and sending the three points to 0,1,infinity, a fractional linear transformation fixing all three has zero off-diagonal entries and equal diagonal entries.

More generally, this conclusion is not confined to automorphisms displayed in advance. Any Q-linear norm similitude on the three-dimensional field K is semilinear. Indeed, in the three embedding coordinates the norm is their product. A linear substitution preserving this product up to a nonzero scalar must permute the three coordinate factors and multiply them by constants. Rationality makes the permutation commute with the Galois three-cycle and makes the constants conjugates of one u in K. Hence the substitution is u sigma^j. Imposing the marker plane then gives Section 2.3.

#### 2.4.1. Finitely many related binary cubic forms do not fix this problem

PROVED FINITE-GROUPOID STATEMENT. Let F_1,...,F_s be fixed rational binary cubic forms with three distinct projective zeros each. Consider invertible rational linear maps satisfying

    F_j(Mv)=±F_i(v).

For fixed i,j there are at most twelve such maps. Consequently, from a fixed initial form and vector, all compositions of such maps produce at most 12s tagged images.

Proof. A projective transformation carrying the three-root set of F_i to that of F_j is determined by one of at most 3!=6 bijections. For each projective transformation, homogeneity and the multiplier ±1 allow at most two rational scalings. Compositions are still maps of the displayed type, so the same finite count applies to every word in the maps.

All these matrices and their inverses are fixed. If the list is nonempty, there is a positive constant c, depending only on the finite list, such that every image satisfies

    ||Mv||_infinity >= c ||v||_infinity.

For instance use ||v||<=||M^(-1)||*||Mv|| and take the minimum of 1/||M^(-1)|| over the finite list. Thus this class of related-equation changes has no asymptotic contraction to a bounded region. It can choose finite-orbit representatives, but cannot itself supply a new unbounded descent.

This theorem does not cover nonlinear covers, infinitely many coefficient forms, or maps depending on the solution through unbounded new data. Those are precisely the possible escape routes, and they need a separate joint height controlling both the unknown and the changing equation.

### 2.5. Norms and resolvents: what they actually retain

PROVED. A cubic field has no proper intermediate field between it and Q: the tower law makes the intermediate degree divide 3. Therefore the only proper field norm from K to a subfield is the scalar norm to Q, which is already ±1 on the Thue solutions.

For the cyclic cubic, the discriminant is a rational square and the usual quadratic resolvent is split over Q; it gives no new nontrivial field-norm direction. This can be read directly from the three rational-in-theta roots and their cyclic permutation: the alternating product of root differences is fixed by the three-cycle and hence rational.

For an irreducible non-Galois cubic, pass to its degree-six normal closure and its quadratic resolvent field. The norm to that quadratic field of an element originally in the cubic field is the product of its three cubic conjugates, hence its rational norm. Thus merely taking the original unit through this relative norm also returns the already known scalar. A new expression in the normal closure can contain new information, but then its relation to the original arithmetic property must be proved; it is not a free rank reduction.

### 2.6. A genuine cubic quotient, with every identity explicit

There is more available than the finite symmetry. Define

    D=t^2+3t+9,
    Q=x^2+xy+y^2,
    K_t=(2t+3)x^3+3(t+6)x^2y+3(3-t)xy^2-(2t+3)y^3.

PROVED IDENTITY:

    K_t(x,y)^2+27 F(x,y)^2=4D Q(x,y)^3.

A compact verification also explains its arithmetic meaning. Work in Q(s), where s^2=-3, and set omega=(-1+s)/2. Then omega^2+omega+1=0, omega^3=1, and

    K_t(x,y)+3s F(x,y)
      =[(2t+3)+3s]*(x-omega y)^3.                 (2.6.1)

Verify each coefficient. Write C=(2t+3)+3s. Since

    (x-omega y)^3=x^3-3omega x^2y+3omega^2 xy^2-y^3,

the x^3 and y^3 coefficients are C and -C, as required. Using s^2=-3 gives

    -3C omega=3(t+6)-3t s,
    3C omega^2=3(3-t)-3(t+3)s,

which are exactly the x^2y and xy^2 coefficients of K_t+3sF. This proves (2.6.1) by expansion. Multiply it by its conjugate s->-s. On the right,

    C*C_bar=(2t+3)^2+27=4(t^2+3t+9)=4D,
    (x-omega y)(x-omega^2 y)=x^2+xy+y^2=Q.

On the left, the product is K_t^2+27F^2. The identity follows.

Consequently |F(x,y)|=1 gives

    K_t^2=4D Q^3-27.

Equivalently, the integers

    X=4D Q,    Y=4D K_t

satisfy

    Y^2=X^3-432D^2.                              (2.6.2)

These quantities are invariant under R. Indeed

    Q(R(x,y))=Q(x,y),
    y-omega(-x-y)=omega(x-omega y),

and omega^3=1, so (2.6.1) makes both K_t and F invariant. For a fixed sign F=1 or F=-1, the map identifies exactly the three-element R-orbits over the algebraic closure. To check this, use the invertible linear coordinates w=x-omega y and w'=x-omega^2 y. The fixed values of K_t,F prescribe w^3 and w'^3 by (2.6.1) and its conjugate, and Q=ww'. If Q!=0, the three cube roots for w determine w'=Q/w, giving exactly three points, permuted by (w,w')->(omega w,omega^2 w'). If Q=0, exactly one of w^3,w'^3 is zero because F!=0; the other is nonzero and again gives exactly three points. Thus this is the natural degree-three quotient. The elementary identities are enough to use the map without importing elliptic-group terminology.

#### 2.6.1. Why this quotient is not yet the requested descent

For every real x,y,

    (x^2+y^2)/2 <= Q <= 3(x^2+y^2)/2,

because - (x^2+y^2)/2 <= xy <= (x^2+y^2)/2. Thus X=4D Q has quadratic size in the original coordinates. The displayed forward map increases an ordinary coordinate height; it does not produce a smaller exponent vector.

The equation (2.6.2) has additional divisibility and lifting restrictions: X must be 4D times an Eisenstein norm Q, Y must be 4D K_t, and (2.6.1) must recover integer x,y with F=±1. An arbitrary point of (2.6.2) need not lift. Therefore replacing the Thue equation by all integral points of (2.6.2) may enlarge the problem.

The cubic quotient is nevertheless a concrete possible entry point for a new descent. To make it one, prove a finite and exhaustive set of covers on the quotient, exact reconstruction into the original norm-form locus, and an inverse height inequality. Those are new lemmas; the invariant identity does not contain them implicitly.

Factoring (2.6.1) in the Eisenstein ring is another legitimate elementary direction. That ring has a Euclidean division proof, so one can obtain finite multiplier branches after treating primes dividing 6D. But one branch is already C*(x-omega y)^3. Re-expressing its fixed imaginary part is exactly the original cubic equation. A factorization that returns this branch without a new height comparison or separator has reorganized the problem rather than descended it.

The two conclusions should therefore be kept together: natural plane-preserving unit/Galois moves have an exact finite-symmetry obstruction; the cubic covariant quotient exists and is explicit, but the arithmetic contraction after taking the quotient remains TARGET.

### 2.7. Optional geometric strengthening: regular self-maps of the affine curve

PROVED GEOMETRIC THEOREM. For t>=1, every nonconstant regular algebraic self-map over Q of the affine curve C: F(x,y)=1 is one of R^j, j=0,1,2.

This proof uses elementary facts about compact complex algebraic curves (a holomorphic function on a compact curve is constant, and the degree of a nonconstant map counts fibers with multiplicity). It uses no Mordell–Weil theorem, height theory, unit theorem, or arithmetic finiteness theorem. If the programme's meaning of elementary excludes even these compact-curve facts, Sections 2.2–2.4 remain completely independent algebraic proofs for the stated linear/semilinear class.

Proof. Compactify C as F(X,Y)=Z^3. The curve is absolutely irreducible: in the chart Y=1, Z^3−f(X) is irreducible over C(X), since f has simple roots and cannot be a cube in C(X); for a cubic polynomial in Z over a field containing the cube roots of unity, reducibility would give a root and hence such a cube. Gauss's lemma gives the claimed irreducibility. It is nonsingular. On the affine chart Z=1, simultaneous vanishing of F_x,F_y is impossible because Euler's identity xF_x+yF_y=3F equals 3. At Z=0 the three roots of f are distinct, so the infinity points are nonsingular too.

The differential

    eta=dx/F_y=-dy/F_x

is holomorphic and nonzero at every affine point. To check infinity explicitly, use u=X/Y and v=Z/Y. Then f(u)=v^3, x=u/v, y=1/v, and

    f'(u)du=3v^2 dv,
    F_y(u,1)=3v^3-u f'(u).

Hence

    eta=(v du-u dv)/F_y(u,1)=dv/f'(u).

At v=0 the denominator f'(u) is nonzero. Thus eta has no zeros or poles anywhere on the compact curve.

A regular affine self-map has polynomial coordinate functions modulo F-1. These rational functions define a map on the compact curve: at any pole, multiply its homogeneous triple [P:Q:1] by the largest necessary power of a local parameter, leaving at least one nonzero coordinate. The defining homogeneous equation persists, so the map extends across that point.

The pullback of eta under a nonconstant map is a nonzero holomorphic differential. Its ratio with eta is a holomorphic function on the compact curve, hence a nonzero constant. Therefore there is no ramification: locally a map of degree e would make the pulled-back differential vanish to order e-1.

Let delta be the degree of the map. It has exactly delta distinct preimages of each point, since it is unramified. The three points at infinity have all their preimages at infinity, because the original affine self-map takes affine points to affine points. Thus 3delta<=3, and delta=1. The map is an automorphism and permutes the three infinity points.

Its coordinate polynomials P,Q have pole order at most one at each infinity point. Choose a representative P of smallest total degree e in its class modulo F-1. If e>=2, its top homogeneous term P_e must vanish at each infinity root, otherwise P has a pole of order e there. The three distinct linear factors of F therefore divide P_e. For e=2 this is impossible unless P_e=0; for e>=3 write P_e=F H and replace P by P-(F-1)H. This lowers the total degree, contradicting minimality. Hence P has degree at most one; the same holds for Q.

Write the map as v -> Av+b. Its image is a curve, so A is invertible: rank at most one would put the image in a line whose intersection with the irreducible cubic is finite. The polynomial identity is

    F(Av+b)-1=c(F(v)-1)

for a nonzero scalar c, because both sides define the same irreducible cubic and have degree three. The degree-two terms give b dot grad F(Av)=0 identically. If b were nonzero, a linear change of coordinates would make F independent of one variable, hence a cube of a linear form; this contradicts its three distinct roots. Thus b=0. Constant terms give c=1. Section 2.4 then gives A=R^j, since the negative maps change F=1 to F=-1.

For a nonconstant map from either fixed component F=1 or F=−1 to either fixed component, composing with simultaneous negation reduces to the proved case. Its formula is therefore a restriction of one of ±R^j. A regular map of the disjoint union may choose these restrictions independently on its two components; its entire automorphism group should not be identified with the six uniformly linear maps. The conclusion needed here remains valid: on each component every nonconstant regular move sends a point into its finite six-element signed R-orbit.

LIMIT OF THIS THEOREM. A rational map with affine poles, even if those poles happen not to be integer points, is not a regular self-map of C and is not covered. A map from a different cover Y to C is not covered. Such maps are exactly where a genuine descent could live, but denominator control, global liftability, and a decreasing height must then be supplied explicitly.

## 3. Q3: constructing arithmetic markers by real and p-adic determinants

### 3.1. The correct claim, and the distinction between counting and construction

The proposed method is mathematically sound after two qualifications.

(i) A bound showing that every determinant formed from m unknown integral points is zero proves that those points lie on an auxiliary polynomial. It does not by itself construct that polynomial without finding the points. The polynomial can depend on the unknown points.

(ii) A lattice-reduction computation proposing A,B does not prove the uniform bound |A(x)sqrt(F(x))−B(x)|<1 on an interval. That bound needs an exact certificate: rational interval bounds, derivative and remainder estimates, or polynomial sign checks. Once the bound is proved, the integer-gap implication is immediate.

There is an elementary theorem that bridges (i) and (ii): construct a lattice of auxiliary coefficient vectors satisfying finite local-jet congruences; compare its covolume with a certified real interpolation body. It gives an auxiliary that is simultaneously small at infinity and divisible by a large integer at every admissible point. This is a genuine source of additional markers valid in a bounded region. It can cut a smooth local germ without contradicting the rank obstruction, because the extra marker is justified by bounded height and integrality, not by a redundant algebraic identity.

### 3.2. A real determinant lemma with every constant explicit

PROVED. Let I be a real interval of length W. Let f_1,...,f_m be C^(m−1) functions on I. Suppose positive numbers B_j and L satisfy

    sup_(x in I) |f_j^(r)(x)|/r! <= B_j L^(−r)

for 0<=r<=m−1. Put K=m(m−1)/2. For x_1,...,x_m in I,

    |det(f_j(x_i))| <= m! (product_j B_j) (W/L)^K.

Proof. A repeated node makes the determinant zero. Otherwise order the nodes. Successive divided-difference row operations give

    det(f_j(x_i))
      = product_(i<j)(x_j−x_i) * det(f_j[x_1,...,x_i]).

The divided difference of order i−1 equals f_j^(i−1)(xi)/(i−1)! for some xi in I, so its absolute value is at most B_j L^(−i+1). Every term in the last determinant is therefore bounded by (product B_j)L^(−K). There are m! terms and every node difference is at most W. This proves the claim.

This elementary proof can be used instead of citing a determinant-method theorem. More refined bounds replace m! by a Hadamard or permanent bound; no such improvement changes the exponents below.

### 3.3. Exact width exponent for y^2=F(x), degree 2h

Assume F is a rational polynomial of degree 2h, F>0 on [N,2N], and the chosen real branch f(x)=sqrt(F(x)) has certified derivative estimates

    |(x^j f(x))^(r)|/r! <= C_j N^(h+j−r),
    |(x^i)^(r)|/r! <= C'_i N^(i−r)

through the derivative order used. The constants must be stated; they can depend on F and the auxiliary degree. Fixed denominator factors are to be included in the constants or the integral observables.

Use the 2e+2 observables

    1,x,...,x^e, f,xf,...,x^e f.

Their values at integral points (x,y) are integers when x,y are integers. Here

    m=2e+2,
    S=sum_(i=0)^e i + sum_(j=0)^e(h+j)=(e+1)(e+h),
    K=m(m−1)/2=(e+1)(2e+1).

Writing C=m! product_i C'_i product_j C_j, the real determinant bound is

    |D| <= C N^S (W/N)^K.

Thus all m-by-m integral evaluation determinants vanish if

    C N^S (W/N)^K < 1.

Equivalently,

    W < C^(−1/K) N^delta,
    delta=1−S/K=(e+1−h)/(2e+1)
         =1/2−(h−1/2)/(2e+1).

This confirms the user's qualitative exponent W<N^(1/2−O(h/e)), with e defined as the separate degree bound for both A and B. One needs e>=h for a positive exponent. The number of intervals covering [N,2N] is on the scale

    C^(1/K) N^(1−delta)
      =C^(1/K) N^(1/2+(h−1/2)/(2e+1)).

This is a sufficient width, not a theorem that no better certificate can ever use a wider interval. Polynomial-part subtraction and special cancellations can improve the derivative profile substantially.

A weighted basis is sometimes better. If deg B<=E and deg A<=E−h, with E>=h, then

    m=2E−h+2,
    S=E(E+1)−h(h−1)/2,
    K=m(m−1)/2,
    delta=1−S/K.

These are the exact weights; one should optimize them rather than mix the two meanings of auxiliary degree. For fixed m, taking the lowest-weight independent monomials is at least as good for the crude size bound.

PROVED consequence. If all maximal evaluation determinants vanish, there is a nonzero pair A,B in Z[X], with the stated degree bounds, such that A(x)y−B(x)=0 at every integral point in the interval. If F is not a square in Q(X), the polynomial

    R(X)=A(X)^2 F(X)−B(X)^2

is not identically zero (clear fixed denominators if needed). Indeed A=0 would force B=0 if R=0, while A!=0 would make F=(B/A)^2. All eligible x are roots of R. For the balanced basis deg R<=2e+2h. On a fixed real square-root branch, there is at most one point above each x.

Proof of the existence of A,B. The evaluation matrix of all the eligible points has rank <m: otherwise some m rows would have nonzero determinant. Its rational nullspace is nonzero. Clear denominators in a nonzero null vector.

The proof is a counting/covering assertion. An auxiliary computed from m−1 known independent rows is exhaustive, but if the integral points are not already known this alone is not an efficient point-finding algorithm. Section 3.7 supplies the constructive version.

### 3.4. p-adic determinant divisibility is a jet statement

PROVED one-disc lemma. Let f_j(u)=sum_(k>=0)c_(j,k)u^k, with c_(j,k) in Z_p, convergent on p Z_p. Let u_i belong to p^a Z_p, a>=1. Then

    v_p det(f_j(u_i)) >= a*m(m−1)/2.

Proof. Truncate all series to sufficient precision and expand the determinant multilinearly. A nonzero term uses distinct exponents k_1,...,k_m; repeated exponents give proportional monomial columns. The sum of m distinct nonnegative exponents is at least 0+1+...+(m−1). The term is therefore divisible by p^(aK). Passage to the convergent limit proves the claim.

The stronger statement is

    det(f_j(u_i)) is divisible by product_(i<j)(u_j−u_i)

in the relevant integral power-series ring: every alternating polynomial in the u_i is divisible by the Vandermonde polynomial, with integral quotient. Truncation proves the series version. Consequently

    v_p D >= sum_(i<j) v_p(u_i−u_j).

If all the observables are even functions of u, the same argument in u^2 gives the corresponding Vandermonde in u_i^2 and a base bound 2aK. Symmetry of only one observable does not give this bound for an arbitrary collection of other observables.

More generally, let C(k_1,...,k_m) be the coefficient determinant formed from the corresponding series coefficients. Then

    v_p D >= min_(0<=k_1<...<k_m)
      [a*(k_1+...+k_m)+v_p C(k_1,...,k_m)].

Any certified coefficient divisibility improves the lower bound. This is the precise overlap with the ghost lifting engine: both methods use the first nonvanishing jets and an arithmetic gap. A usual lifting lemma isolates a zero of one or several observables. A determinant lemma forces an evaluation matrix to lose rank, producing an auxiliary equation. These are different conclusions.

P03's return interpolation gives, for a single near-identity return H=I+p^e K and each integral observable,

    v_p(c_(j,k)) >= e*k−v_p(k!).

This may be substituted in the minimum above. If m<=p, the simple first m weights give the lower bound e*K even without an additional restriction on u; in general use the minimum over the weights, because k−v_p(k!) need not be monotone. One must not silently replace that minimum by the first m terms for arbitrarily large m.

Several commuting parameters are also allowed. For functions with integral series in r parameters, all lying in p^a Z_p^r, multilinearity gives a lower bound a*E_r(m), where E_r(m) is the sum of total degrees of the m smallest distinct monomials in r variables. If

    binom(r+L,r)<=m<binom(r+L+1,r),

then

    E_r(m)=r*binom(r+L,r+1)
           +(L+1)*(m−binom(r+L,r)).

The proof is the same expansion, with distinct multi-indices replacing distinct integers. This formula makes the local dimension cost explicit. For r=1 it is K; for larger r the growth is proportional to m^(1+1/r), rather than m^2.

### 3.5. A finite global p-adic divisor, with no residue-class multiplication cost

PROVED. Suppose m integral points on a curve reduce into at most s smooth allowed residue discs modulo p. On each disc all chosen observables have integral power-series expansions in a local parameter u in p Z_p. Let n_c be the occupancy of disc c among the m points. Then

    v_p D >= sum_c binom(n_c,2).

Proof. Expand the rows in each disc in that disc's parameter. Within one group a determinant term vanishes if two exponents are equal. That group's total contribution is at least 0+...+(n_c−1). Add these contributions over the groups. The different local coefficient vectors need not coincide across discs.

For fixed total m and at most s classes, the smallest possible sum occurs when occupancies differ by at most 1. To prove this, if a>=b+2, replacing occupancies a,b by a−1,b+1 lowers the sum by a−b−1>0. Write

    m=s*q+r, 0<=r<s.

Then the unconditional divisor exponent is

    E(m,s)=s*q*(q−1)/2+r*q.

If s=0 there are no points. If s>=m the exponent is 0.

At depth a, suppose a certified upper bound R_(p,a) is known for the number of residue classes that can contain admissible points. The Vandermonde refinement, applied within the first-level discs, gives

    v_p D >= sum_(a>=1) E(m,R_(p,a)),

provided the local coordinates identify equality modulo p^a with equality of the point to that precision. A smooth integral curve chart with an integral inverse has this property. One may sum over any finite collection of certified depths. Indeed a pair with parameter difference of valuation b contributes 1 at each of the first b depths, so the sum of all within-class pair counts equals its Vandermonde valuation.

This gives a finite global divisor

    G_m=product_p p^(sum_a E(m,R_(p,a))).

For every m-point determinant one then has G_m|D. Combining it with the real bound gives rank loss whenever

    C N^S (W/N)^K < G_m.

No assumption about the distribution of primes is needed: choose finitely many primes, count the allowed residues, and prove the required smooth charts and exclusions. This is an elementary finite version of the local-to-global determinant philosophy. It is not a substitute for all of Salberger's geometric machinery.

Ghost lifting strengthens this statement by reducing R_(p,a), sometimes drastically. A persistent smooth one-dimensional germ normally contributes about p^a classes and ceases to help at depth a once R_(p,a)>=m. A genuinely isolated branch has a much smaller residue tree. This is one precise way in which the method cards can feed a global auxiliary-polynomial construction.

Bad reductions must be treated. It is not enough that a prime is good for most points: a point lying in a singular residue class may have a different parameterization and a weaker divisor bound. One may omit that prime, split the bad classes, or supply their own jet certificates.

### 3.6. The simple hybrid rank theorem

PROVED. Let S be a finite or infinite set of admissible integral points whose real parameter lies in a compact interval I. Let f_1,...,f_m be integer-valued observables on S. Suppose their real restrictions obey Section 3.2, and finitely many local certificates imply G_m|D for every m-point evaluation determinant. If

    m! (product B_j) (W/L)^K < G_m,

then the evaluation rank is less than m, and a nonzero integer linear combination of the observables vanishes on S.

Proof. Every determinant is an integer multiple of G_m with absolute value strictly less than G_m, and hence is zero. If the evaluation rank were at least m, some m rows would have nonzero determinant. A rational null vector, with denominators cleared, gives the asserted linear combination.

For y^2=F and the basis of Section 3.3, it gives an A y−B auxiliary and the explicit univariate polynomial R=A^2F−B^2. For another algebraic curve, one must prove that the chosen observables are linearly independent in the coordinate ring so the auxiliary does not vanish identically on the curve, then use an explicit elimination/resultant calculation. In higher dimensions one auxiliary lowers dimension at most one; additional independent cuts must be proved.

### 3.7. Constructive auxiliary markers from a jet lattice and a real norm body

This is the main constructive theorem. It directly addresses the request to find A,B and then certify their uniform smallness.

PROVED THEOREM (finite local jets plus a real interpolation body).

Let X be a compact real parameter set, and let f(x)=(f_1(x),...,f_m(x)) be a row of continuous real functions. At each admissible integral point the values f_j are integers. Let Lambda be a full-rank sublattice of Z^m, with index I_Lambda, and let M>=1 be an integer. Assume a finite congruence certificate proves

    sum_j b_j f_j(P) is divisible by M

for every b in Lambda and every admissible integral point P in the region.

Choose m real sample parameters x_1,...,x_m for which the matrix

    T=(f_j(x_i))_(i,j)

is invertible. Supply a certified finite bound

    K_R >= sup_(x in X) ||f(x) T^(−1)||_1.

If

    K_R^m * I_Lambda * |det T| < M^m,

then there exists a nonzero b in Lambda such that

    |sum_j b_j f_j(x)| < M

uniformly on X. Every admissible integral point in the region is therefore a zero of this auxiliary function.

Proof. Choose eta with

    (I_Lambda |det T|)^(1/m) < eta < M/K_R.

The symmetric convex body C={b in R^m: ||T b||_infinity<eta} has volume

    volume(C)=(2eta)^m/|det T| > 2^m I_Lambda.

An elementary lattice pigeonhole argument gives a nonzero point of Lambda in C. For completeness, C/2 has volume greater than the volume I_Lambda of a fundamental parallelepiped for Lambda. Translate its pieces into that parallelepiped. Two distinct pieces overlap on a set of positive measure; corresponding points differ by a nonzero lattice vector. Their difference lies in C by symmetry and convexity. Let b be that vector. Then

    |f(x)b|=|f(x)T^(−1) T b|
             <= ||f(x)T^(−1)||_1 ||T b||_infinity
             < K_R eta < M.

At admissible integral points this value is also divisible by M; it must be zero.

Effectivity. For an effective construction, assume the real sample values can be enclosed to arbitrary rational precision and the stated uniform bounds are effectively certified; these hypotheses hold for the algebraic branches considered here. A certified bound for ||T^(−1)|| supplies a finite bound on ||b|| from the body C, so exhaustive enumeration with successively refined sample enclosures is an elementary terminating construction. Refining all candidate tests avoids having to decide an exact boundary equality: the existence proof supplies a vector satisfying a strict inequality. LLL or another lattice algorithm is a practical search tool. The final certificate is the coefficient vector b, its local congruences, and its uniform real bound, rather than an appeal to numerical lattice reduction. The volume condition is sufficient, not necessary: a useful vector may be found even when the crude volume estimates fail.

The interpolation constant K_R cannot be omitted. A small evaluation determinant does not by itself bound interpolation between the nodes, and a bound only at the sample nodes is not a uniform bound between them. Conversely, apparent near dependence of the original coordinates does not by itself make K_R large: K_R is invariant under invertible changes of the function basis, as proved in Section 3.11.1. For the algebraic square-root branches at issue, K_R can be bounded by exact algebraic interval calculations or by a separately proved Chebyshev/interpolation estimate; it should be part of the output.

#### 3.7.1. How to build Lambda explicitly in one p-adic disc

Suppose on a certified local disc

    f_j(u)=sum_(k>=0)c_(j,k)u^k,  c_(j,k) in Z_p,
    u in p^a Z_p.

For a chosen integer L>=1, impose on b the finitely many congruences

    sum_j b_j c_(j,k) = 0 mod p^(L−a*k)

for 0<=k and a*k<L. Every higher term already has valuation at least L. Thus b in this lattice implies f(P)b=0 mod p^L throughout the disc.

The coefficients are only required modulo the displayed finite powers of p. A rational or integral recurrence chart, or an elementary implicit-function/Hensel recursion at a smooth curve point, supplies them. At a point of y^2=F(x) with p odd and y nonzero mod p, division by 2y in the recursion is legitimate; points with y=0 require a different smooth parameter or a separate certificate.

Taking L=a*m gives m congruences with total index at most

    p^(a*(m+(m−1)+...+1))=p^(a*m*(m+1)/2).

For M=p^(a*m),

    M^m / I_Lambda >= p^(a*m*(m−1)/2)=p^(aK).

This is exactly the divisor scale in the one-disc determinant lemma. Thus the constructive lattice theorem and the rank theorem have the same basic exponent, apart from the explicit real interpolation conditioning.

#### 3.7.2. A global construction matching the occupancy exponent

Suppose there are s certified allowed smooth discs modulo p, each with u in p Z_p and integral Taylor coefficients. Put

    L=floor(m/s)

when s>=1. If L=0, use no constraints at p. Otherwise impose

    sum_j b_j c_(c,j,k)=0 mod p^(L−k)

for every allowed disc c and 0<=k<L. This guarantees p^L|f(P)b at every admissible point, without splitting the real interval into s separate computation tasks. The lattice index is at most

    p^(s*L*(L+1)/2).

Since m=sL+r,

    mL−sL(L+1)/2
      =sL(L−1)/2+rL
      =E(m,s).

Consequently M^m/I_Lambda >= p^E(m,s). For several primes, intersect their lattices and multiply the moduli M. The index is at most the product of the individual indices, so the gains multiply. In practice one computes the exact index by integer Hermite or Smith reduction; dependent jet constraints can improve it.

This gives a fully constructive finite global determinant certificate. It avoids the misleading argument “one modulus lets each interval be M times wider, therefore the total work drops by M”: that argument forgets the number of residue classes. The global jet lattice packages all the classes at once and its precise index measures the actual gain.

Deeper residue trees and known divisibilities of Taylor coefficients can be added to the lattice. Their exact index, rather than an optimistic count of constraints, should be used in the real norm-body criterion.

### 3.8. How to combine the auxiliaries with the ghost lifting cards

PROVED CONDITIONAL COMPLETION RULE. Cover a bounded region by finitely many real cells and necessary residue conditions. On each cell produce a verified auxiliary polynomial by Section 3.7, or produce a finite-rank certificate by Section 3.6 with an explicitly known auxiliary. Then do either of the following:

1. Eliminate with the original curve equation and verify every remaining integer root exactly; or
2. Pull the auxiliary back to the actual ghost orbit, together with the original markers, and prove a full-rank or higher-order primitive-direction isolation statement on every surviving residue class. Every bounded residual class is checked exactly.

The cell covering and residue covering must be exhaustive. A strict bound and an exact congruence, not a floating-point suggestion, are the leaf certificates.

The extra auxiliary may have a derivative independent of the original marker even when the original p-adic germ is smooth and positive-dimensional. There is no contradiction: the auxiliary is known to vanish only at the integer points in this bounded real cell. It need not vanish on the entire original p-adic solution germ. This is precisely the new information that an auxiliary-polynomial method can introduce where redundant covers or transported markers cannot.

UNPROVED IN GENERAL. Applying the construction separately to infinitely many dyadic boxes does not prove that there are only finitely many solutions. One still needs a real cutoff, a uniform exclusion theorem for the unbounded cells, or a new descent. For the middle range of a problem with an already proved Runge cutoff, this global issue has already been resolved, so a finite auxiliary cover is a legitimate completion strategy.

### 3.9. Quantitative assessment for the S_63 interval

The following operation counts are scale estimates from the displayed sufficient widths, with their constants set to 1. They are not rigorous lower bounds on all possible algorithms and are not timing claims.

#### 3.9.1. Applying the crude basis directly is not a credible plan

The n-model has degree 64 and h=32. At N=2.8*10^18, the generic balanced-basis scale gives:

|---|---:|---:|---:|

| 64 | 130 | 33/129 | about 5.35*10^13 |
| 128 | 258 | 97/257 | about 3.05*10^11 |
| 256 | 514 | 225/513 | about 2.27*10^10 |

Even the limiting exponent gives sqrt(N)=about 1.67*10^9 intervals for one dyadic band, before the constants, auxiliary construction, and root certificates. Choosing huge e to approach this limit makes the coefficient lattices huge as well. The raw O(sqrt N) slogan therefore does not establish feasible cost.

The T-model is not a free improvement. Here S_63(n)=T^2 R(T), T=n(n+1), deg R=30, so h=15 in T. But T has size N^2, making the generic sqrt(T) scale about N. The triangular restriction must be exploited quantitatively if one uses that model. If R=P/D and y/T=a/b in lowest terms, b^2|D; hence a fixed denominator-cleared integral model exists. Pulling it back to n reduces the degree from 64 to 60 (h=30), which is helpful but only a small change in the crude exponent.

#### 3.9.2. Use the known Runge residual, not the original y-height

Suppose a verified exact normalization supplies an integer observable

    z=D*y−Q(x)

at every eligible integer point, with x an integer coordinate or a coordinate on a fixed denominator lattice, and suppose on x in [N,2N]

    |z^(r)(x)|/r! <= C_r H N^(−rho−r)

for all derivatives needed. The typical centered even-degree square-root expansion has rho=2, but the actual denominator and actual H must be computed. A reported Runge cutoff alone is not a certificate that H equals its square.

For the balanced basis 1,x,...,x^e,z,xz,...,x^e z, the same determinant calculation gives

    |D_aux| <= C H^(e+1) N^((e+1)(e−rho)) (W/N)^K,

where K=(e+1)(2e+1). Thus a sufficient width is

    W < C^(−1/K) H^(−1/(2e+1)) N^((e+1+rho)/(2e+1)),

and the interval-count scale is

    C^(1/K) H^(1/(2e+1)) N^((e−rho)/(2e+1)).

For rho=2 and e=2 this is H^(1/5), independent of N at the level of exponents. For e=0 it is H/N^2, corresponding to the small range of possible integer residual values. As e grows the scale tends to sqrt(N). Ignoring degree-dependent constants, the envelope of the endpoint choices is

    min(sqrt(N), H/N^rho).

Its maximum occurs at

    N=H^(2/(2rho+1)),

with value H^(1/(2rho+1)). For rho=2 this is H^(1/5).

The exact normalization in Section 4 proves H=9*10^36, and Section 3.13 supplies an all-order derivative profile. For this H the exponent-scale peak is about 2.46*10^7 cells, around N=6.05*10^14. This is a baseline calculation with derivative and interpolation constants suppressed, not a proved work estimate. More importantly, Section 3.11 improves the basis itself and yields a much smaller scale; the H^(1/5) profile should not be treated as the recommended final algorithm.

#### 3.9.3. Further cancellation is worth testing

There is more structure than this crude residual model records. Since z begins with a negative-power expansion, columns x^j z can have polynomial leading parts. Those parts may be removed using the 1,x,... columns. Linear combinations can cancel further negative powers. This is a rational basis-reduction or Padé construction at infinity, and it can improve the derivative profile beyond assigning z the single weight −rho.

The actual reduced leading coefficients and tails, the interpolation factor, and the transported coefficient lattice must all be retained. A determinant-one column operation introduces no extra denominator factor into the original evaluation determinant; Section 3.11 proves the exact reduction. Treating the new coefficients as arbitrary integers, instead of transporting the original lattice, would change the arithmetic problem. A formal cancellation of ten terms is not by itself a small integer-valued auxiliary.

This is the calculation I would ask Sol to run before a massive interval search:

1. Obtain the exact centered square-root polynomial part and an integer residual z, including the smallest useful denominator lattice.
2. Prove derivative bounds for z through moderate order, initially perhaps m<=20, on each dyadic n-range.
3. Reduce the observable basis by exact cancellation of its Laurent expansions at infinity, retaining the transformation matrix and denominator factors.
4. For several small auxiliary degrees, compute the actual local-jet lattices, their exact indices, real interpolation matrices and certified K_R bounds.
5. Attempt short-vector constructions and verify the strict real inequality. Record the resulting interval widths and root-polynomial sizes before deciding whether a full cover is computationally realistic.
6. Use ghost lifting to prune or isolate the remaining residue branches of each auxiliary intersection, and retain the proof of coverage.

A generic single-class modulus saving should not be counted without paying for the number of classes. If a modulus M permits M times wider intervals but has R(M) surviving classes, the basic work factor changes by R(M)/M. For a smooth curve with no strong arithmetic restriction, R(M) is commonly of the same scale as M. In the global jet construction the same issue is measured by the lattice index.

For a very small basis m=6 (e=2), primes with at least 6 allowed residue discs give no first-level global gain E(6,s)=0. Large collections of primes help only if the basis is larger, the arithmetic sieve leaves very few discs, or deeper/non-generic divisibilities are proved. This prevents an unjustified claim that adding many primes automatically makes the residual calculation cheap.

BELIEF. An optimized residual-and-jet-lattice computation is worth a pilot. A cover using only the original unreduced n,y growth bounds is not. No complete middle-range exclusion or feasible runtime is proved here; the exact missing computation is the finite auxiliary-cover certificate just described.

### 3.11. STRONGER RESULT: Hankel reduction of the Runge-residual basis

The raw sqrt(N) and residual H^(1/5) counts above are costs of unreduced derivative profiles, not intrinsic limits of the determinant method. The exact basis reduction below is much stronger on this evaluation.

PROVED THEOREM. Let z(X) have a convergent Laurent expansion

    z(X)=sum_(k>=1) a_k X^(−2k)

on a real end X>R. The coefficients a_k may be rational or real. Set

    H_0=1,
    H_s=det(a_(i+j+1))_(0<=i,j<s),  s>=1.

Fix e>=0 and suppose H_s!=0 for every 1<=s<=floor(e/2)+1. Consider the function basis

    1,X,...,X^e, z,Xz,...,X^e z.

There is an explicit determinant-one column transformation to a basis having the following leading powers and leading-coefficient product.

If e=2r is even, the polynomial powers remain 0,1,...,e; the negative powers are

    −1,−2,...,−e,−e−2,

so −e−1 is absent. Their combined weight sum is

    S_e=−e−2,

and the absolute product of all leading coefficients is

    |P_e|=|H_(r+1) H_r|.

If e=2r+1 is odd, the negative powers are

    −1,−2,...,−e−1,

the combined weight sum is

    S_e=−e−1,

and

    |P_e|=|H_(r+1)|^2.

Proof. Put t=X^2 and Z(t)=sum_(k>=1)a_k t^(−k). For s>=0 let P_s(t) be the monic polynomial of degree s such that

    P_s(t)Z(t)−Q_s(t)=d_s t^(−s−1)+O(t^(−s−2)),

where Q_s is its polynomial part. For s=0, P_0=1, Q_0=0 and d_0=a_1. For s>=1, write

    P_s(t)=t^s+sum_(i=0)^(s−1)c_i t^i.

The vanishing of the coefficients t^(−1),...,t^(−s) is exactly the linear system

    sum_(i=0)^(s−1)c_i a_(i+l)=−a_(s+l),  l=1,...,s.

Its coefficient matrix is H_s, so it has a unique solution. The next coefficient is

    d_s=a_(2s+1)+sum_(i=0)^(s−1)c_i a_(i+s+1).

The block determinant formula, or elementary elimination of the last column of H_(s+1), gives

    d_s=H_(s+1)/H_s.

For every even original index j=2s, replace X^(2s)z by

    R_s(X)=P_s(X^2)z(X)−Q_s(X^2).

For every odd original index j=2s+1, replace X^(2s+1)z by X R_s(X). Each replacement uses the original column with coefficient 1, lower columns of the same parity, and polynomial columns already present. The transformation is triangular with diagonal 1, hence determinant 1. The polynomial terms have degrees at most 2s−2 or 2s−1, so the available polynomial columns suffice.

The even-column leading powers are −2s−2 with coefficients d_s. The odd-column leading powers are −2s−1 with the same coefficients. The lists in the statement follow. For e=2r, the leading product is

    (product_(s=0)^r d_s)*(product_(s=0)^(r−1)d_s)
      =H_(r+1)H_r.

For e=2r+1 it is

    (product_(s=0)^r d_s)^2=H_(r+1)^2.

Summing the powers gives S_e as displayed. This proves every assertion.

The theorem is a finite linear-algebra identity. To use its leading powers on a specified finite interval, bound the Laurent tails and their derivatives. Nonzero Hankel determinants identify the pivots; they do not alone bound the tails.

Assume the transformed columns g_j have leading terms d_j X^(w_j), and prove on [N,2N], through derivative order m−1, that

The transformation is being used to bound the same real determinant, not to redefine the integer lattice.

For the constructive theorem, keep the original coefficient lattice Lambda. If f is replaced by fU, the real interpolation constant is exactly unchanged:

#### 3.11.4. Connection to tau recurrences, stated carefully

The constants are Hankel determinants of the Laurent coefficients of an algebraic branch. Adjacent shifted Hankel determinants satisfy explicit condensation identities. For integers k>=1 and s>=1, with H_0^(k)=1, define

    H_s^(k)=det(a_(k+i+j))_(0<=i,j<s).

Then the determinant identity

    H_(s+1)^(k) H_(s−1)^(k+2)
      =H_s^(k) H_s^(k+2)−(H_s^(k+1))^2

follows by the Desnanot–Jacobi identity applied to the Hankel matrix. Here is its elementary determinant proof. When the central (s−1)-by-(s−1) block is invertible, eliminate that block by row and column operations. The remaining two boundary rows and columns form a two-by-two Schur complement. Its determinant is the product of the diagonal entries minus the product of the off-diagonal entries; the four adjacent minors are the central determinant times those four entries. Multiplying out gives the displayed identity. Clearing denominators makes it a polynomial identity in the matrix entries, so it holds even when the central block is singular. For the Hankel matrix the two off-diagonal minors are equal, giving the square in the formula.

Thus the auxiliary constants already live in a bilinear determinant system, closely suited to the programme's recurrence techniques. This identity does not assert that every such one-dimensional Hankel sequence satisfies a fixed Somos-4, Somos-6 or Somos-8 recurrence. A reduction to one of those recurrences needs its own algebraic proof.

gives the claimed positive upper bound. The ceiling of the sharper rational bound D*rho/[2(1−delta)] is

    8048566317793624702247228645198991337.

Thus the useful residual coefficient is of size 8.05*10^36 in this normalization. It is not inferred from the quoted Runge cutoff. A centered expansion applied directly to 8y has a substantially worse denominator/residual constant; retaining the T^2 endpoint factor matters.

### 4.3. Elimination stays small after the normalization

Write Q_n=Q(n(n+1)) and

    H_n=D^2 f(n(n+1))−Q_n^2.

Then deg Q_n=30 and deg H_n<=28. The residual satisfies the exact equation

    z^2+2Q_n z−H_n=0.

If a certified auxiliary is A(n)z−B(n)=0, with deg A,deg B<=e, every candidate n is a zero of

    A(n)^2 H_n−2A(n)B(n)Q_n−B(n)^2.

After clearing the fixed rational denominators, this is an integer polynomial of degree at most 2e+30. A common zero of A and B is retained by the displayed polynomial and must be tested; no division by A is needed in deriving the necessary equation.

The polynomial is nonzero when (A,B) is nonzero. If A=0, its vanishing would force B=0. Otherwise its identical vanishing would make the quadratic function z rational, making f(n(n+1)) a rational-function square. A rational-function square which is a polynomial is the square of a polynomial, by cancellation of coprime numerator and denominator in Q[n]. Choose its positive-leading square root P(n). Then P and q(n(n+1)) have degree 30 and the same leading coefficient, while

    [P(n)−q(n(n+1))]*[P(n)+q(n(n+1))]=r(n(n+1)).

The second factor has degree 30, while the nonzero right side has degree 28. If the first factor is nonzero this is impossible by degrees, and if it is zero it contradicts r!=0. This proves the needed nonvanishing without any unproved irreducibility assertion.

For e=5,7,9, the elimination degrees are therefore at most 40,44,48. Exact integer-root isolation and substitution, or additional congruence and ghost-lifting certificates, can finish each successful auxiliary cell. Small degree alone does not bound the coefficient sizes or the total verification time, but it removes an unnecessary degree-64 penalty.
