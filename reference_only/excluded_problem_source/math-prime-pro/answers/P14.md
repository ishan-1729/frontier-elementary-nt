## 1. A two-logarithm lemma that allows denominators and very small logarithms

**PROVED.** Let $K\subset\mathbb R$ be a number field of degree $d$. Let $a_1,a_2\in K$ be positive and different from $1$. Suppose an integer $q\ge1$ and an integer $T\ge2$ satisfy
$$q a_i,\ q a_i^{-1}\text{ are algebraic integers},\qquad \log q\le T,\qquad e^{-T}\le|\sigma a_i|\le e^T.$$
For $\lambda=v_1\log a_1+v_2\log a_2\ne0$, put
$$B=\max(3,|v_1|,|v_2|),\quad w=32+\lceil\log_2 B\rceil+dT.$$
Then
$$|\lambda|>\exp[-2^{119}d^8T^4w^5].\tag{N1}$$
There is no lower hypothesis $|\log a_i|\ge1/T$. This change is essential when a short relation in a three-logarithm determinant produces new algebraic numbers.

### 1.1. The elementary arithmetic estimates

The ring and norm facts proved in P13 §1.1 apply. In particular a nonzero algebraic integer in a degree-$d$ field has the product of its conjugate absolute values at least one. If $z=\prod_i a_i^{v_i}$ and $S=\sum_i|v_i|$, then $q^S(z-1)$ is integral. At every embedding,
$$|\sigma(q^S(z-1))|\le2e^{2TS}.$$
If $0<|\log z|\le1$, the real integral identity $e^u-1=\int_0^u e^s ds$ gives $|z-1|\le3|\log z|$. Consequently
$$|\log z|\ge\tfrac13 e^{-TS}(2e^{2TS})^{-(d-1)}.\tag{N2}$$
For two coefficients of absolute value at most $B\ge3$, this implies
$$|\log z|\ge e^{-5dTB}.\tag{N3}$$
For a single number different from one it also gives
$$|\log a_i|\ge e^{-4dT}.\tag{N4}$$
Indeed the negative logarithm of the right side of (N2), with $S=1$, is at most $(2d-1)T+(d-1)\log2+\log3<4dT$. If the logarithm has absolute value greater than one, (N4) is automatic. These arguments also show that for three coefficients with $|v_1|,|v_2|\le B$, $|v_3|=1$, a nonzero form of absolute value at most one satisfies
$$|\log z|\ge e^{-10dTB}.\tag{N5}$$

### 1.2. Full determinant proof of (N1)

Invert either $a_i$ as necessary to make its logarithm positive. All hypotheses are preserved. Forms whose two nonzero coefficients have the same sign, or have only one nonzero coefficient, are covered by (N4). Otherwise divide the coefficients by their gcd and, after exchanging the two numbers and changing an overall sign, write
$$\lambda=b_2\log a_2-b_1\log a_1,\qquad b_1,b_2>0,\quad\gcd(b_1,b_2)=1,$$
with $A=\log a_1\ge\log a_2>0$. Proving the estimate after dividing the gcd proves the original estimate, because its coefficient height only decreases.

Set
$$m=2^{14}dT,\quad t=2^{14}dw,\quad k=m^2t,\quad l=t,\quad R=mt,\quad N=kl=R^2.$$
Thus
$$E=128wN^2=2^{119}d^8T^4w^5.$$
Assume $0<|\lambda|\le e^{-E}$. By (N4), $|\lambda|<A$, so
$$\beta=b_1/b_2\le2.$$
If $B<R$, (N3) contradicts the assumed bound. Otherwise the $R^2$ nodes
$$x_{r,s}=r+\beta s,\qquad0\le r,s<R,$$
are distinct. Equality would force $r-r'=b_1j$, $s-s'=-b_2j$; whichever of $b_1,b_2$ is largest is at least $R$. Thus $j=0$. Only the **maximum** coefficient needs to exceed $R$. Every node lies in $[0,3R]$ and distinct nodes differ by at least $1/B$.

Order these nodes increasingly, and use this order in both determinants
$$D_0=\det[x_{r,s}^{i}e^{jAx_{r,s}}]_{0\le j<l,\,0\le i<k},$$
$$\Delta=\det[(b_2r+b_1s)^i a_1^{jr}a_2^{js}],\qquad D=\Delta/b_2^{S_i},\quad S_i=N(k-1)/2.$$
The positive confluent exponential determinant identity in P13 §1.3 is applicable to $A>0$. With (N4) its explicit lower bound gives
$$D_0\ge e^{-Q_0},\qquad Q_0=N^2(\log(NB)+4dT).\tag{N6}$$
For completeness, the identity is obtained by expanding $\det(e^{\xi_i x_j})$ in power series. Every term is a product of two nonnegative generalized Vandermonde determinants; the lowest-degree term is their ordinary Vandermonde product divided by $\prod_{i<N}i!$. Confluent frequencies $\xi_{j,i}=jA+\varepsilon i$ give $D_0$ and the factors $\prod_{j<j'}((j'-j)A)^{k^2}(\prod_{i<k}i!)^l$. Positivity of the generalized Vandermondes follows from the Rolle induction for exponential sums and their positive confluent Wronskian. All of those identities, sign arguments, and convergence justifications are proved in P13 §1.3; no new zero estimate is being assumed here.

Put
$$J=N\log N+S_i\log(2BR)+4TRNt.$$
If $\Delta\ne0$, then
$$|D|\ge e^{-dJ}.\tag{N7}$$
Indeed $q^{2RtN}\Delta$ is integral. Its conjugates are at most
$$N!(2BR)^{S_i}e^{3TRNt}.$$
Apply the norm inequality, remove $q^{2RtN}$, and divide by $b_2^{S_i}$. The extra negative logarithm $2TRNt+S_i\log B$ is bounded by $J$, proving (N7).

Here are the numerical budgets. Since $w\ge32+dT+\log B$,
$$\log m\le w,\quad\log t\le w,\quad\log R\le2w,\quad\log N\le4w,$$
$$\log(2BR)\le4w,\qquad\log(6R)\le4w.$$
For $\log t\le w$, use $\log w\le w/2$ and $w\ge28+2\log d$. It follows that
$$dJ/N^2\le4dw/N+2dw/t+4dT/m<1,\qquad Q_0\le9wN^2.$$
Scale all the nodes by a real variable $z$. As in P13, distinct monomial degrees force the first possible determinant coefficient to have degree at least $N(N-1)/2$. The absolute power-series majorant at $z=2$ gives
$$|D_0|\le N!2^{-N(N-1)/2}(6R)^{S_i}e^{3TRNt}.$$
Thus
$$\log|D_0|+dJ\le
\left[4(d+1)w/N+(2d+2)w/t+(4d+3)T/m\right]N^2
-\frac{N(N-1)}2\log2.$$
The bracket is at most $19/2^{14}$. Since $N\ge4$ and $\log2\ge1/2$,
$$|D_0|\le e^{-dJ-N^2/8}.\tag{N8}$$

Finally the actual exponential entries differ by the factor $e^{js\lambda/b_2}$. Set
$$V=RNt,\qquad P=N\log N+S_i\log(3R)+3TRNt.$$
The permutation expansion and $|e^v-1|\le3|v|$ for $|v|\le1$ give
$$|D-D_0|\le3V|\lambda|e^P.$$
Here $P<N^2$, $\log(12V)\le10w$, and $E=128wN^2$ exceeds
$P+\log(12V)+\max(Q_0,dJ)$. It also ensures $V|\lambda|<1$. Therefore
$$|D-D_0|\le\tfrac14\min(e^{-Q_0},e^{-dJ}).$$
The first bound and (N6) prove $D>0$, so (N7) applies. The second bound and (N8) contradict (N7). This proves (N1).

## 2. Three logarithms with one coefficient fixed

The polynomial zero lemma proved in §3 below is the only additional ingredient. Its proof is included, rather than imported.

**PROVED.** Let $a_1,a_2,a_3>0$ belong to a real degree-$d$ number field and be multiplicatively independent. Let $q\ge1$, $T\ge2$ be integers with
$$q a_i,\ q a_i^{-1}\text{ integral},\qquad\log q\le T,\qquad e^{-T}\le|\sigma a_i|\le e^T.$$
Let
$$\Lambda=h_1\log a_1+h_2\log a_2+h_3\log a_3,\qquad h_i\in\mathbb Z,\quad h_3=\pm1,$$
and $B=\max(3,|h_1|,|h_2|)$. Then, for $w=32+\lceil\log_2 B\rceil+dT$,
$$\boxed{|\Lambda|>\exp[-2^{1296}d^{67}T^{45}w^{18}].}\tag{T1}$$
In particular,
$$|\Lambda|>\exp[-K_{d,T}(1+\log B)^{18}],\qquad
K_{d,T}=2^{1296}d^{67}T^{45}(33+dT)^{18}.\tag{T2}$$

### 2.1. Parameters and the finite projection alternative

Use
$$m=2^{20}dT,\quad t=2^{20}dw,\quad K_0=m^3t,\quad L_0=t,$$
$$r=16m^2t,\quad R=3r,\quad N=K_0(K_0+1)L_0/2,\quad U=2r^2.$$
The three positive algebraic numbers may be inverted; reorder the first two so the largest coefficient is used as pivot. If their actual maximum is below $3$, (N5) immediately proves (T1). Otherwise, after an overall sign change, write
$$\Lambda=b\log a_2-c\log a_1-e\log a_3,\qquad b=B,\quad |c|\le B,\quad e=\pm1.$$
Assume the contrary of (T1). If $b\le R$, (N5) contradicts that assumption. Hence $b>R$.

Project a cube of side $r$ by
$$\phi(i,j,k)=(i+(c/b)j,\ k+(e/b)j).$$
This map is injective on the cube. Equality of its second coordinates gives $b\,\Delta k=-e\,\Delta j$. Since $e=\pm1$, this implies $b\mid\Delta j$; but $|\Delta j|<b$. Thus $\Delta j=\Delta k=0$, and equality of the first coordinates gives $\Delta i=0$. Its real kernel is the line spanned by $(-c,b,-e)$.

Either every nonzero real linear functional on $\mathbb R^2$ takes more than $2K_0$ values on this projected cube, or there is a nonzero $u\in\mathbb Z^3$ with
$$u\cdot(-c,b,-e)=0,\qquad\|u\|_\infty\le U.\tag{T3}$$
To prove this alternative, suppose a functional has at most $2K_0$ values. Since $r^2>2K_0$, some fibre has more than $r$ lattice points. Every real line contains at most $r$ points of the cube, by projecting onto a coordinate where its direction is nonzero. Choose three noncollinear points in the fibre. The cross product of two difference vectors is a nonzero integer normal of size at most $2(r-1)^2\le U$. It is parallel to the functional pulled back to $\mathbb R^3$, so it annihilates the kernel of $\phi$. This proves (T3). Complex linear functionals cause no additional case: either their real and imaginary parts have a nonzero component, whose number of values is no greater, or the functional is zero.

### 2.2. The determinant when no short relation occurs

For $0\le i,j,k<R$, set
$$x=i+(c/b)j,\qquad y=k+(e/b)j,\qquad z=a_1^i a_2^j a_3^k.$$
Use rows $(v,s,\ell)$ with $v,s\ge0$, $v+s<K_0$, $0\le\ell<L_0$. The zero lemma gives full row rank of the evaluation matrix
$$[(bi+cj)^v(bk+ej)^s z^\ell].\tag{T4}$$
Here its three finite translation sets are all the side-$r$ cube. Their sum is contained in the side-$R$ cube. Multiplicative independence makes all their $z$-coordinates distinct. Their projected additive coordinates are distinct, and
$$r^3>128K_0^2L_0,\qquad r^3>16K_0L_0,\qquad r^3>4K_0^2.$$
The first follows from $r^3=4096m^6t^3$ and $K_0^2L_0=m^6t^3$; the other two follow immediately. The linear-projection condition is the first alternative in §2.1. These are every hypothesis of the zero lemma.

Select any nonzero square $N\times N$ minor $\Delta$ of (T4), and put
$$S=\sum(v+s)\le NK_0,\qquad D=\Delta/b^S.$$
Let $D_0$ be the same determinant with exponential entries replaced by
$$x^v y^s e^{\ell(Ax+Cy)},\qquad A=\log a_1,\quad C=\log a_3.$$
The entries of $D$ equal these ideal entries times $e^{\ell j\Lambda/b}$. Positivity of $D_0$ is not asserted or needed.

Set
$$J=N\log N+S\log(2BR)+6TNRL_0.$$
Multiplying every entry of $\Delta$ by $q^{3RL_0}$ makes it integral. Every conjugate of the resulting determinant has absolute value at most $e^J$. Removing that factor and the normalization $b^S$, the norm inequality gives
$$|D|\ge e^{-dJ}.\tag{T5}$$
Indeed the extra cost $3TNRL_0+S\log B$ is bounded by $J$.

Here is the two-variable analytic estimate in full. The linear change of coordinates $(x,y)\mapsto(Ax+Cy,y)$ is invertible because $A\ne0$. Every row is a polynomial of total degree less than $K_0$ times an exponential of the first new coordinate. Consequently in its power series the second new-coordinate exponent is less than $K_0$. At each total degree there are at most $K_0$ available monomials. When all nodes are scaled by a common variable $z$, any determinant coefficient using a repeated monomial vanishes. The sum of the degrees of $N$ distinct available monomials is at least
$$\frac{N(N-K_0)}{2K_0}\ge\frac{N^2}{4K_0},$$
because $N\ge2K_0$. The first inequality follows by filling at most $K_0$ places at each successive degree: if $N=qK_0+s$, $0\le s<K_0$, the smallest possible sum is $K_0q(q-1)/2+qs$, which exceeds $N(N-K_0)/(2K_0)$ by $s(K_0-s)/(2K_0)$.

Return to the original coordinates for a coefficient majorant. Since $|x|,|y|\le2R$, absolute convergence and the permutation expansion give, at radius $2$,
$$|D_0|\le N!\,2^{-N^2/(4K_0)}(4R)^S e^{4TNRL_0}.\tag{T6}$$
The linear coordinate change was used only to count possible monomials. It is not used in the arithmetic determinant or the majorant. Thus no logarithm enters an algebraic coefficient.

The following elementary budgets make (T6) contradict (T5). From $w\ge32+dT+\log B$,
$$\log m,\log t\le w,\quad\log K_0\le4w,\quad\log R\le4w,\quad\log N\le9w,$$
$$\log(2BR)\le6w,\qquad\log(4R)\le5w.$$
Therefore
$$\frac{\log|D_0|+dJ}{NK_0L_0}
\le\frac{9(d+1)w}{K_0L_0}+\frac{(6d+5)w}{L_0}
+\frac{(6d+4)TR}{K_0}-\frac1{16}.$$
The three positive terms sum to at most $509/2^{20}<1/32$; use $L_0=2^{20}dw$, $R/K_0=48/m$, and $m=2^{20}dT$. Hence
$$|D_0|\le e^{-dJ-NK_0L_0/32}.\tag{T7}$$

Set $V=RNL_0$ and $P=N\log N+S\log(2R)+2TNRL_0$. The same real exponential perturbation calculation used in §1 gives
$$|D-D_0|\le3V|\Lambda|e^P\quad\text{if }V|\Lambda|\le1.$$
The preceding estimates give $P+dJ<NK_0L_0\le N^2$, and $\log(12V)\le15w$. Thus the weaker assumption
$$|\Lambda|\le\exp[-E_{\rm det}],\qquad E_{\rm det}=128wN^2,$$
already gives $|D-D_0|\le\tfrac14 e^{-dJ}$. Together with (T7) this contradicts (T5).

### 2.3. Short relations reduce to the new two-logarithm lemma

Use the original coefficient vector $(h_1,h_2,h_3)$, with $|h_3|=1$. A short relation means $u_1h_1+u_2h_2+u_3h_3=0$, $0<\|u\|_\infty\le U$.

If $u_3\ne0$, set
$$\eta_1=a_1^{u_3}a_3^{-u_1},\qquad \eta_2=a_2^{u_3}a_3^{-u_2}.$$
Then
$$u_3\Lambda=h_1\log\eta_1+h_2\log\eta_2.$$
If $u_3=0$, put $g=\gcd(u_1,u_2)$, $a=u_1/g$, $b=u_2/g$. Coprimality gives $(h_1,h_2)=(bk,-ak)$ for an integer $|k|\le B$. In this case
$$\Lambda=k\log(a_1^b a_2^{-a})+h_3\log a_3.$$
All numbers used here differ from one, by multiplicative independence. They and their inverses are made integral by $q'=q^{2U}$, and their conjugates are between $e^{-T'}$ and $e^{T'}$, where $T'=2UT$. Thus §1 applies with
$$w'=32+\lceil\log_2B\rceil+dT',\qquad E_2=2^{119}d^8(T')^4(w')^5.$$
It proves
$$|\Lambda|>e^{-E_2}/U.\tag{T8}$$

Everything is polynomial in $w$. Indeed
$$U=512m^4t^2,\quad T'=2^{10}m^4t^2T,\quad w'\le2dT'.$$
For the last inequality, $t\ge w$ and $dT\ge2$ give $dT'=2UdT\ge2048w^2\ge w$, whereas $w'=w-dT+dT'\le w+dT'$.
Consequently
$$E_2\le2^{124}d^{13}(T')^9
=2^{1294}d^{67}T^{45}w^{18}.$$
Also $\log U<E_2$, and
$$E_{\rm det}\le2^7wm^{12}t^6=2^{367}d^{18}T^{12}w^7.$$
These estimates, the short-height case (N5), and the determinant case prove (T1), with the stated spare factor $2^2$ in the exponent. Finally $\lceil\log_2B\rceil\le1+2\log B$ and $33+dT\ge2$ imply $w\le(33+dT)(1+\log B)$, proving (T2).

## 3. A complete polynomial zero estimate

This note supplies a proved zero estimate, with deliberately loose constants, for the special positive-real translation grids needed in P14. The only analytic detour is a proof that two complex polynomial equations in three variables cannot have an isolated common zero. That detour is spelled out rather than concealed inside a geometric dimension theorem.

#### Z0. Statement

Let `K >= L >= 1` be integers and let `Sigma_1,Sigma_2,Sigma_3` be finite subsets of

`G = R^2 x R_{>0}`,

with group law `(x,y,z)+(x',y',z')=(x+x',y+y',zz')`, each containing `(0,0,1)`. Assume:

1. For every nonzero complex pair `(a,b)`, the set `{a*x+b*y:(x,y,z) in Sigma_1}` has more than `2K` elements; the set of third coordinates of `Sigma_1` has more than `L` elements.
2. The set of third coordinates of `Sigma_2` has more than `16KL` elements; the set of first-two-coordinate pairs of `Sigma_2` has more than `4K^2` elements.
3. `#Sigma_3 > 128K^2L`.

Then no nonzero polynomial `P in C[X,Y,Z]` with partial degrees at most `(K,K,L)` vanishes on `Sigma_1+Sigma_2+Sigma_3`.

All algebraic sets and components below are taken in the complex open set `Omega=C^2 x C*`; only the finite translation sets lie in G. In particular, a component meeting Omega must be considered even if it has no point in the positive real group G. The use of positive third coordinates for translations removes finite root-of-unity stabilizers. There is no assumption that the coefficients of P are algebraic. The result is proved below, including the polynomial intersection counts.

#### Z1. Algebraic preliminaries without a quoted intersection theorem

#### Z1.1. Finite monomial bases and Hilbert polynomials

For a space I of polynomial relations in `C[X_0,...,X_q]` closed under addition and multiplication by arbitrary polynomials (a polynomial ideal), suppose first that I is homogeneous, and let H_I(n) be the dimension of the degree-n quotient. Everything about these relation spaces used below is proved by polynomial division and finite linear algebra; no theorem about ideals in a number field is involved. Order monomials by degree and then lexicographically. A polynomial's largest monomial is its leading monomial.

Every set of monomials closed under multiplication has finitely many minimal elements under divisibility. Here is Dickson's argument. Every infinite sequence in `N^r` has two terms, in their original order, with the earlier term coordinatewise at most the later one. For r=1 this is immediate: either a value occurs infinitely often, or one extracts a strictly increasing subsequence. For larger r, extract a subsequence nondecreasing in the first coordinate and apply induction to its remaining coordinates. An infinite list of distinct minimal monomials would contradict this property.

Choose polynomials in I giving those finitely many minimal leading monomials. Polynomial division terminates (within any fixed degree there are only finitely many ordered monomials), and shows that the monomials not in the leading-monomial ideal form a basis of the quotient: they span by division, and a dependence would have a leading monomial belonging to that ideal. This finite-generation argument also applies to nonhomogeneous affine relation spaces: degree-then-lexicographic division never increases the degree of the polynomial being reduced, so only finitely many decreasing leading monomials can occur. Divide by the selected polynomials; a nonzero remainder of a polynomial in I is impossible. Thus every polynomial relation space closed under multiplication is finitely generated. Consequently ascending chains of such spaces stabilize and descending chains of algebraic sets stabilize. The usual finite irreducible decomposition follows directly: if a closed set that has no finite decomposition into irreducibles existed, split it into two proper closed sets, choose one with the same property, and repeat, contradicting the descending-chain property.

Write the finitely many leading monomial generators as M_1,...,M_s. Inclusion-exclusion counts the monomials of degree n not divisible by any M_i:

`H_I(n) = sum_{S subset {1,...,s}} (-1)^|S| binom(n-deg(lcm(M_i:i in S))+q,q)`.

For all sufficiently large n every summand is a polynomial in n. Thus H_I is eventually a polynomial. This proof applies to every homogeneous ideal, without a radical or regular-sequence hypothesis.

For a projective algebraic set V use its homogeneous vanishing ideal I(V), and write H_V for this polynomial function. If V is the projective closure of an affine set, H_V(n) also equals the dimension of the restrictions of affine polynomials of total degree at most n: homogenize them to degree n using the homogenizing coordinate and dehomogenize back. A homogeneous polynomial vanishes on the affine set precisely when it vanishes on its projective closure. This identification is used in every growth argument below. If V is irreducible its coordinate ring is a domain: a product vanishes on V only if one factor vanishes on V, because V cannot be a union of two proper closed subsets.

For a finite set of m projective points, `H_V(n)=m` for all sufficiently large n. Evaluation at the points gives the upper bound m. For the lower bound, for each point choose, for every other point, a linear form vanishing at that other point but not at the chosen point. Their product separates the chosen point. A linear form nonzero at all m points adjusts the degrees upward. Conversely, if H_V is bounded, V is finite: apply these separating polynomials to arbitrarily many distinct points if V were infinite. Therefore an infinite set whose Hilbert polynomial has degree at most one has a polynomial `d*n+O(1)`, with d>0. Call d its degree when the set is irreducible; these are precisely the curves needed below.

If distinct irreducible curves V_i are components of a closed set V whose Hilbert polynomial is at most `c*n+O(1)`, then the sum of their degrees is at most c. Choose a homogeneous polynomial A_i vanishing on every other component but not identically on V_i (multiply one separating polynomial for each other component). The maps given by multiplication by A_i inject shifted copies of the coordinate rings of V_i into the coordinate ring of V, and their images have direct sum: restriction to V_i isolates the i-th image. Thus `H_V(n) >= sum_i H_{V_i}(n-deg A_i)`, proving the assertion by comparing the linear terms. This avoids invoking cycle additivity or a nonreduced intersection formula.

#### Z1.2. The only null-set algebra needed

We need only the following special divisibility statement, proved by a one-variable Euclidean algorithm: if f is irreducible in `C[X_1,...,X_n]` and g vanishes at every zero of f, then f divides g. Choose a variable, say X_n, in which f has positive degree. Its coefficients, viewed as polynomials in the remaining variables, have no nonconstant common factor. Gauss's polynomial lemma therefore makes f irreducible over `C(X_1,...,X_{n-1})`. If it did not divide g, the Euclidean algorithm over that field would give `A f+B g=1`. Clear all denominators to obtain polynomial A,B and a nonzero polynomial `D(X_1,...,X_{n-1})` with `A f+B g=D`. Specialize the first n-1 variables at a point where D and the leading X_n-coefficient of f are nonzero. Such a point exists: a nonzero polynomial over an infinite field is not identically zero as a function, by induction on its variables. The specialized f has a complex root, and at that root the identity forces g nonzero, a contradiction. For n=1 this is ordinary polynomial division.

The same divisibility conclusion holds if g is known to vanish only on `V(f) cap {Z!=0}`, provided f is not the polynomial Z up to a constant. In the preceding specialization, also require Z nonzero if it is among the base variables. If Z is the last variable, the constant coefficient of the irreducible f in Z is nonzero (otherwise f would equal Z up to a unit), so require that coefficient nonzero as well. The resulting root then has Z nonzero and gives the same contradiction.

Here Gauss's lemma requires no arithmetic ideal argument. Over a polynomial ring over a field, primitive polynomials have primitive products: modulo any irreducible coefficient factor they remain nonzero and their product remains nonzero in the resulting integral domain. Comparing contents after clearing denominators proves the lemma, and induction on the number of variables proves the unique-factorization statement used to factor the polynomials.

The algebraic closedness of C needed here has the usual short analytic proof. A nonconstant complex polynomial has modulus tending to infinity, hence attains a minimum. If its minimum value at z_0 were nonzero, write `P(z_0+h)/P(z_0)=1+a h^j+O(h^(j+1))` with a!=0. Choose the argument of h so that a*h^j is negative real. For sufficiently small h the modulus is strictly below one, a contradiction. Thus every nonconstant polynomial has a root, and induction gives its complete factorization.

We will also use this finite-dimensional algebra fact. Every nonzero finite-dimensional commutative C-algebra has a homomorphism to C sending 1 to 1. Represent multiplication by a finite list of algebra generators as commuting matrices. The first has a nonzero eigenspace by the preceding root theorem. Every other multiplication matrix preserves that eigenspace. Restrict the next matrix to it and choose a nonzero eigenspace again; continue through the finite list. A resulting common eigenvector supplies eigenvalues lambda_i satisfying every polynomial relation between the generators, by applying that relation in the multiplication matrices to the eigenvector. Substituting the eigenvalues therefore gives the desired homomorphism. This is only finite linear algebra; no maximal ideals, residue fields, or polynomial zero theorem are invoked.

#### Z1.3. Intersection with one polynomial

If V is irreducible and a homogeneous polynomial F of degree d does not vanish identically on it, multiplication by F in its coordinate ring is injective. The degree-n quotient by F consequently has dimension

`H_V(n)-H_V(n-d)`.

It maps onto the degree-n coordinate ring of the reduced intersection `V cap {F=0}`. Thus the Hilbert function of the latter is at most that difference. In particular a degree-e curve meets such a polynomial in at most de points: the difference is eventually de, and the point-separating argument above applies. No scheme multiplicities are required for this inequality.

An irreducible degree-e homogeneous polynomial in three variables similarly defines a plane curve with Hilbert function `binom(n+2,2)-binom(n-e+2,2)=e*n+O(1)`. An irreducible degree-e homogeneous polynomial in four variables defines a surface with Hilbert function

`binom(n+3,3)-binom(n-e+3,3) = (e/2)n^2+O(n)`.

This uses the preceding null-set algebra to identify its vanishing ideal with its principal ideal. Intersecting it with a degree-d polynomial that does not contain it gives a reduced set with Hilbert function at most `ed*n+O(1)`.

#### Z2. The small analytic lemma that supplies purity

**Lemma.** Two polynomial equations in three complex variables have no isolated common zero.

Here are the analytic ingredients and their proofs. For an analytic function f(x,t), where x is one complex variable and t ranges over a small polydisc, suppose f(x,0) has a zero of multiplicity m at zero and no other zero in `|x|<=r`. For sufficiently small t it has exactly m zeros, counted with multiplicity, in that circle, and those zeros tend to zero as t tends to zero. Their monic polynomial F(x,t) has coefficients analytic in t and `F(x,0)=x^m`.

To prove this root-cluster assertion, use the following elementary contour calculation. An analytic function without boundary zeros has

`(1/(2*pi*i)) integral_{|x|=r} f_x/f dx`

equal to its number of interior zeros with multiplicity. Indeed factor out its finitely many interior zeros. The remaining logarithmic derivative is analytic and has zero integral. Analyticity makes zeros isolated unless the function is identically zero (factor its first nonzero Taylor term); compactness then makes their number finite. The integral of an analytic function around a disc is zero by the real Green formula and the Cauchy-Riemann equations. Green's formula here follows from the fundamental theorem of calculus and Fubini on rectangles, followed by approximation of the disc and any removed small circles. The integral of `(x-a)^{-1}` is `2*pi*i` for an interior a, as follows by a geometric-series expansion on a circle centered at zero; the same expansion gives zero for an exterior a. These observations prove the displayed zero count without quoting an argument principle.

For small t the straight-line homotopy between f(x,0) and f(x,t) has no boundary zero, by uniform continuity and the positive minimum of |f(x,0)| on the circle. The displayed integral varies continuously along that homotopy and is integer-valued, so it is constant m. The same argument with arbitrarily smaller circles proves convergence of all m zeros to zero.

For j=1,...,m the integrals

`s_j(t)=(1/(2*pi*i)) integral_{|x|=r} x^j f_x/f dx`

are their power sums. They are analytic in t: on the circle the inverse denominator expands in a uniformly convergent geometric series for sufficiently small t, so one may integrate its convergent power series termwise. Newton's finite recursion

`j e_j = sum_{i=1}^j (-1)^(i-1) e_{j-i} s_i`, with `e_0=1`,

gives analytic elementary symmetric functions. The monic polynomial with these coefficients is F. This recursion is proved by differentiating the formal finite product `prod(1+root_i z)` and comparing coefficients in its logarithmic derivative. The root-cluster assertion is established. The same proof works for a general analytic f, not merely a polynomial.

Now suppose f and g have an isolated common zero at the origin. At least one polynomial is nonzero; call it f. If necessary choose a linear coordinate x for which f(x,0,0) is not identically zero; the first nonzero homogeneous part of f gives such a direction. Form its root-cluster polynomial F(x,y,z). Divide the polynomial g by this monic polynomial in x. The remainder has degree less than m and coefficients analytic in (y,z). Let R(y,z) be the determinant of multiplication by that remainder in the m-dimensional algebra with basis `1,x,...,x^(m-1)` and relation F=0. It is analytic in (y,z). Its vanishing is equivalent to a common root: for distinct roots of F a Vandermonde basis diagonalizes this multiplication, so its determinant is the product of the values of the remainder at those roots; continuity proves the identity for repeated roots as well. Thus R(0,0)=0. This construction avoids any convention about resultants when the remainder's degree drops.

If R is identically zero, every nearby pair (y,z) gives a common root x of f and g tending to zero, contradicting isolation. Otherwise select a linear y-direction for which R(y,0) is not identically zero. Apply the same analytic root-cluster assertion to R as a function of y. For every sufficiently small nonzero z there is y tending to zero with R(y,z)=0, and then a common root x tending to zero. This is again a contradiction. This proves the lemma.

It follows that a proper intersection of two projective hypersurfaces in P^3 has no zero-dimensional component: such a component would be a point isolated from the finitely many other closed components and would contradict the lemma in an affine chart. Combined with the Hilbert bound in Z1.3, all its components are curves. This is the only purity assertion used.

#### Z3. Polynomial intersection counts

#### Z3.1. Ordinary two- and three-variable bounds

Two polynomials of total degrees d,e in two variables, with no common nonconstant factor, have at most de common zeros. Homogenize in three variables at their actual degrees (without multiplying by an extra homogenizing-coordinate power), factor the first polynomial into irreducibles, apply the injective-multiplication Hilbert calculation on each resulting plane curve, and sum the resulting bounds. Components at infinity may be discarded and do not increase the count. The homogenizations remain coprime: a common factor that becomes constant on dehomogenizing would be a power of the homogenizing coordinate, and neither actual-degree homogenization has such a factor.

For three polynomials of total degrees at most D in three variables, suppose the first two have no common hypersurface and the third contains no common curve meeting the open set being counted. Homogenize in four variables at their actual degrees. The first polynomial's irreducible factors have total degrees summing at most D. By Z1.3 the intersection with the second has total curve degree at most D^2; by Z2 there are no additional isolated components. Discard curves outside the open set. Each remaining curve has at most D times its degree zeros of the third polynomial, by Z1.3. Therefore there are at most D^3 counted common zeros. If the open set is `Z != 0`, first remove all powers of Z from the polynomials. A common hypersurface supported in its complement is then impossible; common curves supported in its complement are simply discarded. None of these operations increases the degrees.

#### Z3.2. Weighted bounds for partial degrees (K,K,L)

Put `m=floor(K/L)`, so `m>=K/(2L)`. Substitute `Z=W^m`.

For three polynomials with partial degrees at most (K,K,L), the transformed total degree is at most `2K+mL<=3K`. On `Z!=0` every point has m distinct preimages. If the first two form a proper intersection there and the third contains none of their curves there, their number of common points is therefore at most

`(3K)^3/m <=54 K^2 L <128 K^2 L`.

The finite covering preserves the relevant dimension assertions. An especially direct proof for a potential common hypersurface upstairs is this: if its image lay in a finite union V of curves and points downstairs, reduce every polynomial of degree at most n in X,Y,W using `W^m=Z`. It becomes `sum_{j=0}^{m-1} W^j A_j(X,Y,Z)`, where every A_j has degree at most n. Its restrictions therefore span at most `m H_V(n)=O(n)` dimensions. That contradicts the quadratic Hilbert growth of a hypersurface. A curve upstairs cannot map to a point because all fibres have at most m points. This proves the properties needed to pull back a proper intersection. Here is also a field-based verification. A curve's coordinate field has transcendence degree one: a nonconstant coordinate X is transcendental and every other coordinate is algebraic over C(X), because two algebraically independent coordinates would contradict linear Hilbert growth. Adjoining W with `W^m=Z` does not change that transcendence degree. Conversely a finitely generated coordinate algebra whose field is algebraic over C(X) has at most linear Hilbert growth: choose a finite field basis, write multiplication by each coordinate as a matrix over C(X), clear a common polynomial denominator D, and reduce every word of length at most n. The result has denominator D^n and numerator degree at most c*n for a fixed c, in that fixed basis. It spans only O(n) dimensions over C. Thus no hypersurface (whose Hilbert growth is quadratic) can lie over a curve. A component over a point is finite: after specialization W satisfies a nonzero monic one-variable equation, while the other coordinates are fixed. Hence a common hypersurface upstairs would give a common hypersurface downstairs, and a common curve upstairs would give a common curve downstairs. These are exactly the properties needed to apply the ordinary bounds after substitution and after discarding components in W=0.

For two polynomials in variables Y,Z with partial degrees at most (K,L), the same substitution gives total degrees at most 2K, hence at most `4K^2/m<=8KL` common zeros in Z!=0 when there is no common curve there.

For two polynomials P,Q of partial degrees at most (K,K,L), without a common hypersurface in Omega, their common curves are finite in number. The number of those on which X is nonconstant is at most 8KL: choose a generic slice X=c, and use the preceding planar bound. The same bound applies with Y. Consequently the total number of nonvertical common curves is at most 16KL. The vertical curves are of the form `(x0,y0,C*)`; a generic slice Z=c counts them using planar total degrees at most 2K and gives at most 4K^2.

Here "generic" can be replaced throughout by "outside a finite exceptional set". The justification, including existence of a point on each curve in such a slice, is given next.

#### Z3.3. Why generic slices meet the curves

Let V be an irreducible affine curve whose projective Hilbert function is `e*n+O(1)`. If X is nonconstant on V, then X is transcendental in its coordinate field: a nonzero one-variable polynomial has only finitely many roots, and irreducibility would otherwise force X constant. Every other coordinate is algebraic over C(X). Indeed, if X and a coordinate Y were algebraically independent, the monomials `X^iY^j`, i+j<=n, would give a quadratic lower bound for the Hilbert function, a contradiction. Clearing finitely many leading coefficients makes the coordinate ring, localized at a nonzero polynomial D(X), integral and finite over `C[X,1/D]`.

For every c with D(c)!=0 this finite algebra has a nonzero quotient by X-c. If the quotient were zero, then `1=(X-c)b` for an integral element b, and its monic equation would imply `1/(X-c)` belongs to `C[X,1/D]`, impossible. Apply the commuting-matrix eigenvector argument of Z1.2 to that nonzero finite-dimensional quotient. The resulting complex eigenvalues satisfy every coordinate relation and supply a point of V with X=c. If V is being considered in Omega, include the coordinate 1/Z among the finitely many algebra generators before clearing denominators; the resulting point then has Z nonzero. Equivalently, discard the finitely many X-values of the intersection of the projective curve with Z=0. The fibre is finite because the linear polynomial X-c is not identically zero on V and Z1.3 bounds its cardinality by e. Thus all but finitely many slices meet V in a finite nonempty set.

There are only finitely many components. A constant-coordinate component affects just one exceptional slice. Distinct irreducible curves meet in finitely many points: choose a polynomial vanishing on one but not the other and apply Z1.3 to the latter. Exclude the finitely many coordinate values of those pairwise intersections as well. Then the points supplied by different curves in a chosen slice are distinct. A common curve in a generic planar slice would create a component of the three-variable intersection contained in that slice; all such exceptional coordinate values have also been excluded. This proves every slicing assertion above, including the conversion of the planar point bound to a number of curve components. The same integral argument shows that a finite polynomial cover does not create a curve over a point: the quotient over a fixed point is a finite-dimensional algebra, so has finitely many points.

#### Z4. Positive stabilizers, proved by finite differences

For pairwise distinct nonzero complex numbers a_1,...,a_s, a sequence

`sum_i p_i(n) a_i^n`

with polynomial p_i cannot vanish for every nonnegative integer n unless all p_i are zero. To prove it, apply powers of the shift operators `E-a_j`, where `(Eu)(n)=u(n+1)`. On `p(n)a_i^n`, `E-a_i` lowers the polynomial degree by one, while `E-a_j` with j!=i preserves its degree and has nonzero leading multiplier a_i-a_j. Kill all summands except any selected one, obtaining a nonzero polynomial times a_i^n if that summand was nonzero, a contradiction. This also proves the asserted degree-lowering and preservation directly by expanding p(n+1).

Consequently the powers `(n v,z^n)` of a group element `(v,z)` with positive z!=1 have algebraic closure containing `C*v x C*`: substitute them in any Laurent polynomial, collect its powers of z^n, and use the preceding independence to show that each polynomial coefficient vanishes for every complex scalar multiplying v. If z=1 and v!=0, its powers have closure containing `C*v x {1}` by ordinary polynomial root counting.

Suppose an irreducible curve V is fixed by a positive-real translation g=(v,z). Every point of V plus all integer powers of g lies in V, and therefore so does that point plus the algebraic closure just described. If z!=1 and v!=0, V would contain a two-parameter set with two algebraically independent coordinates, contrary to its linear Hilbert growth. If z!=1 and v=0, V contains a vertical line and hence equals that line: a proper closed subset of an irreducible curve is finite, by Z1.3 applied to a polynomial separating a missing point. Therefore every nonvertical curve's positive stabilizers have z=1.

For an irreducible hypersurface V defined by f, if a positive stabilizer has z!=1 then the preceding closure makes V invariant under every multiplication of its third coordinate. Write f=sum_j f_j(X,Y) Z^j. For every point of V, all f_j vanish on its first coordinates. Some nonzero f_j therefore vanishes identically on V; the null-set algebra implies f divides f_j, forcing f to be independent of Z. Thus a hypersurface genuinely involving Z has no positive stabilizer with z!=1.

For a hypersurface f(X,Y)=0 independent of Z, its additive stabilizer is a complex linear subspace of C^2: the closure of powers of any translation gives its entire scalar line, and sums of stabilizing translations also stabilize. It is proper, because invariance under all translations would force the hypersurface to be all of C^2. It is consequently contained in the kernel of some nonzero linear form aX+bY.

Translations preserve each polynomial's partial degrees and the degree in Z. Distinct translated irreducible hypersurfaces give distinct irreducible factors, up to scalars, of any polynomial vanishing on them, by Z1.2. There are at most L mixed hypersurface factors (their Z-degrees sum to at most L), and at most 2K pure-additive hypersurface factors (their total additive degrees sum to at most 2K). These elementary factor counts replace multidegrees at the hypersurface stage.

#### Z5. Proof of the zero estimate

Suppose P is nonzero, with the stated partial degrees, and vanishes on the threefold sumset. Remove powers of Z, which has no zero on Omega; call the result P_1.

For each irreducible hypersurface V of P_1 meeting Omega, there is some g in Sigma_1 such that V+g is not a hypersurface component of P_1. Otherwise all its distinct translates would be factors of P_1. If V genuinely involves Z, distinct third coordinates of g produce distinct translates by Z4, giving more than L factors. If V is pure additive, its stabilizer is contained in one linear kernel, so the assumed more than 2K values of the corresponding linear form produce more than 2K distinct factors. Both are impossible.

Thus for each such V at least one translated polynomial `P_1(.+g)` does not vanish identically on V. A complex linear combination P_2 of these finitely many translated polynomials can be chosen not to vanish identically on any such V: each failed condition is a proper linear subspace of the finite coefficient space, and finitely many proper subspaces cannot cover that space (choose a line not contained in them, or successively avoid their finitely many defining linear equations). P_2 still has partial degrees at most (K,K,L), and vanishes on Sigma_2+Sigma_3. The first two polynomials have no common hypersurface in Omega.

Their common curves meeting Omega number at most 16KL if nonvertical and at most 4K^2 if vertical, by Z3.2. For each such curve V, some g in Sigma_2 has V+g not a common-curve component. Otherwise: if V is nonvertical, distinct third coordinates of Sigma_2 give distinct translates by Z4, contradicting the 16KL bound; if V is vertical, distinct additive coordinate pairs give distinct vertical translates, contradicting 4K^2.

For that g, at least one of P_1(.+g), P_2(.+g) does not vanish identically on V. Choose a generic linear combination P_3 of all these finitely many translated polynomials, avoiding all common curves. It has the same partial-degree bounds and vanishes on Sigma_3, because P_1 and P_2 both vanish on Sigma_2+Sigma_3 (the origin belongs to Sigma_1). The weighted three-polynomial bound now gives at most 128K^2L common points in Omega, whereas all of Sigma_3 consists of common points. This contradicts assumption 3 and proves Z0.

For clarity, norm one already makes `v` a unit of the order: multiplication by `v` has an integer matrix and determinant one, whose adjugate is its integer inverse; applying it to `1` gives an inverse in `A`. The proof above only needs multiplication by the already explicit generator inverses, but this observation confirms the terminology.

### 4.4. A fixed-offset two-index separator without LLL

Write `||x||=min_{n in Z}|x-n|`.

**Lemma (PROVED).** Let `alpha,nu` be real, `B>=1` an integer, and `p,q` integers with `q>=1`. Suppose exact bounds prove
\[
 |q\alpha-p|\le d,\qquad \|q\nu\|\ge e_0,
 \qquad \epsilon=e_0-Bd>0.
\]
Then for every integer `r,s` with `|r|<=B` and either sign,
\[
\boxed{|r\alpha+s\pm\nu|\ge\epsilon/q.}
\]
There is no restriction on `s`.

**Proof.** Subtract the integer `rp+sq` from `q(r alpha+s±nu)`. The result is `r(qalpha-p)±qnu`. Its distance from the integers is at least `||qnu||-|r||qalpha-p|`, by the triangle inequality after minimizing over integers. Distance from the integers is at most the absolute value of the original expression. Divide by `q`. The same distance applies to `qnu` and `-qnu`, proving both signs. ∎

This is genuinely a separator on a two-index box with a fixed third coefficient. If it is presented as `h_1 a+h_2 b+h_3`, `h_3=±1` and `b!=0`, divide by `b` and apply the lemma with `alpha=a/b`, `nu=1/b`, `r=h_1`, `s=h_2`; restore the factor `|b|`. The coefficient being fixed is essential to this particularly simple certificate.

The finite data are just `q,p`, rational intervals for `alpha,nu`, `B`, and the positive rational margin `epsilon`. If `alpha` lies in `[a_-,a_+]`, take
\[
d=\max(|qa_--p|,|qa_+-p|).
\]
For `[v_-,v_+]` containing `qnu`, if its two endpoints have different integer parts use lower distance zero. Otherwise let their common integer part be `n` and take
\[
e_0=\min(v_--n,n+1-v_+).
\]
Every quantity is rational. A continued-fraction algorithm can propose `q,p`, but the verifier uses none of its convergence or optimality theorems; it only checks these interval inequalities. There is no lattice reduction in either the current search or verifier.

## 5. Q2: the two-logarithm criterion and further evaluations

### 5.1. The exact criterion

**PROVED (bounded-power criterion).** Suppose a certified tail has
$$0<|\Lambda|\le Ce^{-\kappa H},\qquad
\Lambda=\log|v|-\log|\gamma|.$$
Suppose two fixed algebraic-integer units $\epsilon,\eta$ and an explicit integer $Q$ have this property: for each point there is $1\le m\le Q$ with
$$|v|^m=|\epsilon|^A|\eta|^B,\qquad
|\gamma|^m=|\epsilon|^{a_m}|\eta|^{b_m},$$
and $\max(|A-a_m|,|B-b_m|)\le cQ(H+1)$ for an explicit $c$. Then
$$m\Lambda=(A-a_m)\log|\epsilon|+(B-b_m)\log|\eta|\ne0.$$
P13's two-logarithm theorem applies once its degree and conjugate bounds have been supplied. Dividing by $m\le Q$ costs at most $\log Q$ in the lower-bound exponent. Thus the tail has an explicit finite cutoff by comparison with $Ce^{-\kappa H}$.

The test concerns **ratios of root differences and the projected unit group**. Individual root differences need not be units. Displaying two units or restricting to two indices does not prove that the fixed coefficient belongs to their group. A nonzero power of an algebraic number can be an algebraic-integer unit only if the number itself is such a unit: apply transitivity of integrality to the monic equations for that power and its inverse. Transitivity follows from the same finite-monomial/adjugate argument as P13's ring lemma. Norm one alone is insufficient.

Thomas and its exact $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb Z)$ changes of variables satisfy this criterion. Here is a further family with a complete per-fibre effectivity proof.

### 5.2. Simplest quartics: an unconditional two-logarithm reduction

Consider the explicitly specified simplest/Gras quartic family
$$F_t(X,Y)=X^4-tX^3Y-6X^2Y^2+tXY^3+Y^4,
\qquad t\ge1,\quad t\ne3.$$
**PROVED:** every fixed fibre $|F_t(x,y)|=1$ has the explicit cutoff below using only P13's two-logarithm theorem. A full rank-three unit basis is unnecessary.

**Field and coefficient.** Put $f_t(X)=F_t(X,1)$ and $R=t+3$. Irreducibility follows directly. There are no rational roots since the possible integral roots $\pm1$ both give $-4$. A monic integral quadratic factorization has constants both $1$ or both $-1$. In the first case cubic and linear coefficients force $t=0$; in the second they require $ac=-4$, $a+c=-t$, forcing $t\in\{0,\pm3\}$. Gauss's lemma permits this integral comparison.

There is a root $r\in(2,R)$, since $f_t(2)=-7-6t<0$ and $f_t(R)=3R^2(R-2)+tR+1>0$. The identity
$$ (X+1)^4 f_t((X-1)/(X+1))=-4f_t(X)$$
gives its four distinct real conjugates
$$r_0=r,\quad r_1=s=(r-1)/(r+1),\quad r_2=-1/r,\quad r_3=-1/s.$$
The Möbius map defines an automorphism $\sigma$ of order four. All roots and their inverses are integral, their absolute values are between $1/R$ and $R$, and every root gap exceeds $1/2$. For the only small adjacent gap use
$$s+1/r=(r^2+1)/(r(r+1))>1/2;$$
the other two adjacent gaps exceed one. On the tail at $r_k$, choose $i=k+1,j=k+3$. Direct subtraction, followed by conjugation, proves
$$\boxed{\gamma_k=(r_k-r_{k+1})/(r_k-r_{k+3})=r_{k+1}.}\tag{F1}$$
The fixed coefficient is a root unit.

**Coverage of the needed relative-unit group.** Let $V$ consist of the totally positive integral units $v$ with $\sigma^2v=v^{-1}$. Write $L=\log r>0$, $M=\log s<0$. Its log vectors have the shape $(z_0,z_1,-z_0,-z_1)$. The vectors for $r^2,s^2$ span this plane, with determinant $-4(L^2+M^2)$. Subtract nearest integers from the two coefficients of any vector. The resulting unit $z$ in its coset modulo $\langle r^2,s^2\rangle$ satisfies
$$|\sigma^i z|\le e^{L+|M|}=r/s<3R.$$
For $0\le j<4$ the four traces $\operatorname{Tr}(zr^j)$ are integers of absolute value below $12R^{j+1}$. These traces distinguish $z$: their differences give the invertible Vandermonde system in the four conjugates of $z-z'$. Hence there are at most
$$Q=\prod_{j=0}^3(24R^{j+1}+1)\tag{F2}$$
cosets. Pigeonhole $1,v,\ldots,v^Q$ to get
$$v^m=r^{2A}s^{2B}\quad\text{for some }1\le m\le Q.\tag{F3}$$
This proves the required finite-power coverage for the actual relative units. Integral traces follow by taking the trace of the monic integer minimal polynomial, with the appropriate field-degree multiplicity. No general unit theorem is used.

**Tail and coefficient estimates.** Let $u_i=x-r_i y$ and $|y|\ge4R$. Their absolute product is one. The nearest root to $x/y$ is within $1/|y|\le1/4$; the other three distances exceed $1/4$. Thus $|u_k|\le64|y|^{-3}$ and $|u_j|\ge|y|/4$ for $j\ne k$. The exact identity
$$\frac{u_i/u_j}{\gamma_k}-1
=\frac{r_i-r_j}{r_k-r_i}\frac{u_k}{u_j}$$
is nonzero and has modulus at most $1024R|y|^{-4}\le1/2$. Integration of $1/(1+x)$ gives
$$0<|\Lambda_k|\le2048R|y|^{-4},\qquad
\Lambda_k=\log|u_i/u_j|-\log|\gamma_k|.\tag{F4}$$
The nonvanishing follows because $u_k\ne0$ and the roots are distinct; a nonzero real error of size at most $1/2$ cannot have logarithm zero. The unit $(u_i/u_j)^2$ belongs to $V$. Apply (F3):
$$m\log|u_i/u_j|=AL+BM.$$
Since $\log|\gamma_k|$ is one of $M,-L,-M,L$, this makes $m\Lambda_k$ a nonzero two-logarithm form.

Its coefficients are controlled explicitly. The norm equation forces $|x|\le R|y|+1$, so $|u_i|\le D=3R|y|$ and $|u_i|\ge D^{-3}$. Every log ratio of two conjugates is therefore at most $4\log D$ in absolute value. Inverting the two log equations and using $(L+|M|)/(L^2+M^2)\le2/L\le4$ gives $|A|,|B|\le16m\log D$. With $H=\log|y|\ge1$, $\log D\le2H$; after subtracting the fixed coefficient the two integer coefficients are at most $33QH$.

**Explicit bound.** P13 applies to $r,1/s$ with degree $4$ and budget $T=R$. Indeed $L>\log2\ge1/2>1/R$ and
$$-M=\int_{r-1}^{r+1}dx/x\ge2/(r+1)>1/R.$$
Define
$$W=34+4\lceil\log_2R\rceil+\lceil\log_2Q\rceil,
\qquad K_t=2^{92}4^8R^{12}W^5,\qquad H_t=(10^5K_t)^2.\tag{F5}$$
The bracket in P13 (L2), using coefficient bound $33QH$, is at most $W+\log H\le W(1+\log H)$. Division by $m\le Q$ is absorbed by the extra factor two in $K_t$. Hence $|\Lambda_k|>e^{-K_t(1+\log H)^5}$. For $H\ge1$, maximizing $(1+\log H)^5/\sqrt H$ gives the bound $2\cdot10^5$. If $H>H_t$, its lower-bound exponent is less than $2H$, whereas (F4) has exponent $-4H+\log(2048R)$ and $\log(2048R)<2H_t$. This is impossible. The omitted $|y|<4R$ range is already smaller. Therefore
$$\boxed{|y|\le e^{H_t},\qquad |x|\le Re^{H_t}+1.}\tag{F6}$$
This is per-fibre effectivity, not a completed uniform classification. A smaller certified relative-unit index improves the constants but is not a prerequisite for finiteness.

The finite reduction uses only one rational approximation. From $H\le H_*$, certify coprime $p,q$ with
$$q>33QH_*,\qquad |L/(-M)-p/q|\le1/[2q(33QH_*)].$$
P13's rational lemma gives $|m\Lambda_k|\ge(-M)/(2q)$, hence
$$H\le\tfrac14\log\frac{4096RQq}{-M}.\tag{F7}$$
Use outward rational intervals and iterate. Sol/Astra can start with $t=1,2,4,6$, compute compact relative-unit boxes, then run these chains and the final finite cover.

### 5.3. Ennola cubics: the fixed coefficient fails the test

For $g_a(X)=X^3+(a-1)X^2-aX-1$, $a\ge3$, the displayed numbers $r,r-1$ are units. That does not imply the tail coefficient is a unit. A counterexample occurs already at $a=3$.

The exact discriminant is $(a^2+3a-1)^2-32$, positive and nonsquare: for $n=a^2+3a-1\ge17$ it lies strictly between $(n-1)^2$ and $n^2$. The cubic is irreducible by the rational-root test. Its splitting field has degree six, since the remaining two roots require the discriminant's square root, which cannot lie in a degree-three field.

For ordered roots $r_0>r_1>r_2$, put $\gamma=(r_0-r_1)/(r_0-r_2)$. For a monic cubic $X^3+bX^2+cX+d$, setting $A=r_0-r_1$, $B=r_0-r_2$ gives
$$A^2-AB+B^2=b^2-3c,\qquad \operatorname{disc}=A^2B^2(A-B)^2.$$
At $a=3$ this yields the coefficient polynomial
$$257(X^2-X+1)^3-2197X^2(X-1)^2.$$
It is the minimal polynomial up to scaling: all six root permutations give the six anharmonic ratios of $\gamma$; for real $0<\gamma<1$ a repetition would force $\gamma=1/2$, whereas the depressed cubic constant $43/27\ne0$ excludes roots in arithmetic progression. The monic rational polynomial is
$$X^6-3X^5-\frac{655}{257}X^4+\frac{2595}{257}X^3
-\frac{655}{257}X^2-3X+1.$$
It is nonintegral. Thus this coefficient, or any nonzero power, cannot be absorbed into a group of algebraic-integer units. This disproves the proposed blanket two-logarithm reduction for the Ennola family; a different cover would need a new exact identity.

### 5.4. Simplest sextics: a proved subgroup statement

For
$$f_t^{(6)}(X)=X^6-2tX^5-5(t+3)X^4-20X^3+5tX^2+2(t+3)X+1,$$
the Möbius orbit is
$$r,\quad\frac{r-1}{r+2},\quad-\frac1{r+1},\quad
-\frac{r+2}{2r+1},\quad-\frac{r+1}{r},\quad-\frac{2r+1}{r-1}.$$
The certificate verifies $(X+2)^6 f_t^{(6)}((X-1)/(X+2))=-27f_t^{(6)}(X)$ and the identity
$$f_t^{(6)}(X)=[X^3-tX^2-(t+3)X-1]^2-(t^2+3t+9)X^2(X+1)^2.$$
For an irreducible totally real fibre, $r,r+1$ are integral units, since the constant term and the value at $-1$ are both one. On the **specified subgroup** $\{\pm r^A(r+1)^B\}$, choose the even pair $k+2,k+4$. Subtraction gives
$$\frac{r_k-r_{k+2}}{r_k-r_{k+4}}=\frac{r_k}{r_k+1},$$
and the exact tail form is
$$\Lambda_k=(A+2B-1)L_k+(-2A-B+1)M_k,
\quad L_k=\log|r_k|,\quad M_k=\log|r_k+1|.$$
The even conjugates prove independence by the same matrix determinant $L_k^2-L_kM_k+M_k^2>0$ as Thomas; the two logs cannot both vanish for real $r_k$. Exact zero would force $A=B=1/3$. P13 therefore applies after the fibre's root and conjugate bounds are supplied.

This does not cover the full sextic unit orbit. On its five-dimensional norm-one real log hyperplane, $\sigma^2-\sigma^4$ has a one-dimensional kernel of alternating period-two vectors, so its image has dimension four. An independently proved rank-two cover or marker elimination is necessary. The displayed polynomial is the precise family meant here; no assertion is made for every polynomial called a Lehmer or Gras family.
