## 1. A self-contained explicit two-logarithm theorem

### 1.1. Precise hypotheses and constants

**PROVED (explicit two-logarithm lower bound).** Let $K\subset\mathbb R$ be a number field of degree $d\ge1$, with its chosen real embedding. Let $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ be algebraic-integer units: both $\alpha_i$ and $\alpha_i^{-1}$ are algebraic integers. Assume they are positive and greater than one in the chosen embedding. Let $T\ge2$ be an integer such that

$$T^{-1}\le\log\alpha_i\le T,\qquad e^{-T}\le|\sigma(\alpha_i)|\le e^T\quad(i=1,2)$$

for every embedding $\sigma:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb C$. Negative logarithms can be made positive by replacing their units by their inverses; when doing that, the stated conjugate bounds are required for the replaced units. The logarithm of the unit $1$ is simply removed.

For integers $h_1,h_2$, put

$$\Lambda=h_1\log\alpha_1+h_2\log\alpha_2\ne0,\qquad B=\max(3,|h_1|,|h_2|).$$

Define $c_2(n)=\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$ for positive integers $n$, with $c_2(1)=0$, and

$$w=20+c_2(B)+2c_2(d)+4c_2(T),\qquad
\mathcal E(d,T,B)=2^{86}d^8T^{12}w^5.$$

Then

$$\boxed{|\Lambda|>\exp(-\mathcal E(d,T,B)).} \tag{L1}$$

In particular, using $\log2\ge1/2$,

$$\boxed{|\Lambda|>\exp\left[-2^{91}d^8T^{12}
  (20+\log B+2\log d+4\log T)^5\right].} \tag{L2}$$

No multiplicative independence hypothesis is necessary in the theorem: the displayed nonvanishing hypothesis suffices. In Thomas's application it follows independently from the elementary independence of the two unit logarithm vectors.

#### Elementary norm fact used

If $z$ is a nonzero algebraic integer in a degree-$d$ field, then $\prod_\sigma|\sigma z|\ge1$. Its minimal polynomial is monic with integer coefficients; the product over its distinct conjugates is its nonzero constant coefficient up to sign, hence is a nonzero integer. The product over embeddings of the whole field repeats that product $d/[\mathbb Q(z):\mathbb Q]$ times. Here is an elementary justification of the integrality facts being used. For finitely many integral generators, reduce powers by their monic equations to a finite list of generating monomials including $1$. This list spans an additive module stable under multiplication by every integer polynomial $z$ in the generators. Record the multiplication relations in an integer matrix $M$, without assuming the list is a basis. Multiplying the relations $(zI-M)v=0$ by the adjugate shows that $\det(zI-M)$ annihilates every generator, including $1$. This is a monic integer annihilating polynomial. Hence algebraic integers form a ring, and every determinant with integral entries is integral. The monic minimal polynomial of an algebraic integer has integer coefficients by Gauss's elementary polynomial lemma: primitive integer polynomials have primitive products, because reduction modulo any prime is a product of two nonzero polynomials in a field; clearing contents in a monic rational factorization then leaves monic integer factors. The product over embeddings is therefore the claimed nonzero integer power. In an explicitly presented order, it is also the determinant of multiplication. No freeness theorem for arbitrary integer modules, ideals, or class-group statement is used.

### 1.2. Direct exponential lower bound (used only for short coefficient pairs)

If $b_1,b_2$ are positive integers and
$\Lambda=b_2\log\alpha_2-b_1\log\alpha_1\ne0$, with $B=\max(3,b_1,b_2)$ and $|\Lambda|\le1$, then

$$|\Lambda|\ge e^{-3dTB}. \tag{L3}$$

Indeed $u=\alpha_1^{b_1}\alpha_2^{-b_2}$ is a unit, so $u-1$ is a nonzero algebraic integer. $|\sigma(u-1)|\le2e^{2TB}$ for every other embedding. The norm fact gives
$|u-1|\ge(2e^{2TB})^{-(d-1)}$. On the chosen embedding $u=e^{-\Lambda}$ and $|e^{-\Lambda}-1|\le3|\Lambda|$, by integrating $e^{-s}$ on the interval from $0$ to $\Lambda$ and using $e<3$. Therefore

$$|\Lambda|\ge3^{-1}2^{-(d-1)}e^{-2(d-1)TB}\ge e^{-3dTB}.$$

The last inequality follows from $T\ge2,B\ge3$ and $\log2<1,\log3<2$.

### 1.3. Quantitative real determinant lemma

Let $K_0,L_0$ be positive integers, $N=K_0L_0$, $A>0$, and
$0\le x_1<\cdots<x_N$. Rows are ordered lexicographically by $(\ell,k)$, with $0\le\ell<L_0$, $0\le k<K_0$. Then

$$D_0=\det[x_j^k e^{\ell A x_j}]_{(\ell,k),j}>0$$

and

$$D_0\ge
\frac{\prod_{i<j}(x_j-x_i)}{\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}n!}
\left(\prod_{\ell<\ell'}((\ell'-\ell)A)^{K_0^2}\right)
\left(\prod_{k=0}^{K_0-1}k!\right)^{L_0}. \tag{L4}$$

**Proof of every ingredient.** A nonzero linear combination of $q$ distinct real exponentials $e^{\nu_i t}$ has at most $q-1$ real zeros, counted with multiplicity. Discard zero coefficients first. For $q=1$ the assertion is immediate. For larger $q$, divide by the exponential of the smallest frequency, differentiate, and apply the induction hypothesis to the remaining $q-1$ exponentials. Rolle's theorem, including the derivative zeros at multiple roots, says that $m$ zeros of the original function force at least $m-1$ derivative zeros on the interval between the extreme zeros. The assertion on any finite list of zeros follows, hence the global assertion. Division by a nonvanishing function preserves multiplicities. This proves the induction.

Consequently, for strictly increasing nonnegative integers $n_1<\cdots<n_N$ and strictly increasing positive $y_i$, the generalized Vandermonde $\det[y_i^{n_j}]$ is nonzero: a singular matrix would give a nonzero combination with $N$ positive zeros, and putting $y=e^t$ contradicts the preceding lemma. Its sign is positive. Indeed its sign is constant on the connected region $0<y_1<\cdots<y_N$. Coalesce all $y_i$ to any fixed $y>0$. Taylor expansion shows its sign equals that of the Wronskian
$$
\det\left[\frac{d^{i-1}}{dy^{i-1}}y^{n_j}\right]_{i,j}
=y^{\sum n_j-N(N-1)/2}\prod_{i<j}(n_j-n_i)>0.
$$
The equality follows by factoring the powers of $y$ and observing that the falling factorial $(n)_i$ is a monic polynomial in $n$ of degree $i$; elementary row operations turn its evaluation determinant into the ordinary Vandermonde. The Taylor leading coefficient is this Wronskian times the positive Vandermonde of the relative node displacements divided by $\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}n!$. Continuity permits $y_1=0$ and gives nonnegativity there.

For $0\le\lambda_1<\cdots<\lambda_N$, first truncate the exponential series and expand the determinant by multilinearity. Terms with a repeated monomial degree have two equal monomial rows and vanish. Group the remaining terms by their increasing degree set; the signed sum over its assignments to the rows is exactly $\det[\lambda_i^{n_j}]$. This proves the finite Cauchy--Binet identity needed here directly. Now let the truncation increase. Uniform convergence of the exponential series on the finitely many bounded arguments justifies the limit. Equivalently the resulting multiple series is absolutely convergent, since the sum of absolute values before cancellations is bounded by $N!\prod_i e^{\lambda_i\max_jx_j}$. It gives
$$
\det[e^{\lambda_i x_j}]
=\sum_{0\le n_1<\cdots<n_N}
\frac{\det[\lambda_i^{n_j}]\det[x_i^{n_j}]}{\prod_j n_j!}.
$$
Every summand is nonnegative by the generalized Vandermonde result. Keeping $n_j=j-1$ gives
$$
\det[e^{\lambda_i x_j}]
\ge\frac{\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_j-\lambda_i)\prod_{i<j}(x_j-x_i)}{\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}n!}.
$$
For each $0\le\ell<L_0$, put $\lambda_{\ell,k}=\ell A+\varepsilon k$ for $0\le k<K_0$, with $\varepsilon>0$ small enough that the blocks remain ordered. Divide by all within-block Vandermonde factors and let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Taylor expansion in each block shows that the left side tends to $D_0/(\prod_{k=0}^{K_0-1}k!)^{L_0}$: successive row differences extract derivative rows, and $\partial_\lambda^k e^{\lambda x}=x^k e^{\lambda x}$. On the right the cross-block factors tend to $\prod_{\ell<\ell'}((\ell'-\ell)A)^{K_0^2}$. This proves (L4).


In particular, if $A\ge1/T$, $x_j-x_i\ge1/B$ for distinct nodes and $N,B,T\ge1$, then

$$D_0\ge\exp[-N^2\log(NBT)]. \tag{L5}$$

For the last assertion, bound the three potentially small factors in (L4) by $B^{-N(N-1)/2}$, $T^{-K_0^2L_0(L_0-1)/2}$ and $N^{-N(N-1)/2}$, and discard the other integer factors, each at least one.

#### Analytic upper bound, with no complex analysis

Let $X\ge\max(1,\max_jx_j)$, and put
$S_k=N(K_0-1)/2$, $S_\ell=N(L_0-1)/2$, $Q=N(N-1)/2$. Then for every $\rho>1$,

$$|D_0|\le N!\,\rho^{-Q}(\rho X)^{S_k}
       \exp(\rho A X S_\ell). \tag{L6}$$

Proof: set $D_0(z)=\det[(zx_j)^k e^{\ell A zx_j}]$. Expand every row in its absolutely convergent power series. Any term using the same monomial degree twice has zero determinant. Distinct nonnegative monomial degrees have sum at least $Q$, so $D_0(z)$ has no coefficient below degree $Q$. By the triangle inequality and the determinant permutation expansion, the sum of absolute coefficients weighted by $\rho^n$ is at most
$N!\prod_{(\ell,k)} (\rho X)^k e^{\ell A\rho X}$.
At $z=1$, remove the factor $\rho^Q$ from that weighted sum. This is (L6). All exchanges of sums are justified by the finite number of rows and absolute convergence of the exponential series. No maximum principle, Schwarz lemma or Cauchy estimate has been used.

### 1.4. Proof of the two-logarithm theorem

For this proof use the symmetric hypothesis

$$e^{-T}\le|\sigma\alpha_i|\le e^T\quad\hbox{for every embedding}. \tag{L7}$$

The coefficient pairs of the same sign, or with one coefficient zero, satisfy $|\Lambda|\ge1/T$, which is stronger than (L1). Otherwise, after changing an overall sign, write $\Lambda=b_2\log\alpha_2-b_1\log\alpha_1$ with $b_1,b_2>0$. Divide $b_1,b_2$ by their gcd. A bound for the resulting smaller coefficient height implies the same bound for the original pair, since $\mathcal E$ is nondecreasing in $B$ and multiplying the form by the positive gcd only increases its absolute value. Thus assume $\gcd(b_1,b_2)=1$.

Set

$$m=1024dT^3,\quad t=1024dw,\quad
K_0=m^2t,\quad L_0=t,\quad R=mt,\quad N=K_0L_0=R^2.$$

Then $64wN^2=\mathcal E(d,T,B)$. Suppose, for a contradiction, that

$$0<|\Lambda|\le e^{-64wN^2}. \tag{L8}$$

In particular $|\Lambda|<1$. Let $A=\log\alpha_1$, $\beta=b_1/b_2$. Since both logarithms lie in $[1/T,T]$, this implies

$$\max(b_1,b_2)\le2T^2\min(b_1,b_2),\qquad0<\beta\le2T^2. \tag{L9}$$

If $\min(b_1,b_2)<R$, then $B\le2T^2R$ (also when the artificial maximum $3$ defines $B$). By (L3),

$$|\Lambda|\ge e^{-6dT^3mt}>e^{-64w m^4t^4},$$

contrary to (L8). The strict final inequality follows immediately from $m=1024dT^3$, $m,t,w\ge1$. Therefore $R\le\min(b_1,b_2)$.

For all $0\le r,s<R$, define $x_{r,s}=r+\beta s$. These $N=R^2$ real numbers are distinct. Indeed equality would give $b_2(r-r')=-b_1(s-s')$; coprimality forces $b_1\mid r-r'$ and $b_2\mid s-s'$, while the differences have smaller absolute values. Moreover distinct nodes differ by at least $1/b_2\ge1/B$, and all lie in $[0,X]$ where $X=3T^2R$. Sort them in increasing order; use that same column order in each determinant below.

Define the algebraic determinant

$$\Delta=\det[(b_2r+b_1s)^k\alpha_1^{\ell r}\alpha_2^{\ell s}]_{(\ell,k),(r,s)}$$

and its normalized value $D=\Delta/b_2^{S_k}$. The corresponding ideal real determinant is $D_0$ from (L4). Since

$$\alpha_1^{\ell r}\alpha_2^{\ell s}
 =e^{\ell A x_{r,s}}e^{\ell s\Lambda/b_2},$$

$D$ differs from $D_0$ by a small, explicitly bounded perturbation.

Put

$$J=\log(N!)+S_k\log(2BR)+2TRNt.$$

If $\Delta\ne0$, its algebraic integrality and the norm fact imply

$$|D|\ge e^{-dJ}. \tag{L10}$$

Indeed each conjugate determinant has modulus at most
$N!(2BR)^{S_k}e^{2TRS_\ell}$, using $r+s<2R$ and the conjugate bounds. The other $d-1$ conjugates give a reciprocal lower bound for $|\Delta|$. Dividing by $b_2^{S_k}\le B^{S_k}$ only adds $S_k\log B$ to its negative logarithm. The definition of $dJ$ exceeds that sum, since $\log B\le\log(2BR)$ and $S_\ell\le Nt/2$.

Here are all parameter estimates, with generous rational slack. The definition of $w$ gives

$$w\ge20+\log B+2\log d+4\log T,\qquad
\log m\le w,\quad\log t\le w,\quad
\log R\le2w,\quad\log N\le4w.$$

For $\log t\le w$, use $\log1024<7$, $\log w\le w/2$ for $w\ge2$, and $w\ge20+2\log d$. Consequently

$$\log(2BR)\le4w,\quad \log(2X)\le6w,\quad
\log(NBT)\le6w,\quad\log(N!)\le4Nw.$$

Since $S_k\le N^2/(2t)$ and $RNt/N^2=1/m$, these give

$$dJ/N^2\le4dw/N+2dw/t+2dT/m<1,$$

and, by (L6) with $\rho=2$,

$$\log|D_0|+dJ
\le\left[\frac{4(d+1)w}{N}+\frac{(2d+3)w}{t}
+\frac{2dT+3T^3}{m}\right]N^2-Q\log2.$$

The bracket is at most $18/1024$: use $N\ge t$, $t=1024dw$, $m=1024dT^3$, $d\ge1$, $T\ge2$. Also $N\ge4$ and $\log2\ge1/2$ give $Q\log2\ge3N^2/16$. Because $3/16-18/1024>1/8$, it follows that

$$|D_0|\le e^{-dJ-N^2/8}. \tag{L11}$$

For the perturbation put $V=RNt$ and

$$P=\log(N!)+S_k\log X+TXNt.$$

Every determinant permutation term changes by a factor $e^v$ with $|v|\le V|\Lambda|$. If this is at most one, then $|e^v-1|\le3|v|$, so

$$|D-D_0|\le3V|\Lambda|e^P. \tag{L12}$$

Explicitly, $P/N^2\le4w/N+3w/t+3T^3/m\le10/1024<1$. Also $\log(12V)=\log12+\log R+\log N+\log t\le\log12+7w\le10w$. The lower bound (L5) gives $D_0\ge e^{-Q_0}$ with $Q_0=N^2\log(NBT)\le6wN^2$. Assumption (L8) both implies $V|\Lambda|\le1$ and gives

$$|D-D_0|\le\frac14\min(e^{-Q_0},e^{-dJ}), \tag{L13}$$

because $64wN^2>P+\log(12V)+\max(Q_0,dJ)$; the right side is bounded by $N^2+10w+6wN^2$, strictly less than the left for $w\ge20,N\ge4$.

The first part of (L13) implies $D>0$, hence $\Delta\ne0$. Thus (L10) applies. But (L11) and the second part of (L13) imply

$$|D|\le e^{-dJ}(e^{-N^2/8}+1/4)<e^{-dJ},$$

contradiction. This proves (L1) under (L7).

Finally $c_2(n)\le1+2\log n$ gives
$w\le27+2\log B+4\log d+8\log T\le2(20+\log B+2\log d+4\log T)$.
Substitution proves (L2).


The analytic tools used in this proof are real differentiation and Rolle’s theorem, the exponential power series with its absolute convergence, finite Taylor expansion and confluent limits, and the fundamental theorem of calculus. The elementary inequalities used above follow, for example, from $e=\sum_{n\ge0}1/n!<3$, $\log2=\int_1^2dx/x\ge1/2$, and $\log x\le x-1$. In particular $\log w\le w/2$ for $w\ge2$, by differentiation. No Schwarz lemma, maximum principle, Cauchy estimate, or imported zero estimate is required.

## 2. Thomas’s exact tail forms, coverage, and explicit cutoffs

**PROVED.** The third logarithm in the Thomas tail forms is redundant. In the precise normalization of P03 and P12, every fixed coefficient ratio is a monomial in the two displayed units. Consequently the missing arithmetic input for these tails is a *homogeneous two-logarithm* lower bound. The reduction below is uniform for every integer parameter `a>=1`; it also supplies the previously missing elementary coverage certificates for `1<=a<=15`.

Put

$$
f_a(T)=T^3-(a-1)T^2-(a+2)T-1,
\quad \theta>1,\quad L=\log\theta,\quad M=\log(\theta+1),
\quad D=L^2-LM+M^2.
$$

Here `theta` is the unique positive root. Indeed `f_a(0)=-1`, its derivative has exactly one positive root, and beyond that point `f_a` increases strictly to infinity. Direct substitution gives

$$
f_a(a)=-2a-1<0,\qquad f_a(a+1)=a^2+a-1>0.
$$

Thus `a<theta<a+1`. If `phi=(1+sqrt(5))/2`, then

$$
f_a(\phi)=(1-2a)\phi+1-a<0,
$$

so `theta>phi`, and in particular

$$
0<L<M<2L. \tag{T13.1}
$$

The rational-root test gives irreducibility, since the only possible rational roots are `+1,-1` and the values are `-2a-1,1`. Multiplication gives

$$
(T+1)^3f_a(-1/(T+1))=-f_a(T).
$$

Its three roots, all in the same cubic field, are

$$
\theta_0=\theta,\quad \theta_1=-1/(\theta+1),\quad
\theta_2=-(\theta+1)/\theta.
$$

The automorphism `sigma(theta)=theta_1` cycles these roots. Their absolute logarithm rows for `u=theta^A(theta+1)^B` are

$$
\ell_0=(L,M),\quad\ell_1=(-M,L-M),\quad\ell_2=(M-L,-L).
$$

The first two rows have determinant `D>0`. A relation `rL+sM=0` with integers `r,s` would give the algebraic equality `theta^r(theta+1)^s=1`. Applying the conjugates and taking absolute logarithms gives both independent row equations, so `r=s=0`. This proves the required multiplicative independence without an irrationality-measure theorem.

### 2.1. The coefficient ratios are units

Let `Q=theta^2+theta+1`. The root differences are

$$
\theta_0-\theta_1=Q/(\theta+1),\quad
\theta_0-\theta_2=Q/\theta,\quad
\theta_1-\theta_2=Q/[\theta(\theta+1)].
$$

Consequently

$$
f'_a(\theta_0)=\frac{Q^2}{\theta(\theta+1)},\quad
f'_a(\theta_1)=-\frac{Q^2}{\theta(\theta+1)^2},\quad
f'_a(\theta_2)=\frac{Q^2}{\theta^2(\theta+1)}. \tag{T13.2}
$$

In particular the three absolute derivative ratios are respectively

$$
|f'_a(\theta_0)/f'_a(\theta_1)|=\theta+1,\quad
|f'_a(\theta_0)/f'_a(\theta_2)|=\theta,\quad
|f'_a(\theta_1)/f'_a(\theta_2)|=\theta/(\theta+1).
$$

The tail forms of P12 therefore become

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Lambda_{01}&=(A-B)L+(A+2B-1)M,\\
\Lambda_{02}&=(2A+B-1)L+(-A+B)M,\\
\Lambda_{12}&=(A+2B-1)L+(-2A-B+1)M. \tag{T13.3}
\end{aligned}
$$

All three asymptotes pass through `(A,B)=(1/3,1/3)`. In each of the three cases, vanishing of both integer coefficients in (T13.3) forces exactly that pair. Thus **no form in (T13.3) vanishes at an integer pair**. The exact-equality exception in (D2) is completely removed here.

### 2.2. A uniform tail bound valid on the entire real zero curve

Fix a parity class. The raw coefficient marker is

$$
\mathcal M(A,B)=\sum_{i=0}^2 \frac{\varepsilon_i}{f'_a(\theta_i)}
                 e^{\ell_i\cdot(A,B)},\qquad
(\varepsilon_0,\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2)
 =(1,(-1)^A,(-1)^{A+B}),
$$

where the parity signs remain fixed on its real extension. Set

$$
z=(A-1/3,B-1/3),\qquad v_i=\ell_i\cdot z.
$$

The three positive coefficient magnitudes after this translation are equal:

$$
\frac{e^{\ell_i\cdot(1/3,1/3)}}{|f'_a(\theta_i)|}
=\frac{[\theta(\theta+1)]^{4/3}}{Q^2}.
$$

Also `v_0+v_1+v_2=0`. Thus the marker equation is exactly a signed equation among `e^{v_0},e^{v_1},e^{v_2}` with coefficients `+1,-1`. In parity `(1,1)` they all have the same sign, so there are no zeros. In the other three parity classes the term whose sign differs from the other two is the largest, and equals their sum.

Write the ordered logarithms as `h>=m>=n`. Necessarily the largest is unique, `h+m+n=0`, and

$$
\lambda=h-m=\log(1+e^{n-m}),\qquad
0<\lambda\le\log2.
$$

The chosen pair `(largest,middle)` is one of the two allowed balancing pairs of that parity; `lambda=|Lambda_ij|`. With `g=m-n>=0`, the zero-sum identity gives `g=3h-2lambda`, and hence

$$
0<|\Lambda_{ij}|=\log(1+e^{-g})\le e^{-g}
=e^{-3h+2\lambda}\le4e^{-3h}. \tag{T13.4}
$$

This is exact and needs no sufficiently-large-tail qualification. The two choices of middle term split the entire curve into two closed sectors, meeting at the unique point where the two smaller terms agree. These are the two tails with their finite initial portions attached.

Let `H_0=max(|A-1/3|,|B-1/3|)`. Inverting the first two logarithm rows gives

$$
z_1=((L-M)v_0-Mv_1)/D,\qquad
z_2=(Mv_0+Lv_1)/D.
$$

### 2.5. Explicit height compiler from a supplied two-logarithm theorem

The following implication is **PROVED**, independently of whether the lower-bound hypothesis has yet been supplied. Suppose an explicit `K(a)>=1` and integer `k>=1` satisfy

$$

|rL+sM|\ge\exp\{-K(a)[1+\log B]^k\},

\max\left\{1,
 \left[\frac{8K(a)(2k)^k}{\kappa}\right]^2,
 \frac{2\log C_a}{\kappa}\right\}.\ } \tag{T13.10}

$$

$$

$$

\log C_a<\kappa H/2.

The lower bound (T13.LB) is then strictly greater than the upper bound (T13.5), a contradiction. The case `H<1` is already included.

### 3.1. Finite rational separation lemma

**PROVED.** Let `alpha` be real, `B>=1` an integer, and `p,q` coprime integers with

$$
q>B,\qquad |\alpha-p/q|\le\frac1{2qB}. \tag{FR1}
$$

Then, for every integer pair `(r,s) != (0,0)` with `|r|,|s|<=B`,

$$
|r\alpha+s|\ge\frac1{2q}. \tag{FR2}
$$

Indeed, if `rp+sq=0`, coprimality implies `q|r`. Since `|r|<q`, this gives `r=0`, then `s=0`, contradiction. Thus the integer `rp+sq` has absolute value at least one, and

$$
|r\alpha+s|\ge\frac{|rp+sq|}{q}-|r|\,|\alpha-p/q|
\ge\frac1q-\frac1{2q}=\frac1{2q}.
$$

No optimality or convergence property of `p/q` is required. This elementary inequality is why the huge initial height is computationally harmless once an effective bound exists.

### 3.2. Applying the separator to one certified Thomas exponent box

$$

\boxed{\quad H\le\frac13+\frac{\log(8q/M)}{\kappa_a}.\quad} \tag{FR3}

### 3.3. Exact logarithm intervals: every rounding rule and remainder

The certificate uses the fixed integer scale `S=2^1200`. An integer pair `(l,u)` represents the closed real interval `[l/S,u/S]`. All powers, divisions and comparisons below are integer operations or exact rational operations; no floating-point numbers enter the proof.

First bisect the positive root of `f_a` until its interval has endpoints `t_-/S,t_+/S` with `t_+-t_-=1`. The sign test is the integer

$$
t^3-(a-1)t^2S-(a+2)tS^2-S^3.
$$

Its signs are respectively negative and positive at the two endpoints. The root-existence and uniqueness proof earlier identifies this interval with the required `theta`.

For a real `x` in a rational interval contained in `[1,2]`, put `q=(x-1)/(x+1)`. Then `0<=q<=1/3`, and the fundamental theorem of calculus and the finite geometric identity give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log x
&=\int_0^q\frac{2\,dt}{1-t^2}\\
&=2\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{q^{2j+1}}{2j+1}+R_N,\\
0\le R_N
&\le\frac{2q^{2N+1}}{(2N+1)(1-q^2)}
\le\frac9{4(2N+1)3^{2N+1}}. \tag{FR5}
\end{aligned}
$$

This proof uses a finite geometric sum and its positive integral remainder; it needs no complex analytic theorem or interchange of infinite sums.

From the exact rational input endpoints, compute

$$
q_- =\left\lfloor S\frac{x_- -1}{x_-+1}\right\rfloor,
\qquad
q_+ =\left\lceil S\frac{x_+ -1}{x_++1}\right\rceil.
$$

The true `q` lies in `[q_-/S,q_+/S]`. Squaring and every subsequent multiplication are rounded down at the lower endpoint and up at the upper endpoint. Explicitly, the interval product for nonnegative endpoint integers `(l,u)` and `(l',u')` is

$$
\left(\left\lfloor ll'/S\right\rfloor,
      \left\lceil uu'/S\right\rceil\right).
$$

Start the odd-power interval at `(q_-,q_+)`, multiply repeatedly by the interval for `q^2`, and round the lower and upper summands `2 q^{2j+1}/(2j+1)` outward. Summing these integer endpoints preserves enclosure. Add to the upper endpoint the integer

$$
\left\lceil\frac{9S}{4(2N+1)3^{2N+1}}\right\rceil.
$$

The code takes `N=floor((1200+5)/3)+5=406`. Formula (FR5) proves the bound for this exact finite value; no asymptotic statement about the truncation is used. Every outward rounding preserves its inequality by induction. The same computation at `x=2` produces `log 2` bounds.

For an input greater than two, choose the exact integer `k>=0` for which `1<=x/2^k<=2`, apply the preceding formula to `x/2^k`, and add `k` times the certified interval for `log2`. The certificate verifies that both endpoints lie in the chosen range. It applies this procedure to the root intervals for `theta` and `theta+1`, obtaining integers

$$
0<L_-\le SL\le L_+,\qquad
0<M_-\le SM\le M_+.
$$

Thus

$$
\frac{L_-}{M_+}\le\frac LM\le\frac{L_+}{M_-}. \tag{FR6}
$$

For a candidate rational `p/q`, its maximum distance from the two rational endpoints in (FR6) bounds its distance from `L/M`. That maximum is compared exactly with `1/(2qB_0)`, proving (FR1).

A convenient exact lower bound for the tail exponent is

$$
\kappa_-=
\frac{3(L_-^2-L_+M_++M_-^2)}{S(L_++M_+)}>0. \tag{FR7}
$$

The numerator is a lower bound for `S^2(L^2-LM+M^2)`, and the denominator uses an upper bound for `S(L+M)`. The program checks positivity. It computes a logarithm upper interval for the exact rational `8qS/M_-`, using (FR5) after power-of-two scaling. Substitution in (FR4) now involves only exact integers and fractions.

The search computes continued fractions of the rational midpoint of (FR6). For each candidate it checks coprimality, `q>B_0`, and the exact endpoint-error inequality. ## 5. Q2: uniform quotient arithmetic and one auxiliary for an entire end

### 5.1. Status and recommendation

**PROVED:** the engine's quotient moment generating function has algebraic degree **exactly two**, for every even `u>=4`. Its radicand degree grows with `u`; its algebraic degree does not. Thus growing algebraic degree is not an explanation of the difficult Hankels. Neither a large radicand nor irregular initial Jacobi coefficients proves the absence of a closed form. The phrase “closed form” would itself need a specified class before such an impossibility theorem could be stated.

The supplied `sol_b/report.md` proves the signed Jacobi identity and checks `b_1<0` for `u=4,6,...,20`; it does not contain an all-u proof of that sign. A negative `b_1=H_2/H_1^2` excludes a positive moment functional in that normalization: its quadratic Gram determinant is negative. A sign or geometric rescaling cannot fix this, because the determinant receives a positive square factor. It does not exclude signed path formulas or unrelated auxiliary constructions.

The useful replacement target is an **adaptive Padé--Runge norm certificate**, with a single auxiliary certified on a whole half-line. There is no need to prescribe a nonzero leading Hankel minor, and no need to run LLL independently on many overlapping cells. What remains unproved is the quantitative statement that such an auxiliary reaches the desired middle range with affordable degree and coefficient height. The construction below does not pretend to supply that quantitative gain.

### 5.2. Uniform integrality of the Faulhaber quotient (PROVED)

Put `d=u^2=4m`, `T=n(n+1)`. There is a monic polynomial

`f_u(T) in Q[T]`, `deg f_u=2m-2`,

with **squarefree coefficient denominator** such that

`d S_(d-1)(n)=T^2 f_u(T)`.

Consequently every integer solution with `n>=1` lifts to an **integer**

`w=u y / T`, with `w^2=f_u(T)` and `u | T w`.

Conversely these conditions, together with `T=n(n+1)`, recover the original integer solution. The condition `u|Tw` is the scale ledger; an exclusion theorem may work on the larger set without using it.

Here is a self-contained proof, including the denominator assertion that was only checked on the supplied finite table. Define the Bernoulli numbers by the formal identity

`B(z)=z/(exp(z)-1)=sum B_j z^j/j!`.

All statements below are identities of formal power series over `Q`; no analytic convergence theorem is needed. The identity `B(-z)=B(z)+z` shows that `B_1=-1/2` and every odd `B_j` with `j>1` vanishes. Define `B_s(x)=sum_j binom(s,j) B_j x^(s-j)`. Multiplication of the generating function by `exp(z)-1` gives

`B_s(x+1)-B_s(x)=s x^(s-1)`.

It follows by telescoping, or polynomial uniqueness under finite differences, that

`sum_(a=0)^(N-1) a^s = sum_(j=0)^s binom(s,j) B_j N^(s+1-j)/(s+1-j)`.  (U1)

Fix any prime `p`. We prove `v_p(B_s)>=-1` by induction on `s`. It is clear for `s=0`. Substitute `N=p` into (U1) and isolate its `j=s` term, which is `p B_s`. Each earlier term has `q=s+1-j>=2` and valuation at least

`q-v_p(q)-1 >=0`.

Here the binomial coefficient is integral, the induction gives valuation at least `-1` for `B_j`, and `v_p(q)<=q-1` is elementary. The left side of (U1) is an integer. Hence `p B_s` is integral at `p`, proving the induction. As this holds for every prime, each Bernoulli denominator is squarefree. Their finite least common multiple is squarefree as well.

Now `P(n)=B_d(n+1)-B_d(0)=d S_(d-1)(n)`. Its coefficients have denominator dividing the squarefree least common multiple just obtained. The formal identity `B_d(1-x)=B_d(x)` for even `d` follows from `B(-z)=exp(z) B(z)`, and gives `P(-n-1)=P(n)`. Every polynomial with this symmetry belongs to `Q[n(n+1)]`: its degree is even, and subtracting its leading coefficient times `T^(degree/2)` lowers the degree while preserving the symmetry. This algorithm uses only integer binomial coefficients; it does not introduce new denominator factors.

Furthermore `P(0)=P(-1)=0` and

`P'(0)=d B_(d-1)(1)=0`, `P'(-1)=d B_(d-1)(0)=0`,

because `d-1` is odd and greater than one. Thus `n^2(n+1)^2=T^2` divides `P(n)`. In the invariant polynomial representation this says `P(n)=T^2 f_u(T)`. Its leading coefficient is one, its degree is `2m-2`, and its coefficient denominator is squarefree.

Finally write `w=a/b` in lowest terms with `b>0`. If `D` clears the coefficients of `f_u`, then `D a^2=b^2 D f_u(T)`, so `b^2|D`. Since `D` is squarefree, `b=1`. This proves the claimed integral lift for every even `u>=4`, not merely the nine computed fibres.

### 5.3. The quadratic series is not rational (PROVED)

For every even `u>=4`, `f_u` is not a square in `Q(T)`. Indeed a rational square which is a polynomial is a polynomial square: in a reduced fraction, a nonconstant denominator could not divide the square of its coprime numerator. If `f_u` were a square, then `d S_(d-1)(n)=q(n)^2` for a polynomial `q` of degree `D=d/2` and, after changing sign, leading coefficient one. Taking the finite difference gives

`(q(n)-q(n-1))(q(n)+q(n-1))=d n^(d-1)`.

The two factors have degrees `D-1,D`, with leading coefficients `D,2`. Since their product has only the irreducible factor `n`, they must be `D n^(D-1)` and `2 n^D`, respectively. Hence

`q(n)=n^D+(D/2)n^(D-1)`.

But `q(-1)=0` from `S_(d-1)(-1)=0`, so `D=2`, contradicting `u>=4`. (The exceptional `u=2` is the familiar square sum of cubes.)

Let `r=m-1`, write `sqrt(f_u(T))=T^r sum beta_j T^-j`, and let `q` be its polynomial part. The engine series is

`z(T)=Delta (sqrt(f_u(T))-q(T))`.

It satisfies `(z+Delta q)^2=Delta^2 f_u`, and is not rational by the preceding argument. Thus its algebraic degree over `Q(T)` is exactly two. The ordinary moment generating function in `t=1/T` has the same degree.

There is even an explicit uniform linear recurrence for its coefficient source. Put `F(t)=t^(2r) f_u(1/t)=sum_(j=0)^(2r) f_j t^j`, `f_0=1`, and `beta(t)=sqrt(F(t))`. Differentiating the formal identity yields `2F beta'=F' beta`, and coefficient comparison gives

`2n beta_n = sum_(j=1)^min(n,2r) (3j-2n) f_j beta_(n-j)`.  (U2)

This is a genuine finite recurrence. It does not imply a product formula for the nonlinear Hankel determinants, but it rules out describing the coefficient sequence as devoid of all uniform structure.

### 5.4. A single adaptive auxiliary covers a whole end (PROVED)

The following theorem applies to any monic nonsquare `f in Q[T]` of even degree `2r>=2`. Suppose the arithmetic problem has integer `T,w` satisfying `w^2=f(T)`, with `w>=0`.

Fix any `h>=0`, and expand `sqrt(f)=T^r sum beta_j T^-j` at infinity. Solve the `h` homogeneous equations

`sum_(i=0)^h a_i beta_(r+i+j)=0`, `j=1,...,h`,  (U3)

in `h+1` unknowns. A nonzero rational solution always exists; Gaussian elimination with adaptive pivots suffices. Clear denominators and content to obtain `A(T) in Z[T]`, `A!=0`, `deg A=a<=h`, with positive leading coefficient. Let `B(T)` be the polynomial part of `A(T)sqrt(f(T))`, and put

`N(T)=B(T)^2-A(T)^2 f(T)`.

The cancellation equations say `A sqrt(f)-B=O(T^(-h-1))`. As `deg B=r+a`, multiplication by `A sqrt(f)+B` proves

`deg N <= r+a-h-1 < deg B`, `N!=0`.  (U4)

Nonvanishing follows from the nonsquareness of `f`. This works even when some or all of the conventionally selected leading Hankel pivots vanish. No claim of normal Padé degree is required.

Let `Q>=1` clear the denominators of `B`. A particularly convenient **finite whole-end certificate** at a real cutoff `T_0` is

`A(T)>0`, `B(T)>0`, `N(T)!=0`, `B(T)-Q N(T)>0`, `B(T)+Q N(T)>0`

for every `T>=T_0`. All five conditions concern rational polynomials and can be certified by, for example, expansion in nonnegative powers of `T-T_0` with nonnegative coefficients and a positive constant, or by exact real-root isolation and signs. Then no arithmetic point exists with `T>=T_0`: at such a point

`0<|A(T)w-B(T)|=|N(T)|/(A(T)w+B(T)) < 1/Q`,

whereas `Q(A(T)w-B(T))` is an integer.

For completeness there is a completely explicit coefficient-only cutoff, requiring no real-root algorithm. For a polynomial `P`, write `L(P)` for the sum of the absolute values of its coefficients below the leading term, and `||P||_1` for the sum of all absolute coefficients. Write `a_0>0,b_0>0,n_0!=0` for the leading coefficients of `A,B,N`, and `g=deg B-deg N>=h+1`. Any integer `T_0>=1` satisfying

`T_0 > 2L(A)/a_0`,

`T_0 > 2L(B)/b_0`,

`T_0 > L(N)/|n_0|`,

`T_0^g > 2Q ||N||_1/b_0`  (U5)

is a valid whole-end cutoff. For `T>=1`, each sum of lower terms is bounded by its coefficient sum times the next lower power of `T`. The first two inequalities give `A>0` and `B>(b_0/2)T^(deg B)`, the third excludes a zero of `N`, and the last gives `Q|N|<B`. These observations prove every required inequality. The smallest integer satisfying the last displayed condition can be found by integer bisection; no floating-point root is needed.

This is uniform in `u` and `h`, and **does give a finite, computable height bound in each power-sum fibre** using only rational linear algebra. It is not a newly small bound. The uniform middle-range target is now precise: construct solutions of (U3), or other norm auxiliaries, for which the full polynomial inequalities hold at the proposed lower middle-range endpoint, with proved bounds on degree and coefficient size as functions of `u`. A formal existence theorem for a kernel vector does not supply those inequalities at a useful endpoint.

One can also bound the output coefficients without LLL if desired. After clearing a common denominator in (U3), suppose its integer entries have absolute value at most `K` and its rank is `s`. Choose `s` independent rows, `s` independent columns, and one further column; their signed maximal minors give a nonzero kernel vector, supported on those `s+1` columns. Each coordinate has absolute value at most `s! K^s` by the permutation expansion. All original rows vanish on it because they lie in the span of the selected rows. This bound is explicit, though usually too large for an economical cover.

### 5.5. Factor covers: what is actually gained

An elementary factor-cover alternative is available whenever a particular `D f_u=c product F_i^(e_i)` has been computed over `Z[T]`, with distinct primitive irreducible factors. For each pair choose a nonzero integer Bezout constant `R_ij=U_ij F_i+V_ij F_j` after clearing denominators. Put into `S` every prime dividing `D c product R_ij`. Outside `S`, at most one factor value can be divisible by a given prime; at an integer-square solution its valuation must be even whenever `e_i` is odd. Therefore each such factor satisfies `F_i(T)=d_i z_i^2`, with `d_i` a signed squarefree product of primes in `S`. Zero factor values are a separate finite polynomial-root case. This is a finite exact cover, proved only by prime valuations and the Bezout identities.

Its utility depends on bounded factor degree and manageable resultants, neither of which is supplied merely by the identity `T^2 f_u(T)`. A uniform bounded-degree factor cover for this family remains TARGET. It would be worth pursuing if exact factorizations exhibit a stable low-degree factor, but the supplied Hankel data do not establish one.

**Catalogue effect.** This closes the all-u arithmetic-normalization gap and removes prescribed Hankel nonvanishing from the existence of a Runge-end certificate. It does not close the affordable uniform middle range. Next finite tasks: (i) optimize a small set of adaptive degrees using whole-half-line polynomial sign certificates, rather than many cellwise LLL searches; (ii) record the actual coefficient/denominator growth and achieved endpoint; (iii) factor a few exact `f_u` and compute the Bezout-resultant prime sets before investing in a factor-cover route. A uniform estimate on these concrete quantities is the remaining theorem to seek.

## 6. Programme consequences, certificate ledger, and next finite work

### 6.1. Which gaps are closed

**The cancellation-wall gap closes for tails whose fixed coefficients lie in a two-unit logarithm lattice.** More precisely, after an explicit fixed denominator is cleared, the tail form must be a nonzero integer combination of two fixed real unit logarithms, its two coefficients must be bounded linearly in the orbit height, and the real tail error must be exponentially small in that height. Section 1 supplies a fifth-power logarithmic lower bound under explicit degree and conjugate bounds. Comparing it with the exponential tail error gives an explicit height cutoff by the calculation in §2. The finite rational separator in §3 then turns that cutoff into a small, auditable box when suitable rational approximations are supplied. All equalities and possible exact-zero cases remain certificate obligations.

This advances `lifting.real_count` beyond rational ends for that arithmetic class, and adds a fully elementary logarithmic separator to `separation.archimedean`. The finite rational reduction is also a useful `coverage.compiler` leaf: input a proved coefficient box, output a smaller box with a coprimality witness and interval inequalities. A continued-fraction search can propose the rational; the proof depends only on the verifier's inequalities.

**Thomas's evaluation is complete.** P03's unit coverage for $a\ge16$, the new finite unit certificates for $1\le a\le15$, the all-index determinant theorem, and the exact reduction/exhaustion close every previously open fibre below 182. The already accepted positive-recurrence proof covers $a\ge182$. A p-adic isolation step is unnecessary for this particular completion because the finite exponent boxes are so small. No claim about arbitrary higher-rank orbit intersections follows from this special two-logarithm reduction.

**The S-003 fixed-third-logarithm gap remains.** Its four constants, nonvanishing, four-tail cover, and numerical $C,\kappa$ are now explicit. The exact norm $256/61$ prevents counting its immediate norm identity as a strict new separator. The missing marked-row construction is stated with concrete integer output and exact interval checks. The existence and quantitative size bound for those rows, and complete arithmetic orbit coverage where needed, have not been proved here.

**The uniform power-sum normalization gap is closed.** Every even $u\ge4$ has the integral quotient lift in §5. Prescribed leading Hankel nonvanishing is no longer an assumption for existence of an adaptive end auxiliary. A useful uniform middle-range bound requires a stronger quantitative auxiliary estimate; the large cutoffs actually computed in §5 make that remaining obligation visible.
