## 0. Result and scope

The useful combination is **Bernoulli critical coefficients → global prime-content allocation → quadratic norm orbits → a modulus selected by the first differing binary digit of the orbit index**. The last step is not a sieve with a fixed set of primes, not a Runge bound, and not a logarithmic lower bound. It gives an all-height exclusion by an explicit integer descent.

Here are the proved outputs, with their limitations stated at the point of use.

**PROVED, uniform global separation.** Define
\[
V_0(a)=2,\quad V_1(a)=a,\quad V_{j+1}(a)=aV_j(a)-V_{j-1}(a),\quad V_{-j}=V_j.
\]
For every odd integer \(a\ge3\), every positive odd integer \(r\), and every odd integer \(t\),
\[
V_t(a)=V_r(a)z^2\iff |t|=r,\ |z|=1,
\tag{T0}
\]
\[
3V_t(a)+V_r(a)=4V_r(a)z^2\iff |t|=r,\ |z|=1.
\tag{T1}
\]
Section 1 proves both assertions without a height bound. Section 2 gives the affine-marker and finite-graph version that the coverage compiler can use.

**PROVED, a complete parametric simultaneous-Pell evaluation.** For every positive odd integer \(b\), put
\[
D=b^2+4,\quad A=b(b^2+3)/4,\quad Z_0=(b^2+1)/2.
\]
The complete integer solutions of
\[
DZ^2-4X^2=1,\qquad (8A^2+1)Y^2-6X^2=2A^2+1
\tag{P}
\]
are \((|X|,|Y|,|Z|)=(A,1,Z_0)\). Coverage of the actual quadratic order is proved by a decreasing integer trace, not imported from a unit theorem. At \(b=1\), this closes the global endpoint-allocation branch
\[
\operatorname{sf}(n)=2,\quad \operatorname{sf}(n+1)=3,\quad
\operatorname{sf}(2n+1)=5:
\qquad n=2\text{ only}.
\tag{E0}
\]
Here \(\operatorname{sf}\) is the squarefree kernel, not the assertion that the integer itself is squarefree. On this entire branch, the only power-sum perfect power is \(S_3(2)=3^2\). In particular the branch is excluded for **every even \(k\)** and **every \(m\ge2\)**. Other allocation branches have not all been closed.

**PROVED, global non-Runge endpoint bounds from Bernoulli content.** For every even \(k\ge2\), solutions with any one of \(n,n+1,2n+1\) squarefree lie in explicit finite divisor lists determined by two Bernoulli-content integers. This is uniform in \(k\), includes all \(m\ge2\), and uses no condition that \(k+1\) be a perfect power. For odd \(k\ge3\), the analogous theorem covers \(m\ge3\) when \(n\) or \(n+1\) is squarefree. More generally there are explicit bounds on every stratum with bounded extracted power part. Sections 4–5 prove the assertions and the exact content-descent rule.

**PROVED by complete finite certificates:** for every even \(k=2,4,\ldots,32\), the squarefree-endpoint stratum just described has only \(n=1\), for all \(m\ge2\). For \(k=13\), the stratum with \(n\) or \(n+1\) squarefree has only \(n=1\), for all \(m\ge3\). These are exhaustive divisor calculations, not scans to a guessed height.

**Q2:** the proposed integral lift for \(S_{13}\) is exactly the old cover after an integer scaling. Its earlier exploratory calculation already had three surviving dimensions. Section 7 gives the exact identities, the extra denominator prime, and the remaining higher-cover obligation. ## 1. A moving-modulus theorem, with all algebra written out

### 1.1. The trace recurrence and the negative identity

Let \(M\) be any integral two-by-two matrix with determinant one and odd trace \(a\ge3\). Direct expansion of its four entries proves
\[
M^2-aM+I=0.
\tag{M1}
\]
Multiplying by integer powers, which exist because the adjugate is the integral inverse, proves that \(\operatorname{tr}(M^t)=V_t(a)\) for every integer \(t\). Also \(\operatorname{tr}(M^{-t})=\operatorname{tr}(M^t)\). Applying (M1) to \(M^h\) gives
\[
M^{2h}-V_hM^h+I=0,
\qquad V_{2h}=V_h^2-2.
\tag{M2}
\]
For existence one may always take \(M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&a\end{smallmatrix}\right)\).

For \(s\ge1\), define
\[
h_s=2^s,\qquad q_s=V_{h_s}(a).
\]
The recurrence shows that \(V_j\) is positive and strictly increasing for \(j\ge0\): the initial pair is \(2<a\), and \(aV_j-V_{j-1}>V_j\) when \(V_j>V_{j-1}>0\). In particular \(q_s>1\). More precisely,
\[
q_1=a^2-2\equiv3\pmod4,
\quad q_{s+1}=q_s^2-2\equiv3\pmod4.
\tag{M3}
\]
These are odd, positive moduli. They need not be prime. From (M2),
\[
\boxed{M^{2h_s}\equiv-I\pmod{q_s}.}
\tag{M4}
\]
There is also a certified square root of two modulo every such modulus:
\[
\boxed{V_{h_s/2}^2\equiv2\pmod{q_s}.}
\tag{M5}
\]
It is a unit, because \(q_s\) is odd.

### 1.2. The only nonsquare fact needed

**Lemma.** If a positive odd integer \(q\) is \(3\pmod4\), then \(-1\) is not a square modulo \(q\).

**Proof.** Factor \(q\) into ordinary primes by repeated division. Some prime divisor \(p\) is \(3\pmod4\), since otherwise their product, with multiplicities, would be \(1\pmod4\). If \(z^2\equiv-1\pmod p\), then \(z\) is a unit. Multiplication by \(z\) permutes the nonzero residues, so cancellation of their product gives \(z^{p-1}\equiv1\). But raising \(z^2\equiv-1\) to the odd power \((p-1)/2\) gives \(z^{p-1}\equiv-1\), a contradiction. ∎

This is not quadratic reciprocity or a Hilbert-symbol calculation.

### 1.3. A useful coprimality certificate

For every odd integer \(r\),
\[
\gcd(q_s,V_r)=1.
\tag{M6}
\]
If an odd prime \(p\) divided both, (M2) would give
\(M^{2h_s}\equiv-I\) and \(M^{2r}\equiv-I\pmod p\).
Raise the first identity to the odd power \(r\), and the second to the even power \(h_s\). Their left sides are equal, whereas their right sides are \(-I\) and \(I\). This is impossible. The prime two is absent from \(q_s\). Negative \(r\) is harmless because \(V_{-r}=V_r\).

### 1.4. Proof of the two uniform trace-square theorems

Suppose \(r,t\) are odd and \(t\ne r,-r\). Exactly one of \(t-r,t+r\) is divisible by four: their difference is \(2r\equiv2\pmod4\). Choose \(\rho\in\{r,-r\}\) so that \(t-\rho\) is divisible by four, and put
\[
s=v_2(t-\rho)-1\ge1,\qquad
 t-\rho=2h_s u,\quad u\text{ odd}.
\tag{M7}
\]
The integer \(u\) may be negative. Since \(-I\) is its own inverse, (M4) gives
\[
V_t\equiv-V_\rho=-V_r\pmod{q_s}.
\tag{M8}
\]
If \(V_t=V_rz^2\), cancellation by (M6) gives \(z^2\equiv-1\), contradicting §1.2. This proves (T0).

If \(3V_t+V_r=4V_rz^2\), the same congruence and cancellation give
\[
2z^2\equiv-1\pmod{q_s}.
\]
By (M5), the left side is the square of \(V_{h_s/2}z\). This is again impossible. At \(t=\pm r\), either original equation is equivalent to \(z^2=1\), proving both directions of (T1).

The contradiction has no dependence on the size of \(t\). The modulus is selected by an exact finite divisibility calculation on \(t-\rho\), not chosen from a fixed finite list.

## 2. The ghost system, affine markers, and its finite graph compiler

### 2.1. The actual descent

Suppose first that a candidate index \(t\) lies in an anchor class \(t\equiv r\pmod4\). Retain the candidate point and its square witness. Introduce a ghost state
\[
(w,h,q),\qquad t-r=2hw,\qquad h=2^s,\quad q=V_h,
\]
initialized with \(w=(t-r)/4\), \(h=2\), \(q=a^2-2\).
If \(w=0\), the index is the known anchor. If \(w\ne0\) is even, make the move
\[
(w,h,q)\longmapsto(w/2,2h,q^2-2).
\tag{G1}
\]
The exact exponent relation and (M2) are its transport identities. The positive integer \(|w|\) strictly decreases on every nonterminal move. A nonzero starting value therefore reaches an odd \(w\). At that terminal state, (M4) gives the negative return and the transported square property contradicts §1.2.

The modulus grows; it is not the decreasing rank. The rank is the unremoved factor of two in the index difference, implemented by division of the nonzero integer \(w\). This is a negative ghost proof with a complete termination statement. It makes no claim to decrease the original endpoint \(n\), or to carry a square prefix sum to a smaller square prefix sum.

### 2.2. General affine square markers

**Theorem (affine moving-modulus isolation).** Let \(M\in SL_2(\mathbb Z)\) have odd trace \(a\ge3\), let \(v\in\mathbb Z^2\), and let \(\ell\) be an integer row. Put
\[
f(t)=\ell M^tv+B,
\quad B\in\mathbb Z,
\quad d\in\mathbb Z_{>0}.
\]
Suppose a known anchor satisfies \(f(r)=dc^2\), and there are coprime integers \(A,C\), with \(A\ne0,C>0\), and \(e\in\{0,1\}\), such that
\[
\frac{2B}{d}-c^2=-2^e(A/C)^2.
\tag{G2}
\]
Suppose \(s_0\ge1\) is certified to satisfy
\[
\gcd(q_s,dAC)=1\qquad(s\ge s_0).
\tag{G3}
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{t\equiv r\pmod{2^{s_0+1}},\quad f(t)=dz^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad t=r.}
\tag{G4}
\]

**Proof.** If \(t\ne r\), write \(t-r=2h_su\) with \(u\) odd as above; now \(s\ge s_0\). The signed return gives
\[
f(t)\equiv2B-f(r)=2B-dc^2\pmod{q_s}.
\]
Divide by \(d\), multiply by \(C^2\), and use (G2):
\((Cz)^2\equiv-2^eA^2\pmod{q_s}\).
If \(e=0\), divide by \(A^2\). If \(e=1\), divide by \((A V_{h_s/2})^2\), using (M5). In either case \(-1\) would be a square. ∎

The statement also holds with \(\ell M^tv\) replaced by a finite integer expression \(L(M^tv)\) satisfying the exact identity \(L(-x)=-L(x)\). A quadratic marker is not odd, so that extension cannot be applied to it without a further algebraic change of presentation.

A particularly useful case is \(B=0,c\ne0\): take \(e=0,A=c,C=1\). Thus every known nonzero square value of a linear marker has an **all-height isolated dyadic index class**, provided the odd-trace orbit is actually covered. The assertion is about integer squares, not about zeros of a local analytic function or about local squares at one prime.

### 2.3. The exceptional-prime condition has a finite graph proof

For fixed \(a\) and \(K=|dAC|\ge1\), compute the orbit of
\[
q\longmapsto q^2-2\pmod K,
\qquad q_1=a^2-2\pmod K,
\tag{G5}
\]
until a state repeats. There are at most \(K\) states. The eventual cycle consists entirely of units modulo \(K\).

Indeed all actual \(q_s\) are odd. For an odd prime \(p\mid K\), a state zero modulo \(p\) has future residues
\[
0\longmapsto-2\longmapsto2\longmapsto2\pmod p.
\]
It cannot belong to a cycle containing zero, since \(p\nmid2\). Therefore no state in the cycle modulo \(K\) is divisible by any prime of \(K\). Choose \(s_0\) after the last nonunit in the finite preperiod; this proves (G3) for **all** later indices by induction around the finite cycle. No factorization of the growing moduli \(q_s\) is required.

For a small certificate, one may instead list a finite forward-invariant subset of the unit residues modulo \(K\), together with the path entering it. A solver is not required to enumerate all \(K\) residues when a smaller invariant set is evident.

There is also an explicit prime-wise bound. If an odd prime \(p\) divides \(q_s\), then the order of \(M\pmod p\) is exactly \(2^{s+2}\): (M4) gives that power equal to \(I\), but the half-power is \(-I\ne I\). There are \(p(p^2-1)\) determinant-one two-by-two matrices over \(\mathbb F_p\). To count them, choose any nonzero first column (\(p^2-1\) choices); the determinant-one condition is one nonzero linear equation on the second column (\(p\) choices). Cosets of the cyclic subgroup generated by \(M\) partition this finite group, so its order divides that number. Thus
\[
s+2\le v_2(p^2-1).
\]
Consequently one may take
\[
s_0=\max\left(1,\max_{\substack{p\mid K\\p\text{ odd prime}}}
\{v_2(p^2-1)-1\}\right).
\tag{G6}
\]
The empty maximum contributes nothing. This proof uses only finite group counting and powers of two.

### 2.4. A complete finite-certificate format

The compiler input for a quadratic branch is:

1. An exhaustive finite seed cover by integer matrices of determinant one, with all inverse integrality and sign conditions. The chosen positive unit must have odd trace; this is a hypothesis, not a universal fact about Pell equations.
2. An exact square-marker identity \(f(t)=dz^2\), or a necessary such identity, and any other retained ledgers.
3. A finite collection of index classes covering \(\mathbb Z\). Each class is either excluded by a finite modular calculation, or contained in an anchor class from (G4), with its exact anchor, (G2), and a graph certificate for (G3).
4. Exact recovery and direct checking of the remaining anchors in the original equation.

**Soundness is proved:** lift an arbitrary original solution by item 1; its integer index enters a class in item 3. A modularly excluded class is impossible. In an anchor class, (G1)–(G4) force the stated integer anchor. Item 4 recovers every surviving original solution. Coverage of the classes is a finite residue computation modulo the least common multiple of their moduli.

There is **no theorem here that this finite cover will always be found**. A surviving class without an anchor certificate remains an explicit unresolved leaf. Passing finitely many modulus tests there is not a substitute for one.

For a quadratic order, a supplied positive norm-one unit gives finite seed coverage without a unit theorem. Write the order as \(\mathbb Z[\omega]\), with real conjugates \(\omega,\omega'\), and let \(\epsilon>1\) be an explicit unit of norm one with integral inverse. Given \(N(\gamma)=m\ne0\), first change its sign to make its chosen embedding positive. Integer powers of \(\epsilon\) move it into \(1\le\gamma<\epsilon\). Existence of such a power follows from \(\epsilon^j\ge1+j(\epsilon-1)\), followed by a least-integer choice. Then \(|\gamma'|\le|m|\). If \(\gamma=x+y\omega\),
\[
|y|\le\frac{\epsilon+|m|}{|\omega-\omega'|},\qquad
|x|\le\frac{|\omega||m|+|\omega'|\epsilon}{|\omega-\omega'|}.
\tag{G7}
\]
Rational upper bounds on these quantities give an explicit finite integer box. Every retained norm-\(m\) element is a seed. Multiplication by \(\epsilon\) has an integral determinant-one matrix, and its trace equals \(\epsilon+\epsilon^{-1}\). Extra lattice restrictions are carried as ledgers. An even trace is not repaired by the theorem; another unit or another quadratic presentation must actually be found.

A useful preliminary screen is that a quadratic field admitting an odd-trace norm-one integral unit must have squarefree radicand \(5\pmod8\). Its discriminant equation is \(a^2-4=Dc^2\); if \(a\) is odd, both \(D,c\) are odd and reduction modulo eight gives \(D\equiv5\). This is only a necessary test. No converse or guarantee of an affordable unit is asserted.

### 2.5. Calibration on an entire infinite sequence

Let \(F_0=0,F_1=1,F_{j+1}=F_j+F_{j-1}\), extended in both directions. With
\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad v=\binom11,
\]
the second coordinate of \(M^tv\) is \(F_{2t+1}\). This follows from two applications of the Fibonacci recurrence, including the inverse matrix.

The trace is three. Modulo three, \(M^4=I\), and the four marker values for \(t=0,1,2,3\) are \(1,2,2,1\). Classes \(1,2\) cannot be squares. For class \(0\pmod4\) use anchor \(r=0\), and for class \(3\pmod4\) use anchor \(r=-1\); both marker values are one. Here \(B=0,d=A=C=1\), so (G4) applies with \(s_0=1\). Therefore
\[
\boxed{n\text{ odd},\ F_n\text{ an integer square}\iff n=-1\text{ or }1.}
\tag{G8}
\]
This classical calibration is proved here to demonstrate a finite modular cover plus an unbounded family of certified moduli. It is not a new claim about Fibonacci numbers, nor a proof about the even-index branch.

## 3. A parametric Pell theorem and a completed Schaffer allocation branch

### 3.1. Coverage by an integer trace descent

Let \(b\ge1\) be odd, \(D=b^2+4\), and
\[
\phi=\frac{b+\sqrt D}{2},\qquad \phi^2=b\phi+1,
\qquad \epsilon=\phi^2.
\]
The radicand is nonsquare: for \(b=1\) it is five; for \(b\ge3\) it lies strictly between \(b^2\) and \((b+1)^2\). Thus the two-dimensional integer span \(\mathcal O=\mathbb Z[\phi]\) is an order in a real quadratic field. Its displayed monic relation proves closure. Conjugation sends \(\phi\) to \(-\phi^{-1}\); indeed \(\phi^{-1}=\phi-b\) is integral in the order. Consequently \(N(\phi)=-1\), \(N(\epsilon)=1\), and
\[
\operatorname{Tr}(\epsilon)=b^2+2=:a,
\]
an odd integer at least three.

**Claim.** Every positive norm-one unit in this order is an integer power of \(\epsilon\).

Let \(u>1\) be such a unit. Its conjugate is \(u^{-1}>0\), and it has the form
\[
u=(P+Q\sqrt D)/2,\quad P,Q\in\mathbb Z,
\quad P=u+u^{-1},\quad Q=(u-u^{-1})/\sqrt D.
\]
Thus \(Q\ge1\) and \(P^2-DQ^2=4\), with \(P>0\). In particular \(P>b\), so \(u>\phi\). There is no norm-one unit in \((1,\epsilon)\): such a unit would be greater than \(\phi\), but its quotient \(\epsilon/u\) would be another norm-one unit in \((1,\phi)\), a contradiction.

For \(u>\epsilon\), divide by \(\epsilon\). The quotient is still greater than one, and its positive integer trace is smaller. In fact, for \(1\le v<u\),
\[
(u+u^{-1})-(v+v^{-1})=(u-v)(1-1/(uv))>0.
\]
Repeated division terminates because that trace is a positive integer. The terminal unit is one. Units between zero and one are handled by inversion. This proves the claim by an actual descent, with no assumption that a fundamental unit has been computed. Negative chosen embeddings are handled by the sign \(-1\).

### 3.2. Complete proof of the simultaneous-Pell classification

Because all three variables are squared in (P), take \(X,Y,Z\ge0\); signs will be restored. The first equation forces \(Z>0\) and \(X>0\), since \(DZ^2=1\) is impossible. Put
\[
\eta=2X+Z\sqrt D\in\mathcal O.
\]
It is a positive unit of norm minus one. Therefore \(\eta/\phi\) is a positive norm-one unit and §3.1 gives
\[
\eta=\phi\epsilon^j,\qquad \eta^2=\epsilon^{2j+1}.
\]
Let \(t=2j+1\), an odd integer. Directly,
\[
V_t(a)=\operatorname{Tr}(\eta^2)
=2(4X^2+DZ^2)=16X^2+2.
\tag{P1}
\]
Define \(A=b(b^2+3)/4\) and \(Z_0=(b^2+1)/2\). These are integers, and finite expansion gives
\[
V_3(a)=a^3-3a=16A^2+2,
\qquad DZ_0^2-4A^2=1.
\tag{P2}
\]
The second equation in (P), after multiplication and substitution using (P1)–(P2), is exactly
\[
3V_t(a)+V_3(a)=4V_3(a)Y^2.
\]
Theorem (T1) with \(r=3\) forces \(|t|=3\), \(Y=1\). Equations (P1)–(P2) then give \(X=A\), \(Z=Z_0\). Substitution checks these values, and every independent sign choice is valid. This proves the complete uniform theorem for all odd positive \(b\).

There is a companion consequence of (T0): replacing the second equation of (P) by
\[
(8A^2+1)Y^2-8X^2=1
\]
gives the same solution list, because it becomes \(V_t=V_3Y^2\). It is a separate example of the same certificate, not a necessary dependency below.

### 3.3. The Schaffer endpoint branch \((2,3,5)\)

At \(b=1\), the first system is
\[
5Z^2-4X^2=1,\qquad 3Y^2-2X^2=1,
\]
and its only absolute solution is \((1,1,1)\). Therefore
\[
n=2X^2,\quad n+1=3Y^2,\quad 2n+1=5Z^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n=2.
\tag{P3}
\]
This is an all-height theorem for the whole fixed allocation label, not a bound for a finite box.

For completeness, \(S_k(2)=1+2^k\) is a perfect \(m\)-th power only when \(k=3,m=2\), with positive base three. If \(m\) is odd, its odd base \(y>1\) gives the odd factor
\(1+y+\cdots+y^{m-1}>1\) in \(y^m-1=2^k\), impossible. If \(m\) is even, put \(z=|y|^{m/2}\). Then \((z-1)(z+1)=2^k\). The two positive consecutive integers \((z-1)/2,(z+1)/2\) are powers of two, so they are one and two. Thus \(z=3,k=3\), and \(|y|^{m/2}=3\) forces \(m=2\).

The branch is now a reusable terminal theorem for Astra's even-\(k\) endpoint covers. Its hypotheses must actually be reached by the allocation; it cannot be substituted for all other squarefree labels.

A very small version of its moving-modulus certificate is also available directly. Its trace marker is
\[
V_t(3)+6=24Y^2,\qquad t\text{ odd}.
\]
The anchors \(t=3,-3\) cover the two odd classes modulo four. Here
\(2B/d-1=-1/2=-2(1/2)^2\), and the entire graph of (G5) modulo \(48\) is
\[
7\longmapsto47\longmapsto47.
\]
Both residues are units. This supplies (G3) with \(s_0=1\), and the two anchor certificates close all odd indices. This finite graph is a certificate for infinitely many moduli, not just two tests on the candidate endpoint.

## 4. Bernoulli critical coefficients as a global content ledger

The preceding separator is useful only after an arithmetic cover has supplied the right orbits. Bernoulli arithmetic gives such covers by identifying the primes where endpoint multiplicities can change.

### 4.1. Finite construction and the required denominator facts

Define
\[
B_0=1,\qquad B_j=-\frac1{j+1}\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\binom{j+1}{i}B_i,
\qquad B_j(X)=\sum_{i=0}^j\binom jiB_iX^{j-i}.
\]
Finite coefficient comparison gives
\[
B_j(X+1)-B_j(X)=jX^{j-1},\qquad
S_k(X)=\frac{B_{k+1}(X+1)-B_{k+1}(1)}{k+1}.
\tag{B1}
\]
In the first identity the relevant coefficient is a binomial multiple of the defining recurrence. Telescoping proves the second at all nonnegative integers, with zero value at zero.

Here is a finite way to establish the reflection facts when needed. Fix a degree cutoff \(L\). Form the finite polynomial \(E(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{L+1}t^j/j!\) and retain products through degree \(L+1\). After dividing a polynomial with zero constant term by \(t\), work in \(\mathbb Q[t]/(t^{L+1})\). The finite coefficient inverse of \((E(t)-1)/t\) is \(\sum_{j=0}^LB_jt^j/j!\). Binomial multiplication gives \(E(t)E(-t)=1\) to the requested degree. Hence substitution of \(-t\) in that inverse gives \(B(-t)=B(t)+t\). Comparing coefficients proves \(B_j=0\) for odd \(j>1\). Multiplication by the finite \(E(Xt)\) similarly gives
\[
B_j(1-X)=(-1)^j B_j(X),\quad
B_j(1)=B_j\ (j\ne1),\quad B_1(1)=1/2.
\tag{B2}
\]
The same finite algebra, using
\(tE(t/2)/(E(t)-1)=t/(E(t/2)-1)-t/(E(t)-1)\), gives
\[
B_j(1/2)=(2^{1-j}-1)B_j.
\tag{B3}
\]
Every identity concerns a fixed finite set of coefficients; no infinite generating series is evaluated.

Induction in the defining recurrence gives
\[
|B_j|\le j!,\qquad \operatorname{den}(B_j)\mid(j+1)!.
\tag{B4}
\]
For the first bound, the divided recurrence is at most
\(j!\sum_{v=2}^{j+1}1/v!<j!\); the finite sum is less than one because \(v!\ge2^{v-1}\), with strict inequality when \(v\ge3\). The initial case is direct. For the denominator, all earlier denominators divide \(j!\), so multiplying by \((j+1)!\) proves integrality.

We also need the stronger prime-wise statement
\[
v_p(B_j)\ge-1\quad\text{for every prime }p.
\tag{B5}
\]
Induct on \(j\). The sum over \(0\le a<p\), obtained from (B1), is
\[
\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}a^j
=\sum_{i=0}^j\binom ji B_i\frac{p^{j+1-i}}{j+1-i}.
\]
The last term is \(pB_j\). For an earlier term put \(q=j+1-i\ge2\). Its valuation is at least \(q-1-v_p(q)\ge0\), by the induction hypothesis. The left side is integral, so \(pB_j\) is integral at \(p\). This proves (B5) and the squarefreeness of every Bernoulli denominator.

For even \(j\ge2\), the same identity at \(p=2\) shows
\[
2B_j\equiv1\pmod2.
\tag{B6}
\]
Indeed the left side is one. The term with \(q=2\) vanishes for \(j\ge4\), by (B2); for \(j=2\) it is \(-2\). Terms with \(q\ge3\) have valuation at least \(q-1-v_2(q)\ge1\). Thus even Bernoulli numbers are nonzero. This avoids any use of a zeta value or an analytic nonvanishing theorem.

Rational congruences here and below mean ordinary divisibility after removing denominators prime to \(p\).

### 4.2. The critical-coefficient valuation lemma

**Theorem.** Let \(p\ge5\), and let \(a=v_p(u)\ge1\).

- If \(k\ge2\) is even and \(v_p(B_k)=0\), then
  \[
  v_p(S_k(u))=v_p(S_k(u-1))=a.
  \tag{B7}
  \]
- If \(k\ge2\) is even and \(v_p(B_k(1/2)/2)=0\), then, for odd positive \(u\),
  \[
  v_p(S_k((u-1)/2))=a.
  \tag{B8}
  \]
- If \(k\ge3\) is odd and \(v_p(kB_{k-1}/2)=0\), then
  \[
  v_p(S_k(u))=v_p(S_k(u-1))=2a.
  \tag{B9}
  \]

**Proof.** Finite expansion of (B1) at zero gives the coefficient of \(X^j\), \(j\ge1\), as
\[
\frac1j\binom{k}{j-1}B_{k+1-j}(1).
\tag{B10}
\]
By (B5), its valuation is at least \(-1-v_p(j)\). For even \(k\), the linear coefficient is \(B_k\). The quadratic coefficient vanishes for \(k\ge4\); for \(k=2\) it is \(1/2\). For \(j\ge3,p\ge5\), \(v_p(j)\le j-3\), so each higher term at \(u\) has valuation at least
\[
ja-1-v_p(j)\ge a+1.
\]
The linear term has valuation exactly \(a\), proving the first assertion for \(S_k(u)\).

For odd \(k\), the linear coefficient vanishes, and the quadratic coefficient is \(kB_{k-1}/2\). The cubic coefficient vanishes for \(k\ge5\); for \(k=3\) it is \(1/2\). For \(j\ge4,p\ge5\), \(v_p(j)\le j-4\), so all remaining terms have valuation at least \(2a+1\). The quadratic term has exactly \(2a\), proving the odd assertion. The elementary bounds on \(v_p(j)\) follow from \(5^v\le j\): for \(v\ge1\), \(5^v\ge v+4\); the small indices with \(v=0\) are immediate.

Reflection in (B2) gives \(S_k(-1-X)=(-1)^{k+1}S_k(X)\). Evaluating at \(X=-u\) gives the assertions for \(S_k(u-1)\), with unchanged valuations.

Finally expand \(S_k((u-1)/2)\) in \(u\). Its coefficient of \(u^j\) is
\[
\frac1{j2^j}\binom{k}{j-1}B_{k+1-j}(1/2).
\]
For even \(k\) this polynomial is odd. Its linear coefficient is \(B_k(1/2)/2\); the quadratic term is absent; (B3)–(B5) give the same higher-term valuation bound. This proves (B8). ∎

The lemma deliberately excludes primes at which the critical coefficient is divisible by \(p\), as well as its denominator primes. For example the exact checker gives
\[
v_{691}(S_{12}(691))=2,\qquad v_{691}(S_{13}(691))=3.
\]
It would be wrong to retain only von Staudt denominator primes and discard Bernoulli numerator primes.

### 4.3. The uniform exceptional supports

For even \(k\), write \(B_k=U_k/V_k\) in lowest terms, with \(V_k>0\), and define
\[
R_0(k)=\operatorname{rad}(6|U_k|V_k),
\qquad R_h(k)=\operatorname{rad}(6|U_k|V_k(2^{k-1}-1)).
\tag{B11}
\]
For odd \(k\ge3\), write \(kB_{k-1}/2=A_k/C_k\) in lowest terms and define
\[
R_o(k)=\operatorname{rad}(6|A_k|C_k).
\tag{B12}
\]
Here \(\operatorname{rad}\) is the product of the distinct ordinary prime divisors. These are nonzero explicitly computable integers. The factors two and three are included unconditionally; the lemma does not need their special valuation cases.

If \(S_k(n)=y^m\), \(n\ge1,m\ge2\), then:

- for even \(k\), every prime outside \(R_0\) occurs in each of \(n,n+1\) to an exponent divisible by \(m\), and every prime outside \(R_h\) occurs in \(2n+1\) to an exponent divisible by \(m\);
- for odd \(k\ge3\), every prime outside \(R_o\) occurs in each of \(n,n+1\) to an exponent divisible by
  \[
  h=m/\gcd(m,2).
  \tag{B13}
  \]

This follows immediately by requiring the exact valuations in (B7)–(B9) to be multiples of \(m\). It is a statement about every prime factor of an arbitrary integer solution, not about a bounded list of moduli.

### 4.4. Controlled content descent and the global bounds it gives

**General content theorem.** Suppose an endpoint \(u>0\) of a putative solution has the property that, outside the primes of a squarefree integer \(R\), its prime exponents are divisible by \(h\ge1\). Then
\[
u=dv^h,
\quad d\mid R^{h-1},
\quad d\text{ is }h\text{-th-power-free}.
\tag{B14}
\]
For \(h=1\), this says \(d=1,v=u\), which contains no useful restriction. For \(h\ge2\), it is a finite set of possible defects \(d\).

Here is the ghost proof with a rank and reconstruction. Initialize \(d=u,v=1\) and retain all other original coordinates and equations. Whenever \(p^h\mid d\), make the move
\[
(d,v)\longmapsto(d/p^h,pv).
\tag{B15}
\]
The original endpoint \(dv^h\), and hence its power-sum witness, stay fixed. The integer \(\Omega(d)\), counting prime factors with multiplicity, decreases by exactly \(h\). The moves commute prime by prime; their terminal defect has at prime \(p\) the remainder of \(v_p(u)\) modulo \(h\). The required valuation property forces this remainder to vanish outside \(R\). This proves (B14), termination, and uniqueness of the terminal content ledger.

This descent lowers a **content defect**, not \(n\). Its purpose is to construct a finite cover with exact witness transport, not to pretend that every solution has a smaller solution endpoint.

If the endpoint itself is \(h\)-th-power-free, (B14) implies
\[
u\mid R^{h-1},\qquad u\le R^{h-1}.
\tag{B16}
\]
More generally, on any stratum with extracted power part \(v\le Z\),
\[
u\le R^{h-1}Z^h.
\tag{B17}
\]
These are genuine global finite bounds for unbounded endpoint problems. They do not assume a Runge branch at infinity. They also state precisely which unbounded variable remains when no bound on \(v\) is available.

### 4.5. A theorem uniform in k and in all power exponents

A squarefree endpoint is stronger than the hypotheses in (B16) for every \(h\ge2\): its nonzero prime exponents are exactly one. Consequently:

**PROVED.** If \(k\ge2\) is even and \(S_k(n)=y^m\), \(m\ge2\), then
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
n\text{ squarefree}&\Longrightarrow& n\mid R_0(k),\\
n+1\text{ squarefree}&\Longrightarrow& n+1\mid R_0(k),\\
2n+1\text{ squarefree}&\Longrightarrow& 2n+1\mid R_h(k).
\end{array}
\tag{B18}
\]
If \(k\ge3\) is odd and \(m\ge3\), the analogous implications for \(n,n+1\) use \(R_o(k)\). This time the good-prime valuation is two, and no integer \(m\ge3\) divides two.

The bounds are completely explicit functions of \(k\). From (B4),
\[
R_h(k)<6\,2^{k-1} k!((k+1)!)^2\quad(k\text{ even}),
\tag{B19}
\]
and
\[
R_o(k)\le12(k!)^3\quad(k\ge3\text{ odd}).
\tag{B20}
\]
For (B19), \(|U_k|\le k!V_k\), \(V_k\le(k+1)!\), and a radical is no larger than its positive argument. For (B20), \(C_k\le2k!\) and \(|A_k|\le(k!)^2\). These inequalities follow directly by reducing \(kB_{k-1}/2\) and using (B4).

Thus the squarefree-endpoint part of the even-\(k\) conjecture, and the indicated odd-\(k\), \(m\ge3\) part, have uniform finite divisor compilers. The theorem does not say that their outputs are always empty beyond one; each finite coefficient-dependent output still has to be checked or excluded uniformly.

## 5. The coprime cover, the graph ledger, and completed endpoint strata

### 5.1. Even exponents supply three genuinely coprime factors

For even \(k\), reflection makes \(S_k\) anti-invariant under \(n\mapsto-1-n\), and it has roots at \(0,-1,-1/2\). Divide out these three linear factors. The quotient is invariant, so successive subtraction of its leading coefficient times \((n(n+1))^j\) writes it as a polynomial in \(T=n(n+1)\). This finite triangular procedure proves
\[
S_k(n)=n(n+1)(2n+1)J_k(T),\qquad
\deg J_k=(k-2)/2,
\quad \operatorname{lc}(J_k)=\frac1{2(k+1)}.
\tag{C1}
\]
The endpoint coefficients in §4 give
\[
J_k(0)=B_k,\qquad J_k(-1/4)=-2B_k(1/2).
\tag{C2}
\]
For the second identity, substitute \(n=(u-1)/2\) in (C1) and compare the coefficient of \(u\). This also explains the two exceptional supports in (B11).

For squares, (B14) now gives
\[
n=d_0x^2,\quad n+1=d_1y^2,\quad 2n+1=d_2z^2,
\tag{C3}
\]
with positive squarefree, pairwise coprime \(d_0,d_1,d_2\),
\[
d_0,d_1\mid R_0(k),\qquad d_2\mid R_h(k).
\tag{C4}
\]
The coprimality is literal: each pair of \(n,n+1,2n+1\) has gcd one. It must not be replaced by independently selected square classes at different primes.

The associated simultaneous Pell equations are
\[
d_1y^2-d_0x^2=1,
\qquad d_2z^2-2d_0x^2=1.
\tag{C5}
\]
They are a necessary global cover. Their two equations alone can admit extra points; retain the remaining cofactor mark.

### 5.2. Exact recovery and the extra marker

Let \(D_k>0\) clear the coefficients of \(J_k\), put \(J_{k,\mathrm{int}}=D_kJ_k\), and write \(\delta=d_0d_1d_2\). If the original square root is \(Y\), define
\[
w=\frac{D_kY}{xyz}.
\]
Then
\[
w^2=D_k\delta J_{k,\mathrm{int}}(T),
\qquad D_k\mid xyz\,w.
\tag{C6}
\]
The first right side is integral; a rational number whose square is integral is itself integral, by reducing its numerator and denominator. Thus \(w\in\mathbb Z\). Conversely (C3), (C6) and \(Y=xyz\,w/D_k\) reconstruct an integer square solution exactly.

For higher even \(m\), use \(Y=y_0^{m/2}\) and keep that additional power predicate; the square cover is only a necessary cover unless it is retained. For odd \(m\), §4 gives \(m\)-th-power allocations, not Pell equations. The quadratic moving-modulus theorem has not been asserted for those higher-degree equations.

The finite allocation graph assigns each exceptional prime either to no defect or to exactly one of the three endpoint defects allowed by (C4). Terminal vertices encode the three squarefree labels and the common inverse/recovery data. It is a finite branching cover, not a graph whose every prefix is itself a power-sum solution. For example \(S_2(24)=70^2\) does not require its shorter prefix sums to be squares.

### 5.3. Integration with the moving-modulus compiler

For each label, the solver should try all three quadratic presentations obtained from (C5), including
\(2d_1y^2-d_2z^2=1\). A presentation with an explicitly covered odd-trace unit action is valuable; the first presentation need not be the best one. In the label \((2,3,5)\), the equation involving five, rather than the one involving six, supplies the useful order from §3.

Translate the remaining square condition into an affine linear recurrence when possible. Squaring an underlying quadratic unit can convert a coordinate-square equation into a linear trace condition. The conversion must be printed as an exact identity, with its parity class and inverse lattice restrictions. Applying (M4) directly to an even-degree marker would leave it unchanged and give no negative-square obstruction.

If every label has a certified finite orbit-phase cover as in §2.4, followed by exact cofactor recovery, the original fibre is complete. This is a **proved conditional compiler theorem**, not an assertion that every even \(k\) already has such a cover. A bound or descent on the unhandled labels is still needed for a theorem covering an entire infinite class of \(k\).

### 5.5. The precise unbounded complement

These statements do not cover every endpoint. For example the infinite progression
\[
n=24+4900t,\qquad t\ge0,
\]
has \(4\mid n\), \(25\mid n+1\), and \(49\mid2n+1\). None of its three endpoints is squarefree. This includes the classical \(n=24\) exception for \(k=2,m=2\). Thus even a theorem for every \(k\) on the squarefree-endpoint strata would not prove the whole conjecture.

For odd \(k\) and squares, the endpoint multiplicity is two and the power exponent is two. Formula (B13) then gives \(h=1\): no prime-content restriction is obtained. This exact resonance explains why the new endpoint-content descent does not close either the all-even-\(u\) Runge middle range or \(S_{13}=y^2\). An additional independent mark is required there.

## 6. What the mechanism changes, and the uniform-family gap

The fixed-periodic-ledger obstruction in [GHOST_SCHAFFER][design] remains correct. In §1 the ledger is not fixed-periodic in the endpoint: it contains a growing integer \(q_s\), selected from the first nonzero binary digit of a genuine orbit-index difference. There is a symbolic proof of every transition and of the terminal nonresidue. This is the missing kind of global ingredient, rather than an attempt to infer completeness from the density of a fixed congruence sieve.

The content descent is a second global ingredient of a different kind. It quantifies over every ordinary prime factor, extracts actual integer powers, and ends in finitely many defect labels. It does not create the finite rank/coverage/separation of every resulting Pell or higher-degree branch. Keeping this distinction is essential: the finite defect labels coexist with unbounded extracted power parts.

For the entire square Runge family \(k=u^2-1\), even \(u\), I have not produced an auxiliary cutoff meeting a uniformly excluded middle range. The all-\(u\) whole-end theorem in [Sol-A5][sola5] already supplies an explicit global tail. Its finite complement depends on \(u\); no finite number of new fibres turns that into a uniform classification. In particular, a prime-content argument based only on the double endpoint zeros is vacuous for squares, as §5.5 shows.

Three directions remain mathematically concrete:

- On even-\(k\) covers, the output to seek is a **complete finite phase cover** for each needed quadratic orbit, using exact negative-complement identities. Theorem §2.2 supplies its infinite leaves; §3 supplies a solved nontrivial leaf family.
- On odd square fibres, seek a further global cover with a **simple** marked factor, or an odd-degree recurrence coordinate carrying the square condition. Merely rewriting the same double zero or applying a norm-preserving Pell move to a variable norm does not achieve this.
- For the uniform Runge middle range, an auxiliary theorem must control both coefficient denominators and the absolute integer-lattice gap on the entire unresolved region. A recurrence for Bernoulli numbers or an identity between successive sums gives neither the square witness transport nor that gap by itself.

No blanket impossibility claim is being made. The unproved statements are these specific coverage, mark-transport and global separation assertions, not a presumed limitation arising from the age of the conjecture.

## 7. Q2: S13, the integral lift, and what a second cover must add

### 7.1. The new-looking lift is exactly the old one

Put
\[
P(T)=30T^5-175T^4+574T^3-1180T^2+1382T-691.
\]
The finite difference identity is
\[
[T^2P(T)]_{T=n(n+1)}-[T^2P(T)]_{T=(n-1)n}=420n^{13}.
\tag{S1}
\]
With zero initial value it proves \(420S_{13}=T^2P(T)\). Thus \(H_{13}=P/30\), and the proposed lift is
\[
z=14y/T,\qquad z^2=14H_{13}(T)=7P(T)/15.
\tag{S2}
\]
A reduced rational \(z=A/B\) in (S2) has \(B^2\mid15\), so \(z\) is integral. Then \(15z^2=7P(T)\) implies \(7\mid z\). Writing \(z=7r\) gives exactly
\[
P(T)=105r^2,\qquad r=2y/T\in\mathbb Z.
\tag{S3}
\]
Conversely (S3), with \(T=n(n+1)>0\), gives \(y=Tr/2\in\mathbb Z\) and the original equation. The integral lift has not added an independent arithmetic equation or eliminated a Selmer-type coset.

### 7.2. A complementary quotient, and an exact first-descent redundancy

Retain \(s=2n+1\), so \(s^2=4T+1\). Setting \(V=sr\) gives
\[
105V^2=(4T+1)P(T).
\]
The substitution
\[
X=1/(4T+1)=1/s^2,\qquad Y=3360r/s^5
\]
gives the explicit quintic
\[
\begin{split}
Y^2={}&3150-89250X+1289820X^2-11295060X^3\\
&+56210070X^4-120415050X^5.
\end{split}
\tag{S4}
\]
It has the known point \((1/9,1120/81)\). The exact identity behind it is
\[
1024X^5P\!\left(\frac{1-X}{4X}\right)
=30-850X+12284X^2-107572X^3+535334X^4-1146810X^5.
\tag{S5}
\]
Multiplying by 105 gives the right side of (S4). All coefficients are checked independently.

This quotient was already explored: [the earlier minus-quotient discovery log][minuslog] reports a conditional three-dimensional upper space after its local tests, not a rank-one proof, and explicitly says that elementary global square-class coverage was not supplied. I do not treat that exploratory output as a theorem.

There is an exact reason not to expect the two anchored first square classes to be automatically independent. Let \(\theta\) be a root of \(P\), and set \(\xi=(4\theta+1)^{-1}\). Then
\[
\boxed{
\frac{X-\xi}{1/9-\xi}
=\frac9{4T+1}\frac{T-\theta}{2-\theta}
=\left(\frac3s\right)^2\frac{T-\theta}{2-\theta}.}
\tag{S6}
\]
It is just subtraction of rational functions. Thus, on the actual triangular-endpoint ledger, these two **anchored** square classes in the same quintic field are identical. An algorithm may use different normalizations or extra data, but it must prove that those data add something beyond (S6). Counting the two displayed covers as two new independent first-descent bits would be incorrect.

The denominator ledger changes. The exact field norm is
\[
N(4\theta+1)=38227=7\cdot43\cdot127.
\tag{S7}
\]
It follows either by evaluating the degree-five polynomial at \(-1/4\), with its leading coefficient retained, or from the exact multiplication determinant. In the older monic coordinate \(\alpha=3150\theta\),
\(N(3150+4\alpha)=3150^5\cdot38227\).
Thus prime 127 must be included in a transport audit; using only the old coefficient-prime list would lose information. The script checks the norm in the explicit rational basis, without a number-field package.

### 7.3. Why a direct rational change into a Pell conic is not supplied by the lift

The polynomial \(P\) is primitive and irreducible modulo nineteen. The certificate divides it by every monic polynomial of degrees one and two over \(\mathbb F_{19}\), 380 tests, with nonzero remainders. A reducible degree-five polynomial must have a factor of degree at most two. Its leading coefficient remains nonzero modulo nineteen, so Gauss's elementary polynomial lemma proves irreducibility over \(\mathbb Q\). Its derivative is coprime to it, also checked by an exact Bezout identity.

Consequently the radicand \(P(T)/105\) has five distinct simple roots and an odd pole at infinity, in a splitting field. Under multiplication by the square of a rational function, the order of every zero or pole changes by an even integer. Under a fractional-linear change of the variable, these six odd-order places are simply permuted. A squarefree quadratic radicand has only two odd-order places. Therefore

**PROVED limitation:** a fractional-linear change in \(T\), together with rational rescaling of \(r\), cannot turn (S3) into a Pell conic.

This is a polynomial zero/pole multiplicity argument, not an invocation of genus or complex analysis. It does **not** exclude genuine nontrivial covers, higher-dimensional correspondences, or a different descent. It rules out only the direct simplification just specified.

### 7.4. The local triangular ledger still leaves a full finite lifting branch

For every integer \(n\equiv1\pmod{17}\), the equation
\[
P(n(n+1))-105r^2\equiv0\pmod{17}
\]
has \(r\equiv1\). If \(r\) is a root modulo \(17^j\), replacing it by \(r+17^jd\) changes the next residual, divided by \(17^j\), by
\[
-210r\,d\pmod{17}.
\]
This coefficient is a unit. Exactly one next digit \(d\) works. Induction therefore constructs a root at every requested finite precision while retaining the actual integer \(n\) and its triangular \(T\).

This statement uses no completed local field. It explains why the additional condition \(4T+1=s^2\), by itself, does not destroy the known-point local branch. It says nothing about existence of an additional integer solution.

### 7.5. Exact recommendation for S13

The new global squarefree-endpoint theorem does apply to \(S_{13}=y^m\) for \(m\ge3\), with \(R_o=145110\); its complete divisor certificate leaves only \(n=1\). For squares it is vacuous because of the multiplicity-two resonance.

For the square problem, a useful next certificate must therefore be one of the following genuinely stronger objects: a secondary cover excluding the three undecided rational classes; a proved persistent-halving condition on the target classes; or a finite quadratic-orbit cover with exact reconstruction and square markers suitable for §2. The existing \(T\)-ledger and the two anchored first square classes do not establish any of these. The P11/T16 rank-one gap remains, subject to the finite-local rewrite required by the current standard. No old analytic rank or logarithmic cutoff is used here.
