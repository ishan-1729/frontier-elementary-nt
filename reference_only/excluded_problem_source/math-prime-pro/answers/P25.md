## 2. Polynomial carries compress the cofactor alphabet

### 2.1. A general reciprocal-carry theorem — PROVED

Let \(f\in\mathbb Z[X]\) be monic of degree \(d\), let \(a,q\) be positive integers, and suppose
\[
 f(s)=a q^r,\quad r\ge2,\quad
 \beta(s-\theta)=a g(\theta)^r,\quad
 \deg\beta,\deg g<d,\quad g(s)=qt,
                                                               \tag{2.1}
\]
in the free monic quotient order. Define
\[
 H_s(X)=\frac{f(X)-f(s)}{X-s},\qquad
 C(X)=\frac{a g(X)^r-\beta(X)(s-X)}{f(X)}.
\]
Then \(C\in\mathbb Z[X]\), and, with \(b=\beta_{d-1}\),
\[
\boxed{
 \beta(X)\equiv bH_s(X)\pmod a,\quad
 C(X)\equiv b\pmod a,\quad C(s)=t^r.}                       \tag{2.2}
\]
If \(r\deg g<d\), then \(C=b=t^r\) is constant (possibly zero).

**Proof.** Equality in the monic quotient makes the numerator divisible by \(f\); division by a monic integer polynomial keeps integer coefficients. Modulo \(a\), the polynomial \((s-X)\beta\) is a multiple of \(f\). Its degree is at most \(d\) and its leading coefficient is \(-b\), so it equals \(-bf\) modulo \(a\). Since \(f(s)\equiv0\pmod a\), this says
\[
 (X-s)(\beta-bH_s)=0\quad\text{in }(\mathbb Z/a\mathbb Z)[X].
\]
Multiplication by the monic polynomial \(X-s\) is injective even over this possibly non-field coefficient ring: the highest nonzero coefficient of a polynomial remains the highest nonzero coefficient of its product. This proves the first congruence. Substitute it in the numerator of \(C\) and cancel the monic \(f\) to prove the second. Evaluating at \(s\) gives
\(a q^r C(s)=a q^r t^r\), hence the third. Under the degree hypothesis the quotient has degree zero, with value its leading coefficient \(b\). ∎

This uses the full quotient algebra, rather than only its dual-number quotient. It is valid without irreducibility, discriminant assumptions, or division at a bad prime. It applies to the reciprocal cover for either parity of the power sum and for every fixed target power.

## 3. A simple-component obstruction eliminates entire cofactor classes

### 3.1. Finite component valuation lemma — PROVED

Let \(f\) be monic, let \(p\) be prime, and let \(r_1\) be a simple root modulo \(p\). There is a unique compatible root \(r_N\) modulo each specified \(p^N\): if \(f(r_N)=p^N c\), the next digit \(h\) solves
\[
                         c+h f'(r_N)\equiv0\pmod p.
\]
The derivative here is only the formal derivative of a finite polynomial, and the congruence follows by the finite binomial theorem.

For an order element \(b(\theta)\) with nonzero integer norm, any exponent \(e\) for which
\(b(r_N)\equiv0\pmod{p^e}\), \(N\ge e\), satisfies
\[
                              p^e\mid N(b).               \tag{3.1}
\]
To see this, reduce modulo \(p^e\) and use the unimodular basis
\(1,X-r_N,X(X-r_N),\ldots,X^{d-2}(X-r_N)\). The last \(d-1\) basis vectors span the evaluation kernel, which multiplication by \(b\) preserves. Its quotient block is \(b(r_N)\). Its determinant is therefore divisible by \(p^e\).

It follows that at any precision \(N>v_p(N(b))\), the ordinary valuation
\(e=v_p(b(r_N)\bmod p^N)\) is finite, independent of further lifting, and at most \(v_p(N(b))\). Its normalized residue modulo \(p\) is also determined at precision \(N>e\).

Suppose now an actual integral relation has
\[
 \beta(s-\theta)=a\gamma^r,\qquad s-r_1\not\equiv0\pmod p.
\]
Evaluation at a sufficiently high **finite** precision proves
\[
 e\equiv v_p(a)\pmod r,
\quad
 \frac{\operatorname{unit}_p(\beta(r_N))(s-r_1)}{
       \operatorname{unit}_p(a)}
       \text{ is a nonzero }r\text{-th power modulo }p.     \tag{3.2}
\]
This follows by dividing out the exact valuation on each side; if the right side vanished to a higher order than the left, the finite congruence would already contradict it. No completed local field is needed.

For square-class applications this also controls rational square roots. If \(b=(G(\theta)/d)^2\), monic division makes
\(G(X)^2-d^2b(X)=f(X)A(X)\) with integer \(A\). Lift the simple root to a precision greater than \(2v_p(d)+e\), cross-multiply, and compare valuations. Thus \(e\) must be even, and its normalized unit a square. This argument explicitly allows a denominator in a purported field square.

\[
\boxed{
 e=v_3(\beta(r_N))\text{ is odd},\qquad
       \beta(r_N)/3^e\equiv1\pmod3.}                       \tag{3.3}
\]
This condition is independent of the unknown endpoint.

For every label in (1.2),

Thus **one** simple-root lift to \(3^{103}\) tests (3.3) for the entire finite cofactor set. No unbounded local search or assumption about an exceptional valuation is hidden in that assertion.

In particular a field-square cofactor cannot occur. This is a complete all-height exclusion of that whole cofactor square class, not merely of constant coefficients. It does not force the seed square class: \(\beta_0\) is itself nonsquare, and other nonsquare classes can satisfy (3.3).

## 4. An actual global separator from the exact evaluation mark

### 4.1. Zero-evaluation theorem — PROVED

Use P24's general degree-\(d\), target-\(r\) cover, with
\[
 f(s)=a q^r,\quad s\ge2R,\quad
 |g_i|^dR^{id}\le qG^d,\quad f(s)\le\tfrac32s^d.
\]
If \(g\ne0\) and \(g(s)=0\), then
\[
                    \boxed{2a s^{d(r-1)}\le3G^{dr}.}      \tag{4.1}
\]

**Proof.** Let \(j\) be the least index with \(g_j\ne0\). Dividing \(g(s)=0\) by \(s^j\) shows that \(s\mid g_j\), so \(|g_j|\ge s\). The short coefficient bound gives \(s^d\le qG^d\). Raise this to the power \(r\), insert \(q^r=f(s)/a\le3s^d/(2a)\), and cancel the positive factor \(s^d\). ∎

This is stronger than treating the fixed cofactor bound as a rank. The actual contradiction uses a nonzero integer coefficient divisible by \(s\), squeezed by the **source-dependent short-vector bound**.

For S13 it gives
\[
 70s^5\le10^{40},\qquad s\le42754358,\qquad n\le1193.
                                                               \tag{4.2}
\]
The two adjacent integer-power comparisons and both adjacent endpoint values are checked exactly. The finite remainder sums \(i^{13}\) successively and checks the integer square root for every \(1\le n\le1193\); its full list is \((n,y)=(1,1)\). Consequently
\[
\boxed{\text{the full marked cover (1.2), with }t=0,
                         \text{ has only }n=z=y=1.}       \tag{4.3}
\]
No coefficient-degree restriction is part of (4.3).

## 5. A finite polynomial-pencil compiler for the constant carry

### 5.1. General finiteness on a fixed cofactor, by polynomial algebra

Suppose \(F\in\mathbb Q[X]\) is monic and irreducible of degree \(d\ge3\), \(\beta\ne0\) has degree \(d-1\), and its leading coefficient is \(b\ne0\). Set
\[
                       Q_S(X)=bF(X)+(S-X)\beta(X).         \tag{5.1}
\]
For a source with \(r\deg g<d\), the carry theorem requires \(b=t^r\) and \(Q_s=a g^r\).

This polynomial pencil is not a power pencil over \(\mathbb Q(S)\). Indeed write \(U=bF-X\beta\). Then
\(\gcd(U,\beta)=1\), since an irreducible degree-\(d\) polynomial cannot divide \(\beta\). The polynomial \(U+S\beta\), viewed in the variable \(S\) over \(\mathbb Q[X]\), is primitive and linear, hence irreducible by the elementary Gauss lemma. It remains irreducible over \(\mathbb Q(S)\) in \(X\): otherwise clearing and removing common coefficient factors gives a factorization in \(\mathbb Q[X,S]\). Its degree in \(X\) is \(d-1\ge2\), so it cannot be a scalar times a nonconstant polynomial power.

For completeness, the Gauss fact here is just unique factorization of polynomial coefficients: a product of primitive polynomials remains primitive by reducing at a prime irreducible coefficient factor; division over the coefficient fraction field can then be cleared without introducing a nonconstant content. No intersection theory or analytic zero estimate occurs.

Thus exact coefficient tests for being a power give a nonzero polynomial condition on \(s\). An alternative general certificate is the Sylvester determinant of \(Q_S\) and its formal derivative in \(X\). It is not identically zero, by irreducibility and characteristic zero; it vanishes at a polynomial-power member, including any leading-degree drop when the determinant is formed at the fixed generic degrees. Its size is \(2d-3\), giving an entirely explicit finite candidate computation. For S13 the smaller tests below are more useful.

\quad C_i(S)=b_4F_i+Sb_i-b_{i-1},\quad b_{-1}=0.           \tag{5.2}
\]
Define
\[
\begin{split}

\end{split}                                                \tag{5.3}
\]

\[

## 6. A denominator-sensitive marked return theorem

### 6.1. The general finite obstruction — PROVED

Let \(R\) be a free commutative integer ring with an involution \(A\mapsto\bar A\), and let \(A,\bar A\) have nonzero multiplication determinants. Suppose there is a homomorphism \(\rho:R\to\mathbb F_p\) with
\[
                         \rho(A)=0,\qquad\rho(\bar A)\ne0.
                                                               \tag{6.1}
\]
Define in the rational algebra
\[
             U=A/\bar A,\qquad Z_j=A U^j\quad(j\in\mathbb Z).
\]
Then
\[
                         Z_j\in R\iff j=-1,0.             \tag{6.2}
\]
The two listed elements are \(\bar A,A\).

**Proof.** The map \(\rho\circ\text{bar}\) interchanges the two residues. If \(j\ge1\) and \(Z_j\) were integral, evaluating
\(\bar A^jZ_j=A^{j+1}\) under that second map gives zero equal to a nonzero field element. If \(j=-l\le-2\), evaluate
\(A^{l-1}Z_j=\bar A^l\) under \(\rho\) for the same contradiction. These finite equalities cover every integer index. ∎

More generally an integral unit factor cannot repair a prohibited index: if \(Z_jV\) is integral with \(V,V^{-1}\in R\), then \(Z_j=(Z_jV)V^{-1}\) is integral. This bounds the **denominator direction**, not all the independent unit directions.

This is an exact obstruction to manufacturing an integral orbit from a fixed-norm seed by dividing it by its conjugate. It is not an assertion that every element of the fixed-norm locus belongs to that cyclic rational orbit.
