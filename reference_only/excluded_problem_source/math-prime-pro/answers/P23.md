## 0. Results, and the distinction between a new mechanism and a closed fibre

This answer gives two new mechanisms with complete elementary proofs, and an exact arithmetic reduction for the even-exponent infinitude question.

**First: the Bernoulli sequence prevents degeneration of a moving contact.** For every \(k\equiv1\pmod4\), \(k\ge5\), there is an explicit, finitely computable positive integer \(C_k\) such that every square solution satisfies
\[
 \boxed{u\le C_k\bigl(1+|n(n+1)-\delta u^2|\bigr)^{(k-1)/4}
 \quad\text{for every integer }u\ge1,
 \qquad \delta=\operatorname{sf}(k+1).}                 \tag{0.1}
\]
Here and throughout, \(\operatorname{sf}(a)\) is the positive squarefree kernel: \(a=\operatorname{sf}(a)b^2\). Thus \(u\) can be chosen to be either neighboring integer to \(\sqrt{n(n+1)/\delta}\). No fixed shifted-label hypothesis is needed to state this necessary condition on an actual solution.

For the four initial square fibres the certificate gives
\[
\begin{array}{c|r|r|r}
k&\delta&C_k&(k-1)/4\\\hline
13&14&50071&3\\
17&2&258880&4\\
21&22&711691408&5\\
25&26&5848611237751&6
\end{array}                                                   \tag{0.2}
\]
The constants are deliberately coarse and uniform in the shift. They do not improve the optimized cutoff at a particular previously treated shift. Their new feature is that the shift \(a=T-\delta u^2\) may vary and grow: all pairs with \(u>C_k(1+|a|)^{(k-1)/4}\) are excluded. A dyadic coefficient identity proves that the decisive residual coefficient is nonzero for **every integer shift**, uniformly in \(k\). This is more than an assumed nonzero remainder or a finite survey of shifts.

The region (0.1) does **not** cover every endpoint. In particular, ordinary neighboring-square distances of order \(u\) are much larger than the excluded distances of order \(u^{4/(k-1)}\). Sections 1–3 state both the result and this remaining gap precisely.

**Second: unit saturation does not require logarithms, generation, or a census.** A dyadic-box pigeonhole argument proves the exact multiplicative dependence needed in Terra's index-free descent. Full-rank finite unit increments then constrain the first nonzero direction of **every** norm-one unit. At three possible binomial residue branches these directions are incompatible. This gives
\[
 \boxed{|x^r-2y^r|=1,\quad x,y>0,
 \quad r\in\{3,5,7,11,13,17\}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=y=1.}                   \tag{0.3}
\]
The proof for \(r=17\) uses eight displayed units, one nonzero minor modulo 17, and a 17-row residue scan. It needs no generation theorem or large coefficient box. The smaller exponents are independent controls using the same criterion, not imported census conclusions. The equation itself is historically covered by stronger approximation methods; no priority for its classification is claimed.

Combining (0.3) with the original-sum unit, rather than only the consecutive-endpoint equation, proves the following new uniform region:
\[
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
k\ge3,\quad k\equiv1\pmod{31518},\qquad
H'_k=H_k/103^{v_{103}(H_k)},\\
\gcd(n(n+1),H'_k)=1,
\quad S_k(n)=y^{17}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n=y=1.
\end{gathered}}                                               \tag{0.4}
\]
The exceptional prime 103 is **allowed** in the input region and is eliminated in the proof. The more general progression \(k\equiv1\pmod{102}\), \(103\nmid k\), works on \(\gcd(T,H_k)=1\). These statements cover infinitely many summand exponents and, for each, infinitely many endpoints, but are not complete fibres without the indicated coprimality condition. Target exponents divisible by 17 are excluded on the same regions.

For Q1's requested obstruction alternative, Section 4 proves a new, directly relevant limitation: on the normalized square curves for \(k=13,17,21,25\), there is no nonconstant **polynomial constant-norm transport into any fixed odd-degree number field**, even when the transported element may use both the endpoint and its square witness. Thus the index-free unit argument cannot simply be applied to a polynomial expression in the existing square witness. Denominator removal or an additional arithmetic cover would be genuine new input.

For Q2, Section 8 gives one fully specified finite arithmetic predicate \(G(\ell)\), using \(6B_{2\ell}\), \(2^{2\ell-1}-1\), modular powers, and finite color tests. Infinitely many primes \(\ell\equiv1\pmod3\) with \(G(\ell)=0\) would give infinitely many complete even square fibres. This is left **UNPROVED**. It is substantially less restrictive than the original star condition, but is a sufficient recipe, not a reformulation of Schäffer's conjecture.

## 1. A Bernoulli coefficient that cannot disappear at an integer shift

### 1.1. Finite polynomial normalization

For \(k\ge1\), define \(S_k(X)\) by its finite Bernoulli formula, equivalently by
\[
 S_k(X)-S_k(X-1)=X^k,\qquad S_k(0)=0.
\]
The polynomial is uniquely determined: a nonconstant polynomial has a nonzero finite difference of one smaller degree, and a constant is fixed by its value at zero. The Bernoulli numbers can therefore be defined by their usual finite triangular recurrence. For clarity, their parity identity needs only finite polynomials. To obtain coefficients through a specified degree \(L\), take the factorial polynomial \(E(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{L+2}t^j/j!\) at sufficient truncated precision, and invert \((E(t)-1)/t\), whose constant term is one. Call the inverse \(B(t)\). Finite binomial convolution gives \(E(t)E(-t)=1\) at that precision, hence
\[
 B(-t)=E(t)B(t)=B(t)+t.
\]
Its odd coefficients beyond degree one vanish, and the coefficient of degree one is \(-1/2\). The coefficients agree with the triangular Bernoulli recurrence by multiplication. Applying the same finite identity to \(E(Xt)B(t)\) gives Bernoulli-polynomial reflection. The power-sum reflection also follows by the difference and base-value test:
\[
 S_k(-1-X)=(-1)^{k+1}S_k(X).
\]
For odd \(k\ge3\), the finite coefficient identities \(S'_k(0)=B_k=0\) and reflection give double zeros at 0 and -1. An invariant polynomial under \(X\mapsto-1-X\) belongs to \(\mathbb Q[X(X+1)]\): subtract its leading coefficient times the appropriate power of \(X(X+1)\), and induct on degree. Consequently, with \(T=X(X+1)\),
\[
 S_k(X)=T^2P_k(T)/D_k,
 \quad D_k>0,\ P_k\in\mathbb Z[T],\quad
 d:=\deg P_k=(k-3)/2.                                  \tag{1.1}
\]
Take the least coefficient denominator. Then \(P_k\) is primitive: evaluation at \(n=1\) gives \(D_k=4P_k(2)\), so any common coefficient divisor of \(P_k\) would divide \(D_k\), contradicting minimality. All evaluations here reconstruct (1.1) and its full finite-difference identity independently.

Put
\[
 \mathcal H_k(T)=\frac{(k+1)P_k(T)}{D_k}
   =T^d+f_1T^{d-1}+\cdots+f_d.                         \tag{1.2}
\]
It is monic. For \(k\equiv1\pmod4\), \(k\ge5\), the following two facts are crucial:
\[
 \boxed{v_2(f_j)\ge-1\quad(1\le j\le d),\qquad
 f_1=-\frac{(k+1)(k-3)}{24},\quad v_2(f_1)=-1.}         \tag{1.3}
\]

Here is a finite proof of the denominator fact needed in (1.3). For even \(r\ge2\), binomially splitting the sum
\(A_r(2^a)=\sum_{j=0}^{2^a-1}j^r\) into two blocks proves
\[
 A_r(2^a)\equiv2^{a-1}\pmod{2^a}.
\]
The linear correction has the factor 2 from even \(r\); higher corrections contain \(2(a-1)\ge a\), with the initial case checked directly. In the finite polynomial \(A_r(X)\), the linear coefficient is \(B_r\). Choose an integer \(a\) so large that every nonlinear coefficient, after substitution of \(2^a\) and division by \(2^{a-1}\), is even. There are only finitely many coefficients. It follows that \(2B_r\) is odd at two, so \(v_2(B_r)=-1\). Also \(B_0=1,B_1=-1/2\), and the odd Bernoulli numbers beyond \(B_1\) vanish. The last assertion, and reflection, follow by finite multiplication in a truncated polynomial ring, or direct coefficient comparison in the Bernoulli difference identity. No infinite series value is involved.

Every coefficient of \((k+1)S_k(X)\) is thus integral at two after multiplication by 2. Division by the monic integral polynomial \(T^2\), followed by the leading-term subtraction in \(T\), preserves this property. This proves the first assertion of (1.3). Comparing the three highest powers of \(X\) in (1.2) gives
\[
 f_1=\frac{k(k+1)}{12}-\binom{(k+1)/2}{2}
     =-\frac{(k+1)(k-3)}{24}.
\]
Both \(k+1\) and \(k-3\) have 2-valuation one in the stated residue class, so \(v_2(f_1)=-1\).

### 1.2. Exact dyadic law for finite square-root coefficients — PROVED

Let \(H(T)\) be any monic rational polynomial satisfying (1.3)'s valuation hypotheses, of any degree. For a specified positive integer \(J\), compute \(A_1,\ldots,A_J\) by
\[
 (1+A_1z+\cdots+A_Jz^J)^2
 \equiv1+f_1z+\cdots+f_dz^d\pmod{z^{J+1}}.              \tag{1.4}
\]
Missing \(f_j\)'s are zero. Write \(s_2(j)\) for the number of ones in the binary representation of \(j\). Then
\[
 \boxed{v_2(A_j)=-3j+s_2(j)\qquad(1\le j\le J).}        \tag{1.5}
\]

**Proof.** The finite binomial identity modulo \(z^{J+1}\) gives
\[
 A_j=[z^j]\sum_{q=0}^{J}\binom{1/2}{q}
                  (f_1z+\cdots+f_dz^d)^q.
\]
This identity follows either by squaring and coefficient comparison, or by the triangular uniqueness of (1.4). For \(q\ge1\), the product formula for the binomial coefficient gives
\[
 v_2\binom{1/2}{q}=-q-v_2(q!)=-2q+s_2(q).
\]
The factorial equality follows by counting multiples of \(2,4,8,\ldots\), a finite sum. The term \(q=j\) consists solely of \(\binom{1/2}{j}f_1^j\) and has valuation \(-3j+s_2(j)\). Every \(q<j\) term has valuation at least \(-3q+s_2(q)\), which is strictly larger. Indeed \(-3q+s_2(q)\) strictly decreases as \(q\) increases: adding one changes the digit count by at most one. Thus one term is uniquely least, proving (1.5). ∎

Assume now \(d\) is odd. Define the finite shifted coefficient
\[
 C_m(a)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}A_j
      \binom{d/2-j}{m-j}a^{m-j},\qquad A_0=1.           \tag{1.6}
\]
For every **integer** \(a\), positive, negative or zero,
\[
 \boxed{v_2(C_m(a))=-3m+s_2(m).}                        \tag{1.7}
\]
In particular \(C_m(a)\ne0\) for every integer shift.

To prove it, the term \(j=m\) is \(A_m\). For \(t=m-j>0\), all the numerators in the half-integer binomial product are odd, and
\[
 v_2\binom{d/2-j}{t}=-2t+s_2(t).
\]
The other term in (1.6) therefore exceeds the valuation of \(A_m\) by at least
\[
 m-j+s_2(j)+s_2(m-j)-s_2(m)\ge m-j>0.
\]
The digit inequality follows by adding binary integers: each carry decreases the total number of ones. Multiplication by an integer power of \(a\) cannot lower the valuation. Unique least valuation proves (1.7).

This is a useful general contact principle: **a uniquely least coefficient valuation can certify nonvanishing on every integral shift**, even though the corresponding rational polynomial has real or complex roots elsewhere. The integrality of the shift is essential.

## 2. A moving-anchor square squeeze, uniform in k

### 2.1. The exact integer square witness

Write \(D_k=a_0b^2\), \(a_0>0\) squarefree. At a square solution of (1.1),
\[
 z=by/T\in\mathbb Z,
 \qquad z^2=F(T):=P_k(T)/a_0.                           \tag{2.1}
\]
For integrality, \(P_k(T)=a_0z^2\) and a reduced denominator of \(z\) would have its square divide the squarefree integer \(a_0\). Its denominator is therefore one. The leading coefficient of \(F\) is \(b^2/(k+1)\).

Let \(k=4h+5\), so \(d=2h+1\), and write \(k+1=\delta v^2\). At an arbitrary integer shift \(a\), substitute
\[
 T=a+\delta u^2,
 \qquad c=b\delta^h/v>0.
\]
Finite coefficient matching produces
\[
\begin{aligned}
 q_a(u)&=c u^d+\sum_{j=1}^h q_j(a)u^{d-2j},
                  &&\deg_a q_j\le j,\\
 R_a(u)&=F(a+\delta u^2)-q_a(u)^2
        =\sum_{i=0}^h r_i(a)u^{2i},
                  &&\deg_a r_i\le d-i.
\end{aligned}                                               \tag{2.2}
\]
These are identities in \(\mathbb Q[a,u]\), not approximations. The leading square-back coefficient \(c\) is rational because of the chosen \(\delta\).

The first omitted coefficient from (1.6) gives
\[
 r_h(a)=2c^2\delta^{-(h+1)}C_{h+1}(a).                  \tag{2.3}
\]
Thus Section 1 proves that \(r_h(a)\ne0\) at every integer \(a\). This supplies the uniform nondegeneracy missing from a merely formal square-back. It also proves the residual has exactly degree \(2h\) in \(u\) for every such shift.

For verification of (2.3), expand only through the first omitted order in the finite expression
\[
 c u^d\sum_j C_j(a)\delta^{-j}u^{-2j}.
\]
Its square matches \(F(a+\delta u^2)\) to the specified order by (1.4). The omitted term at \(j=h+1\) has exponent \(-1\). Its product with the leading term contributes exactly (2.3) to \(u^{d-1}=u^{2h}\); all subsequent contributions have smaller degree. This is a finite Laurent-polynomial calculation.

### 2.2. An explicit coefficient-only cutoff — PROVED

For a finite polynomial, let its coefficient norm be the sum of absolute values of its rational coefficients. From (2.2) compute:

- \(L\ge1\), an integer clearing the coefficients of \(q\);
- \(D_R\ge1\), an integer clearing the coefficients of \(R\);
- \(C_Q=\|q-cu^d\|\), \(C_R=\|R\|\), and
  \(C_{\rm tail}=\|R-r_h(a)u^{2h}\|\).

Choose the following integer, with square-root ceilings decided by rational integer-square comparisons:
\[
 C_k=\max\left\{1,
 \left\lceil\frac{2LC_R}{c}\right\rceil,
 \left\lceil\sqrt{2D_R C_{\rm tail}}\right\rceil,
 \left\lceil\sqrt{2C_Q/c}\right\rceil\right\}.             \tag{2.4}
\]
Then no integer square solution has
\[
 T=a+\delta u^2,\quad u>C_k(1+|a|)^{h+1}.               \tag{2.5}
\]

**Proof.** Put \(H=1+|a|\). The stated inequality gives \(u^2>H\). The degree bounds in (2.2) imply
\[
 |q_a(u)-cu^d|\le C_Q H u^{d-2},
 \qquad |R_a(u)|\le C_R H^{h+1}u^{2h}.                  \tag{2.6}
\]
For example, a term of \(q_j(a)\) is bounded by \(H^j u^{d-2j}\), which is at most \(H u^{d-2}\) because \(H/u^2\le1\). Similarly a residual term is bounded by \(H^{2h+1-i}u^{2i}\le H^{h+1}u^{2h}\). Summing coefficient norms proves (2.6).

The tail also satisfies
\[
 \left|R_a(u)/u^{2h}-r_h(a)\right|
       \le C_{\rm tail}H^{h+2}/u^2<1/(2D_R).           \tag{2.7}
\]
For \(h=0\) the tail is zero and this step is unnecessary. For \(h\ge1\), divide each lower term by \(u^{2h}\) and use \(u^2\ge H\). Since \(D_Rr_h(a)\) is a nonzero integer, \(|r_h(a)|\ge1/D_R\). Hence (2.7) proves \(R_a(u)\ne0\).

Equations (2.4)–(2.6) give \(q_a(u)>cu^d/2>0\). At a putative integer square value, replace \(z\) by \(|z|\). The integer \(L(z-q_a(u))\) is nonzero because its difference-of-squares residual is nonzero. But
\[
 0<|L(z-q_a(u))|
 =\frac{L|R_a(u)|}{z+q_a(u)}
 <\frac{2LC_R}{c}\frac{H^{h+1}}u<1,
\]
which is impossible. ∎

For each actual endpoint and each integer \(u\ge1\), the shift \(a=T-\delta u^2\) is an integer. Substituting it into (2.5) proves (0.1). This last substitution is why the theorem is a moving-anchor statement, not an unjustified forcing of one previously fixed label.

## 3. What the moving-anchor theorem settles and leaves

For every fixed integer \(a\), (2.5) gives an explicit finite remainder on the cover
\[
 T=a+\delta u^2,
 \qquad (2n+1)^2-4\delta u^2=4a+1.                    \tag{3.1}
\]
It also excludes a growing collection of shifts simultaneously. For example, at \(k=13\), any square source and either neighboring positive \(u\) to \(\sqrt{T/14}\) must obey
\[
 u\le50071(1+|T-14u^2|)^3.                              \tag{3.2}
\]
The analogous powers are 4,5,6 for 17,21,25. This gives a new reusable **Bernoulli valuation plus real integer-gap** theorem: a local coefficient valuation is used to certify nonvanishing for infinitely many moving real contacts, not to exclude the source by a fixed modulus.

There is still no theorem forcing a square source sufficiently close to one of these square grids. The two nearest offsets usually have magnitude of order \(u\), while (2.5) requires an offset bounded by a constant times \(u^{1/(h+1)}\). In particular, adjoining all the proved fixed-label regions does not make the moving regions exhaustive.

The exact remaining square system for each of the four fibres is
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
 &n\ge2,\quad T=n(n+1),\quad P_k(T)=a_0z^2,\\
 &z\in\mathbb Z_{>0},\quad b\mid Tz,\\
 &u\le C_k(1+|T-\delta u^2|)^{h+1}\quad\text{for every integer }u\ge1.
\end{aligned}}                                               \tag{3.3}
\]
Here \((D_k,a_0,b)\) are respectively \((420,105,2),(180,5,6),(660,165,2),(1092,273,2)\). Reconstruction is \(y=Tz/b\). All earlier exact congruence, contact and closed-label exclusions remain additional necessary restrictions; (3.3) does not discard the original square witness in favor of only a Pell projection.

## 4. A precise obstruction to polynomial unit transports from odd squares

### 4.1. Polynomial units with an unbalanced end — PROVED

Let \(K\) be a characteristic-zero field and \(F\in K[X]\) have positive degree. Suppose either

1. \(\deg F\) is odd; or
2. \(\deg F\) is even and its leading coefficient is not a square in \(K\).

Then the only units in
\[
 \mathcal A=K[X,Z]/(Z^2-F(X))
\]
are the nonzero constants in \(K\).

**Proof.** Every element has a unique form \(A(X)+B(X)Z\). The stated hypotheses imply \(F\) is not a square in \(K(X)\), so this is a domain. Conjugating \(Z\mapsto-Z\) shows that the norm of a unit is a unit in \(K[X]\), hence a nonzero constant:
\[
 A(X)^2-F(X)B(X)^2=c\ne0.
\]
If \(B\ne0\), the two terms on the left cannot cancel their leading terms. In the first case their degrees have different parity. In the second case equality of leading terms would make the leading coefficient of \(F\) a square in \(K\). At least one term has positive degree, contradicting constant norm. Thus \(B=0\), and \(A\) is a nonzero constant. ∎

This is polynomial degree arithmetic, not an imported theorem about curves or units. The odd-degree observation is classical; its role here is to test the proposed transport, not to claim novelty for the underlying degree obstruction.

### 4.2. Constant field norm does not repair that obstruction — PROVED

Let \(F\in\mathbb Q[X]\) and let a fixed number field \(K\) satisfy either hypothesis of §4.1 for \(F\). Suppose
\[
 U(X,Z)\in K[X,Z]/(Z^2-F(X))
\]

together with a claimed transport has
\[
 N_{K/\mathbb Q}(U)=c\in\mathbb Q^\times                 \tag{4.1}
\]
as an identity in the coordinate ring. Then \(U\) is constant.

Indeed choose a rational basis of \(K\). Multiplication by \(U\) is a finite matrix with entries in \(\mathbb Q[X,Z]/(Z^2-F)\). Equation (4.1) says its determinant is a nonzero rational constant. Its adjugate gives an inverse with entries in the same ring, so \(U\) is a unit after extension to \(K\). Section 4.1 then makes \(U\) constant.

There are two relevant applications.

**Quotient in \(T\).** At every \(k\equiv1\pmod4\), the polynomial \(P_k(T)/a_0\) has odd degree. Therefore no nonconstant polynomial norm-one construction using just \(T,z\) can take values in a fixed number field, of any degree, by an identity of the form (4.1).

**Full endpoint retained.** The polynomial \(F(n)=P_k(n(n+1))/a_0\) has even degree and leading coefficient \(b^2/(k+1)\). If \(\delta=\operatorname{sf}(k+1)>1\), this coefficient is nonsquare in every odd-degree number field: otherwise \(\sqrt\delta\in K\), which would make \([K:\mathbb Q]\) divisible by two by the tower formula for vector-space dimensions. In particular,
\[
 \boxed{k=13,17,21,25:
 \text{ no nonconstant polynomial constant-norm transport in }n,z
 \text{ into any fixed odd-degree number field}.}        \tag{4.2}
\]
This includes fixed cubic, quintic, septic and degree-17 rings. Using the original witness \(y=Tz/b\) only restricts the polynomial expressions further, so it cannot evade (4.2). Fixed rational denominators in their coefficients do not evade it either.

The statement concerns a **polynomial identity** on the full marked curve. It does not rule out a theorem applying only to its integral points. A successful bridge to Terra's unit descent can use a variable denominator whose divisibility is forced by the original equation, an additional cover, a field depending on the endpoint, or another genuinely new arithmetic witness. Those are exactly the inputs the obstruction does not supply.

### 4.3. Why an unproved integrality step is fatal

It is easy to make bounded rational norm-one elements, and they do not have the discreteness needed for the unit pigeonhole argument. For example let \(D\ge3\) be odd, \(\theta^D=2\), and take integers \(j>D\):
\[
 U_j=\frac{(j+\theta)^D}{j^D+2},\qquad N(U_j)=1.         \tag{4.3}
\]
All embedding moduli are bounded by \((5/3)^D\) for \(j\ge3\), since \(|\theta\zeta^i|<2\). Nevertheless the elements are distinct. Equality for \(j\ne l\), reduced modulo \(X^D-2\), would force both
\[
 j(l^D+2)=l(j^D+2),\qquad j^2(l^D+2)=l^2(j^D+2),
\]
by comparing degrees \(D-1,D-2\), and hence \(j=l\).

Their \(\theta\)-coefficient is
\[
 0<\frac{D j^{D-1}}{j^D+2}<1,
\]
so none lies in the displayed monomial order. More generally an infinite bounded set cannot lie in any one fixed integer order, by the inverse embedding matrix bound proved next. Thus norm one and boundedness without **fixed-order integrality** do not give the finite set in a pigeonhole proof. Equation (4.3) is an explicit obstruction to that shortcut, not a square-solution counterexample.


### 4.4. An arithmetic implication is different from a polynomial identity

There is a finite test for that distinction. Let \(F\in\mathbb Q[X]\) be nonsquare in \(\mathbb Q(X)\). A polynomial condition on a square source reduces to
\[
 A(X)+B(X)Z=0,\qquad Z^2=F(X).
\]
If this is not the zero element of the coordinate ring, its norm
\[
 E(X)=A(X)^2-F(X)B(X)^2
\]
is a nonzero rational polynomial. Every endpoint satisfying the additional condition is a root of \(E\). Its degree is at most
\(\max(2\deg A,\deg F+2\deg B)\), with absent zero terms omitted. Hence only finitely many endpoints satisfy it, and clearing denominators gives an elementary effective root bound and finite test.

Apply this to \(N_{K/\mathbb Q}(U)-c\). If a proposed polynomial norm constraint is not an identity, it is genuinely an additional finite-endpoint condition, not something automatically supplied by the square equation. If it is an identity under the hypotheses of §4.2, the proposed unit is constant. This gives a precise dichotomy for a finite collection of polynomial norm proposals. It does not prevent an integer-only argument from forcing one of their nonidentity conditions; proving that implication would be exactly the missing global step.

## 5. Finite saturation without logarithms or a generation census

This is the generalization of Terra's odd4 §1 used in the new evaluation. The finite local part is strengthened from two selected coordinates to a complete initial-direction criterion; the global dependence part is replaced by a finite dyadic-box argument.

### 5.1. A rank bound proved by a finite box count — PROVED

Let \(R\) be an explicitly given order of degree \(D\), signature \((r_1,r_2)\), and put
\[
 s=r_1+r_2-1\ge1.
\]
Suppose \(U_1,\ldots,U_s\in R\) are multiplicatively independent units of norm one. Then every norm-one unit \(z\in R\) satisfies
\[
 \boxed{z^a=\prod_{i=1}^s U_i^{b_i}
 \quad\text{for some }a>0,\ b_i\in\mathbb Z.}           \tag{5.1}
\]
No index or generating assertion is part of the conclusion.

**Proof.** For each real embedding use its absolute value; for each conjugate pair use one common modulus. The associated weights in the norm are 1 and 2. Choose an integer \(J\ge1\) such that all these moduli for the finitely many \(U_i,z\), and their inverses, are at most \(2^J\).

For an integer \(H\ge1\), consider the \((H+1)^{s+1}\) products
\[
 z^{a_0}U_1^{a_1}\cdots U_s^{a_s},\qquad0\le a_i\le H.
\]
Every modulus is between \(2^{-J(s+1)H}\) and \(2^{J(s+1)H}\). Partition the first \(s\) embedding moduli into intervals \([2^j,2^{j+1})\), retaining the necessary extreme endpoint separately if needed. There are at most
\[
 (2J(s+1)H+3)^s
\]
boxes. If two products occupy one box, the quotient has its first \(s\) moduli between \(1/2\) and 2. Norm one then bounds the remaining modulus between \(2^{-2s}\) and \(2^{2s}\).

All such quotients are in \(R\), because the original products are units. There are only finitely many possible quotients, independently of \(H\). In detail, the matrix of the \(D\) embeddings on a rational basis is invertible. Multiplication by its inverse bounds each integer coordinate of an element whose embedding moduli are at most \(2^{2s}\). Choose an integer bound \(B_0\) for those coordinates; then at most \(K_0=(2B_0+1)^D\) elements are possible. This uses a finite matrix and the triangle inequality only.

Fix one product in a box. Quotients by it distinguish all other distinct products in that box, so the box contains at most \(K_0\) distinct products. If no integer relation existed among \(z,U_1,\ldots,U_s\), all the original products would be distinct. But
\[
 (H+1)^{s+1}\le K_0(2J(s+1)H+3)^s
\]
fails, for example when \(H>K_0(2J(s+1)+3)^s\). A relation therefore exists. Its coefficient of \(z\) is nonzero by the stipulated independence of the \(U_i\); reverse its sign if needed to obtain (5.1). ∎

Every box and product set in this proof is finite. One need not compute its enormous cardinality in the application, since the unknown positive \(a\) in (5.1) cancels in the valuation comparison. This proves the precise saturation implication needed here without importing the unit theorem or constructing a full finite generating cover.

### 5.2. Initial directions of all norm-one units — PROVED

Let \(p\) be an odd prime and suppose, in addition, that an integer \(M>0\) satisfies
\[
 U_i^M=1+pW_i,\quad W_i\in R,
\]
and the coordinate columns \(\overline W_i\in R/pR\) are independent over \(\mathbb F_p\). Put
\[
 V=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb F_p}\{\overline W_1,\ldots,\overline W_s\}.
\]
Then the \(U_i\) are multiplicatively independent, so §5.1 applies. For every norm-one unit \(z\) with
\[
 z^M\equiv1\pmod{pR},\qquad z^M\ne1,
\]
write \(z^M=1+p^e Z\), where \(e\ge1\) is maximal and \(\bar Z\ne0\). Necessarily
\[
 \boxed{\bar Z\in V.}                                  \tag{5.2}
\]

The proof is entirely at finite precision. In a free integer ring, for \(e\ge1\), a primitive coordinate vector \(A\), and any nonzero integer \(b\),
\[
 (1+p^e A)^b
 \equiv1+p^{e+v_p(b)}(b/p^{v_p(b)})A
       \pmod{p^{e+v_p(b)+1}R}.                         \tag{5.3}
\]
For positive exponents, use the finite binomial theorem and
\[
 v_p\binom bj\ge v_p(b)-v_p(j),\quad
 e(j-1)-v_p(j)\ge1\quad(j\ge2,p\text{ odd}).
\]
For negative exponents use the finite geometric inverse at the requested modulus; the signed binomial coefficients are still integers. This proves (5.3) with no series or limiting object.

For a nonzero integer vector \((b_i)\), let \(t=\min v_p(b_i)\). Multiplication of (5.3) gives
\[
 \prod_i(1+pW_i)^{b_i}
 \equiv1+p^{t+1}\sum_i(b_i/p^t)W_i\pmod{p^{t+2}R}.      \tag{5.4}
\]
Independence of the columns makes the last vector nonzero modulo \(p\). In particular there can be no nontrivial multiplicative relation among the \(U_i\).

Apply §5.1 to \(z\), and raise (5.1) to \(M\). If the vector \((b_i)\) is zero, then \(z^{aM}=1\); (5.3) would contradict \(z^M\ne1\). Otherwise compare (5.3) on the left and (5.4) on the right. They have the same exact first valuation,
\[
 e+v_p(a)=t+1,
\]
and their normalized vectors differ only by a nonzero scalar. This proves (5.2).

There is no requirement that \(p\nmid a\), no knowledge of a unit subgroup's index, and no assumption that the supplied units generate every unit. Full rank in the correct number \(s\) of columns is essential: a few independent units do not suffice when the signature permits more independent directions.

## 6. A 17-row certificate closes the coefficient-2 equation at exponent 17

### 6.1. A reusable prime-degree criterion

Let \(p\ge3\) be prime, \(\theta^p=2\), and \(R=\mathbb Z[\theta]\). The polynomial is irreducible by Eisenstein at 2: a monic integer factorization would reduce to powers of \(X\) modulo 2, so both nonconstant factors would have even constant term, contradicting the constant term \(-2\). The usual primitive-polynomial reduction justifies passing from a rational to a monic integer factorization. There is one real embedding and \((p-1)/2\) conjugate pairs.

Supply \(s=(p-1)/2\) norm-one units \(U_i\), their integer inverses, and verify
\[
 U_i^p\equiv1\pmod{pR},\quad
 W_i=(U_i^p-1)/p,\quad\operatorname{rank}(\bar W_i)=s.   \tag{6.1}
\]
For \(y\in\mathbb F_p\), set \(x=1+2y\) and
\[
 C_p(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}(-1)^j
       \frac{\binom pj}{p}\,x^{p-j}y^j\theta^j
       \quad\text{in }R/pR.                            \tag{6.2}
\]
The binomial quotients are integers. Suppose the following finite conditions hold:
\[
 \{y:C_p(y)\in V\}=\{0,(p-1)/2,p-1\},                 \tag{6.3}
\]
\[
 \theta,\quad\theta^{p-1},\quad
 \kappa:=\frac{2-\theta}{\theta-1}
          =\theta+\theta^2+\cdots+\theta^{p-1}
 \quad\text{all lie outside }V.                        \tag{6.4}
\]
Then
\[
 N(x-y\theta)=1
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad(x,y)=(1,0),(-1,-1).         \tag{6.5}
\]

**Proof.** The multiplication determinant gives
\(N(x-y\theta)=x^p-2y^p\). Modulo \(p\), norm one forces \(x-2y=1\). Also the exact constant term of \((x-y\theta)^p\) is \(x^p-2y^p=1\), while every other term is divisible by \(p\). Thus (6.2) is exactly \(((x-y\theta)^p-1)/p\) modulo \(p\).

If it is nonzero, (5.2) requires it to belong to \(V\). If it is zero, it already belongs to \(V\). Hence (6.3) leaves only three residue cases.

**Case 1: \(y\equiv0\pmod p\).** If \(y=0\), the norm gives \(x=1\). Otherwise put \(a=v_p(y)>0\). In \((x-y\theta)^p-1\), the \(j=1\) term has exact valuation \(1+a\); every \(j\ge2\) nonconstant term has valuation at least \(1+2a\). Its first nonzero direction is \(\theta\), contradicting (5.2) and (6.4).

**Case 2: \(x\equiv0\pmod p\).** This is the residue \(y=(p-1)/2\). The equation cannot have \(x=0\). With \(a=v_p(x)>0\), the unique least term is now \(j=p-1\), of valuation \(1+a\), in direction \(\theta^{p-1}\). The same contradiction follows.

**Case 3: \(x\equiv y\equiv-1\pmod p\).** Put \(\rho=\theta-1\), which has norm one and inverse \(1+\theta+\cdots+\theta^{p-1}\). Write \(x=-1+A,y=-1+B\). Unless both \(A,B\) vanish, let \(a=\min(v_p(A),v_p(B))\ge1\). Subtract the norm equation of \(\rho\) and divide the binomial expansion by \(p\). All nonlinear terms have valuation at least \(2a\), including the final terms, since \(pa-1\ge2a\). Therefore
\[
 v_p(A-2B)\ge2a,
 \qquad v_p(B)=a.
\]
Consequently
\[
 (x-y\theta)/\rho-1
 = B\kappa+(A-2B)/\rho
\]
has first direction \(\kappa\). It is a norm-one unit in the same order. Equation (5.3) gives the same direction for its \(p\)-th power. This contradicts (5.2) and (6.4). Thus \(A=B=0\), giving \(x=y=-1\).

There is no missing torsion case: a norm-one element in this odd-degree field has positive real value, since its other embeddings occur in conjugate pairs. If its positive power is one, its real value is one, and injectivity of the embedding makes the element itself one. This completes (6.5). Negating handles norm \(-1\). ∎

## 7. Uniform consequences for odd summand exponents

Throughout this section \(k\ge3\) is odd, \(T=n(n+1)\), and \(H_k\) is \(|D_kP_k(0)|\) with all powers of 2 and 3 removed. The exact finite endpoint valuation inputs, as proved in the supplied Sol-B/Terra packets, are
\[
\begin{aligned}
 v_2(S_k(n))&=2v_2(T)-2,\\
 3\mid T\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 v_3(S_k(n))&=2v_3(T)-1+v_3(k),\\
 p\ge5,\ p\mid T,\ p\nmid H_k\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 v_p(S_k(n))&=2v_p(T).
\end{aligned}                                               \tag{7.1}
\]
The last line also follows immediately from (1.1), since both its denominator and constant residual coefficient are units at \(p\). No formula for an exceptional collision stratum is asserted by that line.

### 7.1. The degree-17 endpoint region — PROVED

If
\[
 \gcd(T,H_k)=1,\qquad
 3\nmid T\quad\text{or}\quad v_3(k)\equiv1\pmod{17},
\]
then \(S_k(n)=y^{17}\) implies \(n=y=1\).

Indeed (7.1) makes every odd endpoint prime valuation a multiple of 17, while the valuation of 2 in the even endpoint is 1 modulo 17. Thus the two coprime endpoints have the forms
\[
 \{n,n+1\}=\{2u^{17},v^{17}\}.
\]
Their difference is one, so Section 6 forces \(u=v=1\). This leaves \(n=1\), and the original sum gives \(y=1\).

### 7.2. The original sum removes the 3-debt on an infinite progression

For \(q=103\),
\[
 q-1=6\cdot17,\qquad 3^6\equiv8\ne1\pmod{103}.
\]
The complete set of nonzero seventeenth-power residues is
\[
 R_{103}=\{1,46,47,56,57,102\}.                           \tag{7.2}
\]
This is checked by enumerating the 102 units, or by their sixth-power equation and the number of preimages.

Suppose \(k\equiv1\pmod{102}\), \(103\nmid k\), and \(\gcd(T,H_k)=1\). First, \(103\mid D_k\), hence \(103\mid H_k\). To see this, the finite contact calculation below gives
\[
 2S_k(103)\equiv-103k\not\equiv0\pmod{103^2}.
\]
If 103 did not divide \(D_k\), (1.1) at \(T=103\cdot104\) would make its valuation at least two, a contradiction. Therefore the avoidance hypothesis gives \(103\nmid T\).

At all endpoints, Fermat's congruence gives
\[
 S_k(n)\equiv S_1(n)=T/2\pmod{103}.                    \tag{7.3}
\]
For a seventeenth power, (7.1) says
\[
 T=2\cdot3^e w^{17},\qquad0\le e<17.
\]
Both \(w\) and \(y\) are units at 103. Equation (7.3) makes \(3^e\) a seventeenth-power residue. Since 3 is not such a residue, \(e=0\). Explicitly, if \(0<e<17\), integers \(a,b\) with \(ae+17b=1\) would express 3 as a seventeenth power. The endpoints are therefore the pure coefficient-2 pair, and §6 finishes.

We have proved
\[
\boxed{
 k\equiv1\pmod{102},\quad k\ge3,\quad103\nmid k,
 \quad\gcd(T,H_k)=1,
 \quad S_k(n)=y^{17}\ \Longrightarrow\ n=y=1.}          \tag{7.4}
\]
The sum-unit forcing in this argument is Terra odd3 §10.2, now composed with a new exponent-17 global terminal. It is not merely a congruence on the endpoint Thue equation.

### 7.3. Remove the exceptional prime 103 completely

We print the all-depth contact needed for this extension. For any odd \(k\ge3\), prime \(q\ge5\), \(q-1\mid k-1\), \(q\nmid k\), and \(a=v_q(T)>0\),
\[
 v_q(S_k(n))=2a-1,
 \qquad
 \frac{2S_k(n)}{q^{2a-1}}
       \equiv-k\left(T/q^a\right)^2\pmod q.            \tag{7.5}
\]

For a finite proof, let \(M=q^as\) be the endpoint divisible by \(q\). Splitting sums into \(q\) blocks and expanding gives, for every positive exponent \(t\),
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^{q^a-1}j^t\equiv q^{a-1}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}j^t\pmod{q^a}.
\]
At the inductive step the linear translation term contains \(\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}i\), divisible by \(q\); higher terms have enough powers of \(q\). Additional blocks multiply the result by \(s\). Thus every positive-exponent sum over \(1,\ldots,M-1\) has valuation at least \(a-1\); the sum with exponent \(k-1\) is \(-s q^{a-1}\pmod{q^a}\).

Pairing \(j\) with \(M-j\) and using the finite binomial theorem, the first term of twice the odd-power sum is
\(kM\sum j^{k-1}\). All later terms vanish modulo \(q^{2a}\). Indeed for order \(i\ge2\), use
\(v_q\binom ki\ge-v_q(i)\) and \(v_q(i)\le i-2\); the last constant-exponent term and the optional endpoint \(M^k\) also vanish. Therefore
\[
 2S_k(M)\equiv-ks^2q^{2a-1}\pmod{q^{2a}}.
\]
The same congruence holds when the endpoint is \(n+1\), after subtracting \(M^k\). The other endpoint is \(\pm1\pmod q\), so its square disappears. This proves (7.5).

Now assume
\[
 k\ge3,\quad k\equiv1\pmod{31518},\qquad
 \gcd(T,H'_k)=1,\quad H'_k=H_k/103^{v_{103}(H_k)}.
\]
Here \(31518=3\cdot102\cdot103\), so \(v_3(k)=0\) and \(k\equiv1\pmod{103}\). If 103 divides \(T\), (7.5) and a seventeenth-power witness force
\[
 a=v_{103}(T)\equiv9\pmod{17},
 \qquad T=2\cdot3^b\cdot103^9 w^{17},\quad b\in\{0,9\}.
\]
After removing the full 103-power from \(T\), (7.5) requires
\[
 -2k\,3^{2b}\in R_{103}.
\]
For \(b=0\) this is the class of \(-2\). For \(b=9\), the factor \(3^{18}=3\cdot3^{17}\) is in the same seventeenth-power coset as 3, so the required class is that of \(-6\). Neither 101 nor 97 occurs in (7.2). This contradiction proves \(103\nmid T\), reducing to (7.4). This proves (0.4).

There are 90 nonzero residues of \(k\pmod{103}\) for which both \(-2k,-6k\) fail (7.2); the displayed progression chooses the particularly simple residue 1. The script verifies that count, but no count is needed for the proof at residue 1.

For each fixed eligible \(k\), the enlarged endpoint region is infinite and genuinely allows the removed prime: CRT combines \(n\equiv1\pmod{H'_k}\) with \(n\equiv103^9\pmod{103^{10}}\). Those endpoints have \(\gcd(T,H'_k)=1\) and \(103\mid T\). The theorem excludes their power witnesses; it does not assert that they are solutions of the endpoint Thue equation.

### 7.4. An all-odd-prime alphabet consequence

Let
\[
 \mathcal P=\{3,5,7,11,13,17\},
\quad
 \mathcal A_{\mathcal P}
 =\{q\text{ prime}:\text{ every odd prime divisor of }q-1
                         \text{ belongs to }\mathcal P\}.
\]
Let \(E\) be the even member of \(\{n,n+1\}\). Then, for every odd \(k\ge3\),
\[
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
n>1,\quad\gcd(T,H_k)=1,\quad
(3\nmid T\text{ or }v_3(k)=1),\quad
\operatorname{supp}(E)\subseteq\mathcal A_{\mathcal P}\\
\Longrightarrow\quad
 S_k(n)\ne y^m\text{ whenever }m\text{ has an odd prime divisor}.
\end{gathered}}                                               \tag{7.6}
\]
All primes at most 43, and also 103, belong to this alphabet. No infinitude theorem about primes of these special shapes is assumed.

**Proof.** Choose an odd prime \(r\mid m\). The valuations (7.1) force
\(O=W^r,E=2U^r\), where \(O\) is the odd endpoint. For \(r\in\mathcal P\), Section 6 excludes \(n>1\). Otherwise write
\[
 W^r-2U^r=\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}.
\]
Here \(W\ge3\) is odd. The positive integer
\[
 C=\frac{W^r-\varepsilon}{W-\varepsilon}
\]
is greater than \(r\), by the finite geometric sum (even the lower bound \(W^{r-1}/2>r\) suffices). A common prime divisor of \(C\) and \(W-\varepsilon\) must divide \(r\). If \(r\mid C\), the finite binomial LTE calculation gives \(v_r(C)=1\). Therefore \(C\) has a prime divisor \(q\ne r\).

That prime is odd and divides \(U\). Modulo \(q\), either \(W\) or \(-W\), according to the sign, has exact order \(r\): its \(r\)-th power is one and it is not one, since \(q\nmid W-\varepsilon\). Hence \(q\equiv1\pmod{2r}\). This puts the odd prime \(r\notin\mathcal P\) in \(q-1\), contrary to the alphabet hypothesis. ∎

For every fixed \(k\), the region contains infinitely many endpoints. Choose a finite Euler period \(L\) for 2 modulo the odd integer \(H_k\), and take \(n=4^{Lj}\), \(j\ge1\). Then \(n\equiv1\pmod{H_k}\), \(n+1\equiv2\pmod{H_k}\), and \(3\nmid T\), while \(E\) has only prime 2. When \(H_k=1\), take \(L=1\). This is the P22 cofactor mechanism with a larger independently closed terminal alphabet, not a purported solution of every weighted coefficient equation.

 \quad\text{or}\quad

## 8. One explicit Bernoulli arithmetic statement for infinitely many even fibres

This section answers Q2 by the allowed reduction alternative. It does not claim the arithmetic distribution statement is proved.

### 8.1. The infinite denominator-six domain

Let \(\ell\equiv1\pmod3\) be prime and put \(k=2\ell\). The finite Bernoulli denominator lemma gives
\[
 B_k=N_\ell/6,\qquad N_\ell\in\mathbb Z_{>0}.
\]
Indeed \(p-1\mid2\ell\) permits only 2 and 3: the additional possible numbers \(\ell+1\) and \(2\ell+1\) are composite. There are infinitely many such \(\ell\), by the Euclid construction with prime divisors of \(x^2+x+1\) for \(x\) divisible by 3 and a proposed finite list. Each new divisor gives exact order three modulo that prime.

This is the already proved denominator part of P22/even3. It does not control the numerator's factor distribution. Put
\[
 N=N_\ell=6B_k,\qquad M=2^{k-1}-1.
\]
The following definition makes the *entire remaining infinitude assertion for one sufficient recipe* a finite arithmetic predicate. It uses no unknown integral point, Pell index, unit generator, or unbounded search.

### 8.2. Definition of G(ell)

Factor the two specified positive integers \(N,M\). At every prime \(p\ge5\) dividing \(NM\), put
\[
 \lambda_p=v_p(N)-v_p(k),\qquad g_p=v_p(M).
\]
If either of the following sufficient stable-contact gates fails, define \(G(\ell)=1\) and stop:
\[
 0\le\lambda_p<2,
 \qquad g_p>0\Longrightarrow
          \lambda_p+g_p-v_p(k-1)<2.                    \tag{8.1}
\]
These are exactly sufficient hypotheses for the finite normalized endpoint/half-endpoint laws in the supplied packets. They are not asserted for every index. A broader compiler can retain collision polynomials instead; the present predicate deliberately has a simple gate.

Otherwise form every squarefree pairwise coprime triple \((A,B,C)\) by the following finite color rules:

- 2 is assigned to \(A\) or \(B\); 3 to exactly one of \(A,B,C\).
- Each other prime may be absent. It may be assigned to \(A\) or \(B\) only if \(v_p(N)\) is odd, and to \(\!C\) only if \(v_p(N)+v_p(M)\) is odd.

Put \(\beta=N/6\), \(\gamma=-MN/(6\cdot2^k)\). For a nonzero rational \(c\), write \(\operatorname{unit}_p(c)\) for the residue of \(p^{-v_p(c)}c\pmod p\). At an assigned odd prime impose these three nonzero-square tests:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
p\mid A&B,\ C,\ (A/p)\operatorname{unit}_p(\beta)\\
p\mid B&-A,\ -C,\ (B/p)\operatorname{unit}_p(\beta)\\
p\mid C&-2A,\ 2B,\ (C/p)\operatorname{unit}_p(\gamma).
\end{array}                                                 \tag{8.2}
\]
Each entry in its row must be a square modulo \(p\). These are modular-power tests, not reciprocity assertions.

At two retain either the low or high condition in the relevant row:
\[
\begin{array}{c|l|l}
&\text{low}&\text{high}\\\hline
2\mid A&B\equiv A+1,\ C\equiv2A+1\pmod8,
\ S_k(A\bmod16)\equiv1\pmod8
&B\equiv C\equiv1,\ 5A/2\equiv1\pmod8\\
2\mid B&A\equiv B-1,\ C\equiv2B-1\pmod8,
\ S_k((B\bmod16)-1)\equiv1\pmod8
&A\equiv C\equiv7,\ 5B/2\equiv1\pmod8.
\end{array}                                                 \tag{8.3}
\]
This is the proved even-packet dyadic unit profile for \(k\equiv2\pmod4\). The low input in the second row is nonnegative because \(B\) is even squarefree.

Next, for each
\[
 p\in\{5,7,11,13,17,19,23,31,37,43\},
\]
require at least one integer \(0\le n<p^2\) satisfying simultaneously
\[
 n\in A\mathrm{QR}_p,\quad n+1\in B\mathrm{QR}_p,\quad
 2n+1\in C\mathrm{QR}_p,\quad S_k(n)\in\mathrm{QR}_p.    \tag{8.4}
\]
Zero residues are included. The interval is a proved complete period: summands have period \(p\), and \(p\) repeated blocks make the sum periodic modulo \(p\) with period \(p^2\).

For every surviving triple other than \((1,2,3)\), put \(q=ABC\). Test its finite area graph. Assign every prime of \(q\) to one of four coprime squarefree factors \((a,b,c,d)\), with product \(q\), representing
\[
 r=aR^2,\quad s=bS^2,\quad r-s=cC_0^2,\quad r+s=dD_0^2.
\]
Require modulo 16 that some primitive opposite-parity pair \((r,s)\) obey these four square-class conditions. At an odd prime of \(q\), require the following two square tests for its assigned factor:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
p\mid a&bd,-bc\\
p\mid b&ac,ad\\
p\mid c&ab,2ad\\
p\mid d&-ab,2ac.
\end{array}                                                 \tag{8.5}
\]
Call the area graph *closed* if its only possible survivor is \((1,q,1,1)\), or if it has no survivor. Finally define
\[
 \boxed{G(\ell)=\#\{\text{source triples surviving (8.2)--(8.4),
 not }(1,2,3),\text{ whose area graph is not closed}\}.} \tag{8.6}
\]
Together with the gate convention, this is a completely specified nonnegative integer function of \(k,N,M\). All operations are finite integer arithmetic. Exact factorization may be expensive, but termination is supplied by trial division if necessary; no probabilistic prime input is part of the definition.

### 8.3. Why G(ell)=0 closes the entire fibre — PROVED

Every square source has a canonical three-anchor allocation. The exact stable valuation laws under (8.1), including the denominator-prime rules at 2 and 3, place it in the declared color set. The units of the original square and the other two anchors give (8.2). The finite dyadic unit law gives (8.3), and its actual residue gives a witness for every (8.4). These are necessary conditions at all heights, not a bounded endpoint search.

A nonterminal row supplies the primitive integer right triangle with legs
\[
 2n(n+1),\quad2n+1,
 \qquad\text{hypotenuse }2n(n+1)+1,
\]
whose area is \(q\) times a square. Among all integer right triangles of that area square class choose one with least hypotenuse. It is primitive: after dividing a common side divisor the new area is an integer equal to \(q\) times a rational square, whose root is integral because \(q\) is squarefree. Its usual coprime opposite-parity parameters \(r>s>0\) give area \(rs(r-s)(r+s)\). The four factors are pairwise coprime, so its factor allocation passes (8.5) and the modulo-16 test.

If the graph is closed, the allocation must be \((1,q,1,1)\). Thus
\[
 r=R^2,\quad s=qS^2,\quad r-s=C_0^2,\quad r+s=D_0^2,
\]
where \(C_0,D_0\) are odd. Their square difference \(2qS^2\) is divisible by 8, making \(S\) even because \(q\) is squarefree. The positive legs
\[
 (D_0+C_0)/2,\quad(D_0-C_0)/2
\]
form a smaller integer right triangle of hypotenuse \(R\) and area \(q(S/2)^2\). This contradicts minimality. Thus every nonterminal row whose area graph closes is empty.

The sole remaining row \((1,2,3)\) has \(S_2(n)\) square. The inherited P20 elementary cannonball theorem gives \(n=1,24\). Its first anchor \(n\) is a square, excluding 24. Hence
\[
 \boxed{G(\ell)=0\Longrightarrow
       S_{2\ell}(n)\text{ square only at }n=1.}          \tag{8.7}
\]
The P20 classification is the only global terminal imported here. The finite area descent has been written out rather than replaced by an assertion that an arbitrary twist is empty.

### 8.4. The one arithmetic statement, and its exact scope

**TARGET IA (unproved).** For every positive integer \(X\), there is a prime \(\ell>X\), \(\ell\equiv1\pmod3\), with \(G(\ell)=0\).

If IA holds, (8.7) proves infinitely many complete even square fibres and all even target-exponent fibres at those \(k\). The statement mentions only Bernoulli data, a Mersenne integer, and explicit finite residue/color calculations. It has no quantified unknown solution of a power-sum, Pell, or Thue equation. It is therefore an arithmetic-distribution target, not Schäffer's conclusion with a different name.

This criterion no longer requires a squarefree Mersenne integer, a prescribed residue for every Mersenne prime, or the original star's particular cross-character pattern. Its stable-valuation gate is still restrictive. In fact, on the present prime-index family, the known equality \(v_\ell(N_\ell)=1\) and (8.1) imply that \(N_\ell\) is squarefree. **That numerator squarefreeness debt has not been removed.** The criterion still requires sufficiently favorable **joint** arithmetic of both integers. The known denominator-six family alone does not prove IA. Neither the exact congruence \(N_\ell\equiv\ell\pmod{\ell^2}\) nor \(N_\ell\equiv7\pmod{24}\) determines the color graph. No theorem here says that an elementary proof of IA is impossible.

The finite replay gives
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|r|r|r}
\ell&k&\text{initial color rows}&\text{rows after assigned tests}&G(\ell)\\\hline
7&14&48&2&0\\
13&26&768&5&1\\
19&38&384&2&0\\
37&74&768&2&0.
\end{array}
\]
At \(k=26\), the only remaining row for this particular recipe is \((1,2,1803)\). That fibre is already closed by stronger programme methods. Thus \(G=0\) is demonstrably **not necessary** for the truth of a fibre, and IA is sufficient rather than equivalent to the general conjecture. At 38 and 74 the recipe succeeds without the original star pattern; for example the numerator prime 37 at 74 is 13 modulo 24.

All alphabet primes for these four replays have a 40-node exact primality-certificate closure, retained from P21 with attribution. The proof is finite order arithmetic: a certified divisor \(F\mid N-1\), witnesses forcing \(F\mid p-1\) for every prime \(p\mid N\), and checking the possible \(1+jF\le\sqrt N\) prove primality. At the largest prime there is one such candidate. No probable-prime declaration is accepted.

Allowing the entire even3 cyclic graph and compressed-orbit machinery would produce a broader sufficient predicate. It would also require the associated finite graph and unit certificates as inputs. The elementary graph in (8.6) is chosen to make IA a single reproducible arithmetic statement without an unresolved “find a suitable global proof” subroutine.

## 9. The reusable method, the residuals, and the next exact targets

### 9.1. Saturation followed by a finite initial-direction cover

Sections 5–6 give the following general certificate structure.

**PROVED conditional separation principle.** In a fixed explicit integer order of signature rank \(s\), supply \(s\) norm-one units with a full-rank finite return increment at an odd prime. Cover every source element by finitely many exact residue branches. On each nonterminal branch, prove that the first nonzero return direction of either the source unit or its quotient by a specified anchor is outside the increment span. Then only the anchor elements can occur.

Proof: the dyadic-box lemma supplies an unknown power relation for every source unit. The finite binomial lemma forces every first direction into the increment span, without any prime-to-\(p\) index assumption. Each nonterminal branch contradicts that requirement. Exact substitution retains or rejects the anchors. This is precisely §§5.1–5.2, with a complete finite residue cover supplied in §6.

The division of obligations is important. A numerical list of independent units is insufficient without exact inverses and rank; fewer than the full signature rank are insufficient for the saturation argument; a root-prime obstruction is insufficient without a complete residue cover; and rational norm-one elements are insufficient without fixed-order integrality. Sections 4 and 6 respectively show a failure and a successful realization of these obligations.

This supplies a useful answer to why odd target exponents have advanced farther than odd squares. An odd prime target forces consecutive-endpoint power labels and therefore explicit **binomial norm-one elements**. An odd square target instead leaves the endpoint valuations automatically even. Its residual curve does not automatically supply the required units, and the polynomial-unit route is blocked by §4.
