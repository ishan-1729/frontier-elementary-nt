## 1. A simultaneous exponent-phase cover of density greater than 29.5%

### 1.1. General finite phase-intersection lemma — PROVED

Let \(m_1,\ldots,m_s\) be pairwise coprime odd positive integers. Suppose a candidate has its exponent in a fixed residue class
\[
k\equiv a\pmod h,
\]
and all powers of nonunits vanish at the prime-power factors of the \(m_i\), beyond a specified finite lower exponent. Let \(L\) be a common multiple of \(h\) and the unit-group exponent periods at these moduli. For an endpoint residue \(b\pmod{m_i}\), define \(V_i(b)\) to be the set of permitted exponent phases modulo \(L\) for which the corresponding sum residue is a square.

Assume that the sum residue really depends only on \(n\pmod{m_i}\) and the exponent phase; this is a necessary hypothesis, not automatic for arbitrary summands. Then
\[
\bigcap_iV_i(n\bmod m_i)=\varnothing
\tag{1.1}
\]
excludes every square value in the permitted exponent class. This follows simply because the exponent of an actual square would belong to every set.

The important improvement over separate prime holes is logical. No individual \(V_i\) need be empty. Different primes can reject different exponent phases, and their intersection can still be empty.

The exact endpoint density of (1.1) is a finite CRT computation. Let \(c_i(V)\) count residues whose phase set equals \(V\). Starting with one copy of the full phase set, successively update
\[
h_{i+1}(W)=\sum_{U\cap V=W}h_i(U)c_{i+1}(V).
\tag{1.2}
\]
By induction this counts every tuple of endpoint residues exactly once. CRT identifies those tuples with the residues modulo \(\prod m_i\), so \(h_s(\varnothing)/\prod m_i\) is the exact density. Bit masks are only a finite encoding of the sets.

## 2. A binary-rank certificate for the four-color area descent

### 2.1. The marked triangle and its colors

Let \(q=2Q\) be a positive even squarefree integer, with
\[
Q=p_1\cdots p_s,
\]
a product of distinct odd primes. Suppose a positive integer right triangle has area \(q\) times a square. Choose one with least positive hypotenuse. It is primitive: division by the gcd of its sides leaves an integer triangle with area \(q\) times a rational square. In lowest terms its root denominator has square dividing the squarefree integer \(q\), so that denominator is one.

The elementary primitive parametrization gives \(r>s_0>0\), coprime and of opposite parity, with area
\[
r s_0(r+s_0)(r-s_0).
\]
The four factors are pairwise coprime. Hence
\[
r=aR^2,\quad s_0=bS^2,\quad r+s_0=cC^2,\quad r-s_0=eE^2,
\qquad abce=q,
\tag{2.1}
\]
with pairwise coprime squarefree positive coefficients. The symbol \(s_0\) is the triangle parameter, not the number \(s\) of odd primes. Our color order is **\((r,s_0,r+s_0,r-s_0)\)** throughout this section.

Let \(z=1\) when 2 belongs to \(a\), and \(z=0\) when it belongs to \(b\). Encode each odd prime's color by
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
\text{coefficient}&a&b&c&e\\\hline
(x_i,y_i)&(1,1)&(0,0)&(1,0)&(0,1).
\end{array}
\tag{2.2}
\]
Thus \(X=\prod p_i^{x_i}\), \(Y=\prod p_i^{y_i}\) are the odd parts of \(ac,ae\).

For \(p\nmid u\), define \([u/p]\in\mathbb F_2\) to be zero for a nonzero square residue and one otherwise. This is computed by \(u^{(p-1)/2}\pmod p\). The elementary proof is that the nonzero squares are precisely the roots of the degree-\((p-1)/2\) polynomial \(X^{(p-1)/2}-1\), using Fermat's permutation congruence. Multiplication adds these bits. No interchange of numerator and denominator primes is used.

Put
\[
\ell_{ij}=[p_j/p_i]\ (i\ne j),\quad
A_{ij}=\ell_{ij}\ (i\ne j),\quad A_{ii}=\sum_{j\ne i}\ell_{ij},
\]
\[
\epsilon_i=[-1/p_i],\quad t_i=[2/p_i],\quad
E=\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon_i),\quad D=\operatorname{diag}(t_i).
\]
All sums in these definitions and the next systems are in \(\mathbb F_2\).

### 2.2. The assigned-prime conditions are linear — PROVED

The complete odd assigned-prime tests from (2.1) are equivalent to
\[
\boxed{
\begin{pmatrix}A+E&D\\D&A\end{pmatrix}
\binom{x}{y}=z\binom{t}{t}.}
\tag{2.3}
\]
This is a statement about these necessary tests, not sufficient conditions for a triangle.

Here is a direct derivation. At a prime in respectively \(a,b,c,e\), the necessary square residues are
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
a&bc,\ -be\\
b&ac,\ ae\\
c&-ab,\ 2ae\\
e&ab,\ 2ac.
\end{array}
\tag{2.4}
\]
For example at \(p\mid c\), \(s_0=-r\pmod p\); and \(r-s_0=2r\pmod p\). Coprimality makes the other factors units, proving that row. The other three rows follow by the same substitutions.

Fix \(i\), and write \(R_i=t_i+\sum_{j\ne i}\ell_{ij}=[(q/p_i)/p_i]\). The required bits of \(ac\) and \(ae\), after removing their possible factor \(p_i\), are respectively
\[
(R_i+\epsilon_i,R_i),\ (0,0),\
(R_i+\epsilon_i+t_i,t_i),\ (t_i,R_i+t_i)
\]
in the four colors. Their actual bits are
\[
z t_i+\sum_{j\ne i}\ell_{ij}x_j,
\qquad zt_i+\sum_{j\ne i}\ell_{ij}y_j.
\]
Substituting the four encodings (2.2), using \(R_i=t_i+A_{ii}\), gives exactly row \(i\) in each block of (2.3). This proves the equivalence by four finite cases.

### 2.3. The dyadic conditions also give linear equations

There are two depth cases: **low** when the square-root variable belonging to the even triangle parameter is odd, and **high** when it is even. Their necessary residues for \((X,Y)\pmod8\) are
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
z&\text{high}&\text{low}\\\hline
0&(1,1)&(1+2Q,\ 1-2Q)\\
1&(-Q,Q)&(6-Q,\ 6+Q).
\end{array}
\tag{2.5}
\]
Every entry is an odd residue.

For \(z=0\), write \(b=2b'\). In the high case \(s_0\equiv0\pmod8\), while \(r\) is odd, so \(c\equiv e\equiv a\pmod8\); this gives \(X=ac\equiv1\), \(Y=ae\equiv1\). In the low case \(c\equiv a+2b'\), \(e\equiv a-2b'\), whence \(ce\equiv5\) and \(ab'\equiv5Q\). Therefore \(ac\equiv1+2Q\) and \(ae\equiv1-2Q\).

For \(z=1\), write \(a=2a'\). In the high case \(c\equiv b\), \(e\equiv-b\), so \(bc\equiv1\), \(be\equiv-1\). As \(bc=Q/Y\), \(be=Q/X\) in the group of odd residues, this gives the first entry. In the low case \(c\equiv2a'+b\), \(e\equiv2a'-b\), hence \(ce\equiv3\) and \(a'b\equiv3Q\). Substitution gives \(X\equiv6-Q\), \(Y\equiv6+Q\). This proves (2.5).

The two characters \([-1/p]\), \([2/p]\) identify an odd integer's residue modulo 8 through multiplicativity: the pairs for \(1,3,5,7\) are \((0,0),(1,1),(0,1),(1,0)\). Here is the finite product proof of the special formulas. Put \(h=(p-1)/2\). After multiplication by a unit \(u\), the absolute values of the least absolute residues of \(u,2u,\ldots,hu\) permute \(1,\ldots,h\): equality of two absolute values would give \(i=\pm j\pmod p\), impossible in that range unless \(i=j\). Multiplication and cancellation of \(h!\) therefore give \(u^h=(-1)^\nu\pmod p\), where \(\nu\) counts the negative residues. For \(u=-1\), \(\nu=h\); for \(u=2\), \(\nu=h-\lfloor p/4\rfloor\). Checking the four odd residues of \(p\pmod8\) gives exactly the listed bit pairs. This is the elementary supplementary square test, not quadratic reciprocity. Equivalently, (2.5) can be imposed directly using the two group characters of \((\mathbb Z/8\mathbb Z)^*\); the verifier compares these with the modular-power bits at each actual prime.

Thus append to (2.3) the four linear equations
\[
\epsilon\cdot x=e_X,\quad t\cdot x=t_X,\quad
\epsilon\cdot y=e_Y,\quad t\cdot y=t_Y,
\tag{2.6}
\]
where the right side is the bit encoding of the required residues in (2.5). This gives four finite affine systems, one for each \((z,\text{depth})\).

### 2.4. Rank forces a decreasing integer — PROVED

**Binary area-descent criterion.** If the \(z=0\), high system has rank \(2s\), and each of the other three augmented systems is inconsistent, then no positive integer right triangle has area \(q\) times a square.

The high \(z=0\) system is homogeneous, so full rank forces \(x=y=0\). By (2.2) this is exactly
\[
(a,b,c,e)=(1,q,1,1).
\]
Consequently \(r=R^2,s_0=qS^2,r+s_0=C^2,r-s_0=E^2\). The positive odd integers \(C,E\) have square difference \(2qS^2\), divisible by 8. Since \(q\) is squarefree even, \(S\) is even. The integers
\[
(C+E)/2,\quad(C-E)/2,\quad R
\]
form a right triangle of area \(q(S/2)^2\), with hypotenuse \(R<r^2+s_0^2\). This contradicts the chosen least hypotenuse. The same argument works whenever the systems have no solutions other than this pure-second-color pattern.

Row reduction is a finite proof certificate: supply the matrix of row operations, the resulting echelon matrix, its pivots, and a contradictory zero row where appropriate. This avoids enumerating \(4^s\) colors. Acceptance needs only bit addition and multiplication, plus the certified modular square tests used to build the matrix.

In an even-\(k\) allocation \(ABC=6d\), the endpoint supplies the primitive triangle with legs \(2n(n+1),2n+1\), hypotenuse \(2n(n+1)+1\), and area \(ABC\) times a square. Thus the criterion with \(q=ABC\) excludes the branch. No inverse claim from arbitrary triangles to endpoints is used.

## 3. What the even-k arithmetic criterion requires, and what remains

### 3.1. A sufficient theorem stated in Bernoulli data — PROVED

Fix an even \(k\ge4\), write \(B_k=a/b\) in lowest terms with \(b>0\), and put \(M=2^{k-1}-1\). Form the finite position alphabet from the primes of \(|a|bM\). The first two labels may use only primes of \(|a|b\); the third may also use primes of \(M\). The prime 2 has colors first/second, and 3 has colors first/second/third. All other primes may also be absent. Require squarefree pairwise coprime labels \((A,B,C)\).

Here is one fully explicit stable-range hypothesis, sufficient to make the assigned-prime tests uniform at every anchor depth. For every non-denominator prime \(p\ge5\) in the alphabet, put
\[
\lambda_p=v_p(B_k/k),\qquad g_p=v_p(M).
\]
Require
\[
\lambda_p<2,
\quad\text{and, if }g_p>0,\quad
\lambda_p+g_p-v_p(k-1)<2.
\tag{3.1}
\]
These are sufficient, not necessary. The finite normalized Bernoulli laws in [Sol-B P2–P3][b-proof] prove that the corresponding offsets hold at every positive anchor valuation. At denominator primes the offset is \(-1\) with no extra hypothesis. If (3.1) fails, retain the exact finite unit-polynomial strata from the even packet, rather than deleting that prime or applying the stable formula anyway.

Set \(\beta=B_k\), \(\gamma=-MB_k/2^k\). Let \(\beta_p,\gamma_p\) denote their normalized nonzero residue units. The allowed parity at a non-denominator prime is
\[
p\mid A\text{ or }B\Longrightarrow v_p(B_k)\text{ odd},
\qquad
p\mid C\Longrightarrow v_p(B_k)+v_p(M)\text{ odd}.
\tag{3.2}
\]
At an assigned odd prime, impose all three conditions
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
p\mid A&B,\ C,\ (A/p)\beta_p\text{ are nonzero squares}\pmod p;\\
p\mid B&-A,\ -C,\ (B/p)\beta_p\text{ are nonzero squares}\pmod p;\\
p\mid C&-2A,\ 2B,\ (C/p)\gamma_p\text{ are nonzero squares}\pmod p.
\end{array}
\tag{3.3}
\]
The first two entries come from the other anchors, and the third from the normalized original square. Thus the fourth witness has not been dropped. At two impose the low/high profile in the [even packet, §4.1][even-proof], or directly use a certified finite set of dyadic necessary tests. Additional finite residue tests are allowed if their periods are proved.

**Uniform conditional fibre theorem.** Suppose a complete enumeration, or a symbolic graph argument, proves that every row passing these necessary tests is either
\[
(A,B,C)=(1,2,3),
\]
or has \(q=ABC\) satisfying the binary area-descent criterion of Section 2. Then \(S_k(n)\) is a square only at \(n=1\).

Proof: the valuation and unit laws cover every original solution. Section 2 excludes every nonterminal row. On \((1,2,3)\), moment contraction gives \(S_2(n)\) square, so P20 gives \(n=1\) or 24. The first anchor is itself a square, and 24 is not; only 1 remains. This proves the statement at all heights. No statement about the converse of the local graph is required.

This is an arithmetic condition on the Bernoulli numerator/denominator and the Mersenne factor, including their cross-prime square characters, together with the explicitly declared finite residue tests. It is not a statement that the condition holds whenever those integers have few prime factors. The row-reduction certificates remove the exponential **area** enumeration; the potentially large **source** allocation still needs its own covering certificate or structural argument.

### 3.2. A directed-graph elimination rule for third-anchor primes

The following small lemma is useful for symbolic source arguments.

**PROVED.** Suppose optional primes \(p_i\) can be placed only in the third anchor or omitted. Fix the other labels and the fixed third-anchor factor \(C_0\). At a selected prime its unit test has the form
\[
b_i+\sum_{j\ne i,\ j\text{ selected}}\ell_{ij}=0
\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_2.
\tag{3.4}
\]
Draw an arrow \(i\to j\) when \(\ell_{ij}=1\). If this directed graph is acyclic and every \(b_i=1\), no prime can be selected.

Any nonempty induced subgraph has a sink: otherwise, repeatedly follow an outgoing arrow, and finiteness forces a directed cycle. At a selected sink the sum in (3.4) is zero, contrary to \(b_i=1\). This proves the lemma. More generally every selected vertex with \(b_i=1\) must have a selected successor, so a selected component must reach a zero-\(b\) vertex or contain a directed cycle. These are explicit remaining locations for the arithmetic information not eliminated by this argument.

For an optional Mersenne-only prime in (3.3), take
\[
\ell_{ij}=[p_j/p_i],\qquad b_i=[C_0\gamma_{p_i}/p_i].
\]
Indeed \(C/p_i=C_0\prod_{j\ne i,\ j\text{ selected}}p_j\), and multiplicativity gives exactly (3.4). The first two anchor-unit requirements may reject additional choices. Thus the graph lemma is a concrete sufficient rule for removing an entire variable part of the alphabet. It is not an assumption that actual Bernoulli/Mersenne graphs are acyclic.

### 3.3. An infinite denominator-six family, with the missing correlation explicit

**PROVED.** There are infinitely many indices
\[
k=2\ell,\qquad \ell\text{ prime},\quad \ell\equiv1\pmod3,
\tag{3.5}
\]
for which
\[
B_k=\frac{N_\ell}{6},\qquad N_\ell>0,
\qquad N_\ell\equiv7\pmod{24},
\qquad N_\ell\equiv\ell\pmod{\ell^2}.
\tag{3.6}
\]
In particular the prime \(\ell\) occurs to exact exponent one in the numerator.

For infinitude, given finitely many primes \(1\pmod3\), take \(x\) divisible by them and by 3. Every prime divisor of \(x^2+x+1\) is different from those primes and from 3. Modulo such a divisor, \(x\) has order three: its cube is 1, while \(x=1\) would force the prime to divide 3. Fermat then makes the new prime 1 modulo 3. This is a Euclid argument, not Dirichlet's theorem.

For such \(\ell\ge7\), the divisors of \(2\ell\) are \(1,2,\ell,2\ell\). The finite Bernoulli denominator theorem says that its denominator is the product of primes \(p\) with \(p-1\mid2\ell\). Besides 2 and 3, the only possibilities would be \(\ell+1\), an even integer greater than 2, and \(2\ell+1\), divisible by 3 and greater than 3. Both are composite. Thus the denominator is exactly 6. The sign is positive because \(k\equiv2\pmod4\), by the finite Bernoulli sign theorem in the supplied packet.

The calibrated dyadic coefficient unit \(2B_k\equiv5\pmod8\) gives \(N_\ell\equiv7\pmod8\). The finite denominator congruence \(3B_k\equiv-1\pmod3\) gives \(N_\ell\equiv1\pmod3\), proving the first residue in (3.6). Finally the finite normalized Kummer congruence at \(\ell\) gives
\[
\frac{B_{2\ell}}{2\ell}\equiv\frac{B_2}{2}=\frac1{12}\pmod\ell.
\]
Both Euler factors in that congruence are 1 modulo \(\ell\). Since \(B_{2\ell}=N_\ell/6\), multiplication proves \(N_\ell\equiv\ell\pmod{\ell^2}\). The use of the normalized finite congruence is important here; no regular-prime assumption is made.

Since the numerator is prime to 6, its square factors are 1 modulo 24; hence \(\operatorname{sf}(N_\ell)\equiv7\pmod{24}\) as well. This produces a particularly concrete infinite test family for Q1. Its compulsory invisible row is
\[
(6\operatorname{sf}(N_\ell),1,1).
\tag{3.7}
\]
What remains is **not** denominator control. It is a proof, for infinitely many \(\ell\), that the complete source graph has only terminal/descending rows, including the matrix of the primes of \(\operatorname{sf}(N_\ell)\), and the optional primes of \(2^{2\ell-1}-1\). Equations (3.6) do not determine those mutual characters or eliminate the other colors. I have not proved that additional assertion. An inability of these particular congruences to imply it is not a theorem that no elementary argument can.

### 3.4. The invisible branch: what a uniform second split would have to use

For general \(k\equiv2\pmod4\), the even packet proves finite local solubility of
\[
(A_0,1,1),\qquad A_0=b\operatorname{sf}(a),\quad B_k=a/b>0,
\tag{3.8}
\]
from the exact expansion
\[
S_k(A_0u^2)/u^2=R^2+u^2J(u^2).
\]
Taking \(u\) sufficiently divisible by each prescribed prime makes the normalized unit a square to every prescribed finite precision. This explains why an all-depth stable unit does not eliminate that branch.

There is also an explicit global warning against using only its coarse coefficient residue. Set
\[
q=14066106=2\cdot3\cdot17\cdot239\cdot577,
\qquad n=38034750624.
\]
Then
\[
q/6\equiv7\pmod{24},\quad
n=q\cdot52^2,\quad n+1=195025^2,\quad2n+1=275807^2.
\tag{3.9}
\]
Thus even the complete three-anchor system can have a positive point when the squarefree coefficient has the same coarse residue as (3.6). This is **not** asserted to be a Bernoulli coefficient or a power-sum solution. It refutes the proposed conclusion from the residue and the three anchors alone. The actual Bernoulli example \(q=330\) in Section 2 separately refutes universal triangle emptiness for all indices.

Retaining all three anchors on a branch \((q,1,1)\), with \(q=6d\) squarefree, gives the exact second split
\[
a^2R^4-6abR^2S^2+b^2S^4=1,
\qquad ab=q,\quad\gcd(a,b)=1,
\tag{3.10}
\]
\[
n=4qR^2S^2,\quad
u=2RS,\quad v=|aR^2-bS^2|,\quad w=aR^2+bS^2.
\]
Proof: \(v,w\) are odd, and their square difference \(qu^2\) is divisible by 8, so \(u=2t\). The coprime integers \((w+v)/2,(w-v)/2\) have product \(qt^2\), and unique factorization gives \(aR^2,bS^2\) and \(t=RS\). Substitution gives (3.10); conversely the displayed formulas reconstruct the anchors.

The original Faulhaber root is still required. Write \(D S_k=T(2n+1)P_k(T)\). On an arbitrary surviving row \(ABC=6d\), the exact residual system is
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&Bv^2-Au^2=1,\qquad Cw^2-2Au^2=1,\\
&h^2=ABC\,D\,P_k(Au^2(Au^2+1)),\qquad D\mid huvw.
\end{aligned}}
\tag{3.11}
\]
Here \(h=Dy/(uvw)\) is integral because its square is the displayed integer, and conversely \(y=huvw/D\). This is an equivalence, not just a projection. In (3.10) one must substitute the reconstructed \(n,u,v,w\) in (3.11).

**Exact Q1 stopping point.** The rank theorem closes every row satisfying its criterion, uniformly. It does not decide non-descending rows of (3.11), and no infinitude statement for the necessary Bernoulli character correlations is proved. A successful uniform second split must use a genuine property of the Bernoulli coefficient or of the last equation in (3.11); the positive example (3.9) shows why the three-anchor shape and residue alone are insufficient.

## 4. Odd squares: a general branch budget and an exact shift obstruction

This section proves obstruction theorems, not empty-fibre theorems. Their role is to identify precisely which proposed transports cannot supply the missing second hit.

### 4.1. An elementary rational-map branch budget — PROVED

For a nonzero rational function \(f(X)\) over a characteristic-zero field, let \(b(f)\) be the number of points of the algebraic projective line at which its zero or pole order is odd. Multiplication by a rational square does not change this finite set. Orders at infinity are included.

Let \(R=P/Q\) be nonconstant of degree \(d\), with coprime polynomials \(P,Q\). If \(f(R)/g\) is a rational square, then
\[
\boxed{d\bigl(b(f)-4\bigr)\le b(g)-4.}
\tag{4.1}
\]
Only polynomial multiplicities are used in the proof.

First, above every target point, the local multiplicities of \(R\) sum to \(d\). For a finite value this follows by factoring \(P-aQ\), with its missing degree supplied at infinity; for infinity factor \(Q\), again including infinity if necessary. The total of \(e_x-1\) over all source points is \(2d-2\). Here is an elementary proof of the latter identity, rather than an imported dimension or ramification theorem.

Set \(W=P'Q-PQ'\ne0\). At each finite non-pole point its zero order is exactly \(e_x-1\), by differentiating the first nonzero finite polynomial term after subtracting the value of \(R\). At a pole of order \(e_x\), coprimality gives the same zero order in \(W\). For infinity let its local multiplicity be \(e_\infty\). If \(\deg P\ne\deg Q\), the leading terms give
\[
\deg W=\deg P+\deg Q-1=2d-e_\infty-1.
\]
If both degrees equal \(d\), subtract the leading-value multiple of \(Q\) from \(P\). Let the resulting nonzero polynomial have degree \(h<d\). Then \(e_\infty=d-h\), and its leading coefficient in the new expression for \(W\) gives \(\deg W=d+h-1=2d-e_\infty-1\). Thus adding \(e_\infty-1\) to the finite multiplicity sum gives exactly \(2d-2\). Formal differentiation and factor multiplicities suffice; no contour argument occurs.

Above an odd-order zero or pole of \(f\), the pullback order is odd exactly when \(e_x\) is odd. Moreover
\[
e_x\le 1_{\{e_x\text{ odd}\}}+2(e_x-1).
\]
Sum over the preimages of all \(b(f)\) such points. The left side is \(db(f)\). The number of odd preimages is \(b(g)\), because \(f(R)/g\) is a square. The remaining sum is at most \(2(2d-2)\). Rearrangement proves (4.1).

A related consequence is needed. In the quadratic field \(K(X)(\sqrt g)\), an element \(A+B\sqrt g\) whose square is rational satisfies \(AB=0\). Thus a square root of \(f(R)\) is either already rational, or gives \(f(R)/g\) a rational square. If \(b(f)>4\), the first possibility is impossible: apply the same inequality with \(b(g)=0\). Therefore (4.1) applies to every such new square root.

In particular, for \(g=f\) and \(b(f)>4\), one has \(d\le1\). This is an obstruction to same-fibre rational self-transports whose endpoint coordinate is a function of \(X\) alone, at **every degree**, not just at a previously tested small degree.

### 4.2. Four odd fibres: only identity or reflection persists at unbounded integer endpoints

For \(k\in\{13,17,21,25\}\), put
\[
F_k(n)=\frac{S_k(n)}{n^2(n+1)^2}=\frac{P_k(n(n+1))}{D_k}.
\]
These polynomials are squarefree of respective degrees \(10,14,18,22\). This is independently certified here: the primitive \(P_k\) has degree-preserving squarefree reduction modulo respectively \(11,7,13,11\), and \(P_k(-1/4)\ne0\). The latter condition prevents the critical point of \(n(n+1)\) from creating a repeated root. All coefficients are regenerated from the finite difference identity of the sum, not copied from a numerical factorization.

Suppose there is an identity-level rational transport
\[
\widetilde n=R(n)\in\mathbb Q(n),\qquad
\widetilde Y\in\mathbb Q(n,\sqrt{F_k(n)}),\qquad
\widetilde Y^2=F_k(R(n)),
\tag{4.2}
\]
with nonconstant \(R\). Since \(b(F_k)=k-3>4\), Section 4.1 forces \(\deg R=1\).

The affine cases can be classified without finding a root. Set \(x=n+1/2\) and \(N=k-3\). Finite coefficient comparison gives
\[
F_k(x-1/2)=\frac{x^N}{k+1}
-\frac{(k+4)(k-3)}{24(k+1)}x^{N-2}+\text{lower terms}.
\tag{4.3}
\]
It is even. For completeness, the centered leading terms of \(S_k\) are \(x^{k+1}/(k+1)-kx^{k-1}/24\); dividing by \((x^2-1/4)^2\) gives the second coefficient in (4.3) by finite polynomial division.

Thus the sum of the roots is zero and their sum of squares is
\[
\frac{(k+4)(k-3)}{12}\ne0.
\tag{4.4}
\]
An affine map preserving the finite root set must therefore have zero translation (use the root sum) and square of its slope equal to one (use (4.4)). The only affine endpoint maps are
\[
\boxed{n\longmapsto n,\qquad n\longmapsto-1-n.}
\tag{4.5}
\]
Both preserve the original sum by identity/reflection; neither gives a smaller positive endpoint.

A non-affine rational degree-one map has the form
\[
R(n)=\frac{an+b}{cn+d},\qquad a,b,c,d\in\mathbb Z,
\quad c\ne0,\quad \Delta=ad-bc\ne0.
\]
If both \(n\) and \(R(n)\) are integers, then
\[
cn+d\mid\Delta,
\qquad
n\le\frac{|\Delta|+|d|}{|c|}\quad(n\ge0).
\tag{4.6}
\]
Indeed \(c(an+b)-a(cn+d)=-\Delta\). This proves both assertions.

**Finite-atlas consequence.** Above the maximum of the bounds (4.6) for a finite collection of transports (4.2), none of its maps takes any positive integer endpoint to a smaller positive integer endpoint: its non-affine members no longer have integral outputs, and its affine members are (4.5). Thus this identity atlas has no nontrivial descending edge there; it cannot supply the missing descent unless those endpoints have already been excluded. Constant maps simply send everything to a fixed seed and do not supply an invertible or exhaustive descent; recovering arbitrary sources from them is a separate missing assertion.

This conclusion is about algebraic identities of the specified form. It does not exclude a rational new endpoint involving the root witness, an integer-specific implication not induced by such an identity, a changing equation, or infinitely many dynamically chosen presentations. In particular it does not prove that the four square fibres have any nontrivial points, nor does it prove that an elementary solution is impossible.

### 4.3. One marked Pell hit cannot rationally generate its shifted neighbours — PROVED

Let \(G(Z)\in K(Z)\) be nonsquare, and suppose its odd-order zero/pole support has a nonzero finite point. Let \(\eta\in K^*\) not be a root of unity. There is no identity
\[
U(Z,Y)^2=G(\eta Z),\qquad
U\in K(Z,Y),\quad Y^2=G(Z),
\tag{4.7}
\]
when \(K\) has characteristic zero.

Every element of the quadratic field is \(A(Z)+B(Z)Y\). If its square is rational, then \(AB=0\). The case \(B=0\) would make \(G(\eta Z)\) a rational square, hence also \(G(Z)\), impossible. Otherwise \(G(\eta Z)/G(Z)\) is a rational square. Its odd divisor support on the nonzero finite line must consequently be invariant under multiplication by \(\eta\). This support is finite and nonempty; repeated multiplication would make an orbit repeat and hence a positive power of \(\eta\) equal to one. Contradiction.

Apply this to the shifted-label conic
\[
T=2+\delta v^2,\qquad x^2-4\delta v^2=9.
\]
Over \(K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt\delta)\), a parameter \(Z\ne0\) gives
\[
x=\frac32(Z+Z^{-1}),\qquad
v=\frac{3}{4\sqrt\delta}(Z-Z^{-1}),
\qquad
T(Z)=\frac{9Z^4+14Z^2+9}{16Z^2}.
\tag{4.8}
\]
A nontrivial positive Pell return is multiplication of \(Z\) by a real \(\eta>1\), so it is not a root of unity.

For the four fibres, use \(G(Z)=P_k(T(Z))/a_k\), with the fixed squarefree scalar \(a_k\) of the integral lift. The polynomial \(P_k\) is squarefree, and \(P_k(2)P_k(-1/4)\ne0\). The critical nonzero points of \(T(Z)\) satisfy \(Z^4=1\), with critical values 2 and \(-1/4\). Thus the preimages of every root of \(P_k\) are simple and nonzero; the required odd support is present. The certificate also directly checks squarefreeness of the cleared pullback polynomial, modulo the small primes listed above.

Therefore (4.7) is impossible for **every** nonzero fixed Pell shift. The Laurent-annihilator theorem may forbid a long progression of actual hits, but one original hit cannot supply that progression by a rational fixed-shift formula on this cover. Contact information at particular integers does not change this function-field identity obstruction. An arithmetic, integral-point-only implication remains a different and potentially stronger mechanism.

### 4.4. Exact Q2 residuals

For any of the four odd square fibres, let \(D_k=a_kb_k^2\), \(a_k\) squarefree. The exact remaining system is
\[
\boxed{a_kz^2=P_k(T),\quad w^2=4T+1,\quad
w=2n+1>0,\quad b_k\mid Tz,\quad n>1.}
\tag{4.9}
\]
It recovers \(y=Tz/b_k\). At \(k=13,17,21,25\), retain Terra's exceptional-prime exclusions, the filed dyadic/contact conditions, and remove the completed shifted class
\[
T-2=\operatorname{sf}(k+1)v^2.
\]
One may additionally remove the endpoint set \(\mathcal N\) of Section 1. This is an exact description of the residual necessary equations with the stated excluded regions; no finite height is imposed on the complement.

The branch budget rules out fixed rational endpoint self-descents, and (4.7) rules out rational fixed-shift manufacture of the annihilator's neighbours. Neither gives a contradiction in (4.9). The missing input is an arithmetic implication on these integral points that is not one of those identity-level transports, or a separator for the isolated surviving hits.

## 5. Odd target primes: a global cyclotomic marker and a prime-alphabet closure

### 5.1. A forced prime in the even endpoint — PROVED

Let \(r\ge5\) be an odd prime, \(W\ge3\) an odd integer, \(U>0\), \(b\ge0\), and \(\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}\). Suppose
\[
W^r-\varepsilon=2\cdot3^b U^r.
\tag{5.1}
\]
Then some prime \(q\mid U\) satisfies
\[
\boxed{q\equiv1\pmod{2r}.}
\tag{5.2}
\]
In particular the even member \(E=2\cdot3^bU^r\) satisfies
\[
E\ge2(2r+1)^r.
\tag{5.3}
\]
This is a prime supplied by the exact equation, not a prime merely allowed by a local test.

Define the finite polynomial cofactor
\[
C_\varepsilon(W)=\frac{W^r-\varepsilon}{W-\varepsilon}
=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\varepsilon^{r-1-i}W^i.
\tag{5.4}
\]
It is positive, odd and greater than \(r\). For the plus-sign denominator case its terms are all positive. In the alternating case its leading pair alone is \(W^{r-2}(W-1)\), with all subsequent pairs and the final constant positive; this exceeds \(r\) for \(W\ge3,r\ge3\), by an elementary induction in \(r\).

Every prime divisor \(q\ne r\) of (5.4) has multiplicative order of \(W\) equal to \(r\) if \(\varepsilon=1\), and \(2r\) if \(\varepsilon=-1\). Indeed \(W\) is a unit, and if \(W\equiv\varepsilon\pmod q\), evaluation of the cofactor gives \(C_\varepsilon(W)\equiv r\pmod q\), impossible. All remaining proper divisors of the asserted order are therefore excluded. Fermat's congruence gives (5.2); in the order-\(r\) case, the oddness of \(q\) supplies the factor two.

If \(r\) divides the cofactor, then \(W\equiv\varepsilon\pmod r\), and
\[
v_r(C_\varepsilon(W))=1.
\tag{5.5}
\]
To prove this directly, put \(W=\varepsilon+h\), \(r\mid h\). In the finite expansion of \((\varepsilon+h)^r-\varepsilon\), the linear term has valuation \(1+v_r(h)\), and every higher term has strictly greater valuation. Dividing by \(h\) proves (5.5). Conversely Fermat's congruence shows that when \(W\not\equiv\varepsilon\pmod r\), the cofactor is 1 modulo \(r\).

Since \(C_\varepsilon(W)>r\), it has a prime divisor different from \(r\). Such a divisor is neither 2 nor 3: the cofactor is odd, and the order just proved cannot occur in the two-element nonzero residue group modulo 3 when \(r\ge5\). By (5.1) this divisor belongs to \(U\). This proves (5.2) and (5.3). No primitive-divisor theorem or reciprocity statement was used.

### 5.2. The exact second power witness, and why its smaller size is not yet a descent

The same factorization gives more than a prime. Put \(c=2\cdot3^b\), and choose \(\delta\in\{0,1\}\) according as \(r\) divides the cofactor. There are positive integers \(A,V\) with
\[
\boxed{
C_\varepsilon(W)=r^\delta V^r,\qquad
W-\varepsilon=c\,r^{\delta(r-1)}A^r,
\qquad U=r^\delta AV.}
\tag{5.6}
\]
Moreover
\[
\gcd(A,V)=1,\quad\gcd(V,6r)=1,\quad 1<V<W,
\tag{5.7}
\]
and every prime divisor of \(V\) is 1 modulo \(2r\).

The gcd of the two factors in (5.4) divides \(r\), since reduction modulo \(W-\varepsilon\) gives the value \(r\). Every prime other than \(r\) in the cofactor consequently occurs to an exponent divisible by \(r\), by (5.1); its valuation at \(r\) is (5.5). The primes 2 and 3 occur only in the linear factor. Unique factorization proves (5.6)–(5.7), including the exact \(r^{r-1}\) in the linear factor when \(\delta=1\). Finally
\[
0<C_\varepsilon(W)<W^r\qquad(W\ge3)
\]
by the finite geometric sum, so \(V<W\). The inequality \(V>1\) follows from \(C_\varepsilon(W)>r\).

Thus a single witness has genuinely transported to another discrete power marker and a strictly smaller integer \(V\). What is absent is a theorem that this smaller integer satisfies another equation of the form (5.1), or any already closed marked class. Its equation is instead the degree-\((r-1)\) cofactor equation in (5.6), with the simultaneous linear-factor witness retained. Calling \(V<W\) a descent while discarding that change of equation would be invalid. Equations (5.6)–(5.7) specify the remaining problem exactly.

### 5.3. Apply the transport to every odd summand exponent

Fix odd \(k\ge3\), with the primitive normalization
\[
S_k(n)=T^2P_k(T)/D_k,
\]
and let \(H_k\) be \(|D_kP_k(0)|\) after removing powers of 2 and 3. Suppose \(\gcd(T,H_k)=1\), and \(S_k(n)=y^r\) for an odd prime \(r\). The accepted finite endpoint valuation laws give
\[
\begin{aligned}
&v_2(S_k)=2v_2(T)-2,\\
&3\mid T\Longrightarrow v_3(S_k)=2v_3(T)-1+v_3(k),\\
&p\ge5,\ p\mid T\Longrightarrow v_p(S_k)=2v_p(T).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.8}
\]
Consequently, for \(p\ge5\), every endpoint valuation is divisible by \(r\), while the valuation of 2 in the even endpoint is 1 modulo \(r\).

Let \(E\) be the even member and \(O\) the odd member of \(\{n,n+1\}\). If \(3\nmid O\), the entire odd endpoint is an \(r\)-th power, and
\[
O=W^r,\qquad E=2\cdot3^bU^r,
\tag{5.9}
\]
where \(0\le b<r\); if 3 divides \(E\), then
\[
2b\equiv1-v_3(k)\pmod r,
\tag{5.10}
\]
and otherwise \(b=0\). If instead \(v_3(k)\equiv1\pmod r\), every 3-valuation is also divisible by \(r\), so (5.9) holds with \(b=0\), even when 3 divides \(O\). The difference of the two consecutive endpoints is exactly (5.1).

For \(n>1\), \(O\ge3\); if it is \(W^r\), then \(W\ge3\) is odd. Thus Sections 5.1–5.2 apply. This is a global consequence of the summation valuation ledger plus consecutive endpoints: a prime \(1\pmod{2r}\), to at least the \(r\)-th power, is forced into \(E\), and the two equations (5.6) are forced simultaneously.

### 5.4. An all-odd-prime theorem on a genuine infinite endpoint region — PROVED

Define the explicit prime alphabet
\[
\mathcal A_{3,5}
=\{q\text{ prime}:\text{ every odd prime factor of }q-1
\text{ belongs to }\{3,5\}\}.
\tag{5.11}
\]
This definition includes 2 and primes whose predecessor is a power of two. It is a test on a finite integer for each specified prime; no infinitude assertion about primes of a particular shape is needed.

For every odd \(k\ge3\), suppose
\[
n>1,\quad\gcd(T,H_k)=1,\quad
\operatorname{supp}(E)\subseteq\mathcal A_{3,5},
\quad (3\nmid O\text{ or }v_3(k)=1).
\tag{5.12}
\]
Then \(S_k(n)\) is not an \(m\)-th power for any \(m\) having an odd prime divisor.

Choose such a divisor \(r\). At \(r=3\) or 5 use Terra's complete theorem on \(\gcd(T,H_k)=1\), proved in [odd2 §§1–6][odd2-proof] by its explicit finite unit covers and congruence descents. These are declared inherited global inputs. At \(r\ge7\), (5.9) follows, and (5.2) gives a prime \(q\mid E\) whose predecessor has the odd prime factor \(r\). This contradicts (5.11). The contradiction is independent of the size of \(r\), \(k\), or \(n\).

All primes at most 19 belong to (5.11): their predecessors have no odd factors other than 3 and 5. This proves (0.2)–(0.3). The alternative \(3\nmid O\) means exactly \(n\not\equiv2,3\pmod6\). When \(v_3(k)=1\), even those two endpoint classes are allowed.

For each fixed odd \(k\) the region contains infinitely many endpoints. Since \(H_k\) is odd, choose a positive integer \(L\) with \(2^L\equiv1\pmod{H_k}\), for example its Euler period (take \(L=1\) when \(H_k=1\)). Then
\[
n=4^{Lj},\qquad j\ge1,
\]
has \(n\equiv1\pmod{H_k}\), \(n+1\equiv2\pmod{H_k}\), and \(n\equiv1\pmod3\). Its even endpoint is a power of two. No completed local field or prime-distribution theorem enters this construction.

There is a second, larger alphabet on the pure coefficient-2 part. Define \(\mathcal A_{3,5,7}\) by replacing \(\{3,5\}\) with \(\{3,5,7\}\) in (5.11). If
\[
\gcd(T,H_k)=1,\qquad
(3\nmid T\text{ or }v_3(k)=1),\qquad
\operatorname{supp}(E)\subseteq\mathcal A_{3,5,7},
\tag{5.13}
\]
then again no target exponent with an odd prime divisor is possible at \(n>1\). Here all endpoint allocations are \(O=W^r,E=2U^r\). Besides Terra's cube/fifth theorem, use its proved coefficient-2 classification \(|W^7-2U^7|=1\Longrightarrow W=U=1\), from [odd2 §2.2][odd2-proof]. Every \(r\ge11\) is excluded by (5.2). The hypotheses in (5.13) are stronger at the prime 3 than (5.12); they must not be interchanged.

The seed \(W=U=1\) on the coefficient-2 equation gives endpoints 1 and 2, hence \(n=1\), and is correctly excluded by \(n>1\). Nothing here discards the known trivial endpoint.

### 5.5. Exact Q3 residuals and the exceptional primes

This does not prove the requested all-prime theorem on Terra's *entire* region. After Terra's cube/fifth exclusion, for an alleged source at an odd prime \(r\ge7\) with \(\gcd(T,H_k)=1\), the exact alternatives left by our argument are:

1. **The odd endpoint carries the 3-debt:** \(3\mid O\) and \(v_3(k)\not\equiv1\pmod r\). Then
   \[
   O=3^bW^r,\quad E=2U^r,\quad
   2b\equiv1-v_3(k)\pmod r,\quad0<b<r,
   \]
   and \(3^bW^r-2U^r=\pm1\). The remaining power-sum equation is still required. Section 5.1 does not apply to this weighted odd endpoint.
2. **The odd endpoint is a pure power:** (5.9), together with the exact cofactor system (5.6)–(5.7). In particular \(E\) must contain a prime \(q\equiv1\pmod{2r}\) with \(q^r\mid E\) and satisfy (5.3). The alphabet theorems exclude a specified part of this set, not all such primes.
3. **Some prime of \(H_k\) divides \(T\):** retain the exceptional coefficient debts and the exact unit-polynomial strata of the supplied valuation laws. The uniform fixed-debt cover of P21 §7 is still a cover, not a classification. No stable offset is applied across an irregular collision, and no coefficient-one Catalan theorem is silently applied to unequal coefficients.

Every listed residual also retains \(S_k(n)=y^r\), or its exact integral Faulhaber lift, rather than merely the consecutive-power equation. The known unclosed exceptional \(S_{19}\) cubic branch, for example, remains unclosed by this answer. The all-\(r\) conclusion is precisely (5.12) and (5.13), not a statement about all exceptional allocations or a new complete odd square fibre.

The copied prime-certificate nodes have the same elementary acceptance proof as P21: a certified factor \(F\mid N-1\), together with modular orders containing every prime-power factor of \(F\), forces every prime divisor of \(N\) to be 1 modulo \(F\). Testing all integers \(1+jF\le\sqrt N\) then proves primality. The recursive leaves use literal trial division. At the large prime there is only one such integer to test. No probable-prime test is an acceptance step.

The general method statements behind the progress are, respectively: **shared-exponent phase intersection**, **binary prime-color forcing followed by a decreasing hypotenuse**, **finite branch-budget exclusion of rational self-transports**, and **cyclotomic cofactor transport followed by prime-alphabet incompatibility**. Each keeps its global and local obligations separate.

The most specific next targets are these:

- For even fibres, use the four affine area systems instead of exponential color enumeration, but separately prove a structural reduction of the source graph. On the concrete infinite family (3.5), the missing assertion is a correlation between the Bernoulli numerator's prime-character matrix and the Mersenne-only selection graph; (3.6) alone is not that assertion. For invisible branches failing the rank criterion, retain the exact second split (3.10) and the last witness (3.11).
- For odd squares, a proposed second-hit map should first be tested against (4.1) and (4.7). Any successful route for the four starting fibres must go beyond a fixed rational endpoint selfmap or fixed Pell shift, or establish an integer-only implication not asserted as a function-field identity. The isolated integral solutions of (4.9), outside the proved regions, are the remaining target.
- For odd target primes, the new exact marker is (5.6). A descent would have to put its smaller root \(V\) in a class with a reconstructible inverse or a provable obstruction; its small size alone does not do this. The weighted-3 odd endpoint and exceptional \(H_k\)-allocations remain explicitly separate cases.

**UNPROVED here:** infinitude of even summand exponents satisfying the complete-fibre criterion; the complete square fibres 13,17,21,25; all odd prime target exponents on the full unexceptional region; and the general Schäffer conjecture. None of those gaps is disguised as finite verification, local solubility, or a claimed impossibility of elementary methods.
