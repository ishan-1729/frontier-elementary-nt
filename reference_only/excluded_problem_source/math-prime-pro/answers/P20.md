## 0. The global step obtained

Write
\[
S_k(n)=\sum_{i=1}^n i^k,\qquad A(n)=n(n+1)(2n+1),
\]
and let \(\operatorname{sf}(a)\) denote the positive squarefree kernel of a positive integer. In particular, \(\operatorname{sf}(a)=d\) means \(a=d z^2\) for an integer \(z>0\), not that \(a\) itself is squarefree. Roots in the statements below are positive; their negatives can be adjoined for even power exponents.

There is a complete answer to opening **Q1(d) on its specified product label**. Instead of applying a different high-degree Runge bound for each \(k\), retain the third square witness in the allocation. The product label itself transports the problem to the **second moment**:
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{sf}(A(n))=6
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_2(n)\text{ is an integer square}.}
\tag{0.1}
\]
The implication is reversible. The fixed auxiliary equation can be solved by elementary descents and a moving-modulus obstruction. The high exponent \(k\) disappears from this part of the argument.

### Main theorem — PROVED

For integers \(n,k,m,y\) with \(n\ge1,k\ge1,m\ge2,y>0\), under
\[
\operatorname{sf}(n(n+1)(2n+1))=6,
\tag{0.2}
\]
the complete list of solutions of \(S_k(n)=y^m\) is
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&n=1,\quad y=1,\quad\text{every } k\ge1,m\ge2;\\
&(n,k,m,y)=(24,2,2,70),\ (24,3,2,300).
\end{aligned}}
\tag{0.3}
\]
The endpoint statement behind it is
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{sf}(n(n+1)(2n+1))=6
\iff n\in\{1,24\}\qquad(n\ge1).}
\tag{0.4}
\]
Section 5 proves (0.4), rather than citing the cannonball theorem. Section 6 proves the all-exponent classification at 24 by a complete period-32 certificate and the six-term prefix. No search cutoff in \(k\), \(m\), a Pell index, or \(n\) is assumed.

Consequently, for **every integer \(u\ge2\)**,
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{sf}(A(n))=6,\quad n>1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_{3u^2-1}(n)\ne y^m
\text{ for every }m\ge2.}
\tag{0.5}
\]
This includes all the odd \(u\ge3\) in Sol-B P10, and closes their two-Pell remainder uniformly. The same conclusion holds for \(S_{u^2-1}\) on this label for every \(u\ge3\). The \(u=2\) instance of the latter family is \(k=3\), and must not be included.

**What this is not.** It is not a full-fibre theorem for arbitrary \(n\) at infinitely many new exponents. The hypothesis (0.2) has not been forced for arbitrary solutions. Moreover, after (0.4) the label-6 endpoint set is itself finite: it should not be advertised as an infinite family of distinct admissible endpoints. What has been closed is the previously unbounded *necessary Pell cover*, with all its third-square conditions retained. The result is uniform in both exponents because the endpoint classification is independent of them.

### The reusable mechanism

There are two components worth adding to the ghost framework.

1. **Moment contraction through an allocation ledger.** A branch can supply an actual lower-degree perfect-power hit, not merely an approximation to the original hit. Solve that fixed auxiliary problem once, retaining inverse conditions, instead of repeatedly rebuilding parameter-dependent bounds.
2. **Prime-alphabet negative-return descent.** A moving modulus has an explicitly forbidden multiplicative residue class. A transported square would force every prime divisor of that modulus into a multiplicatively closed set of classes, making the modulus's own class impossible. The modulus grows, while an integer index difference strictly descends.

The second component uses a finite fifth-root calculation, proved in Section 3 using polynomial algebra over explicit rings with at most \(p^2\) elements and a cyclic count of five-tuples. Its conclusion includes the familiar special square test for 5. No quadratic reciprocity theorem, Jacobi-symbol product, or reciprocal exchange of symbols is used. This finite-field input is printed in full so its elementary content is auditable.

## 1. Allocation can transport a genuine second power witness

### 1.1. The low-moment identities

Finite expansion gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
&n(n+1)(2n+1)-(n-1)n(2n-1)=6n^2,\\
&\left(\frac{n(n+1)}2\right)^2-
 \left(\frac{(n-1)n}2\right)^2=n^3.
\end{aligned}
\]
Both expressions vanish at \(n=0\). Telescoping proves
\[
6S_2(n)=A(n),\qquad
S_3(n)=\left(\frac{n(n+1)}2\right)^2.
\tag{1.1}
\]
These are the degree-two and degree-three Bernoulli/Faulhaber identities, with a direct finite-difference proof. In particular (0.1) is an equality of integer power witnesses, not a heuristic comparison of the sizes of two sums.

### 1.2. General moment-contraction lemma — PROVED

Suppose an integer-valued auxiliary expression \(G(n)\) has an exact factorization
\[
cG(n)=\prod_{i=1}^r A_i(n),\qquad c\in\mathbb Z_{>0}.
\tag{1.2}
\]
Let a covering branch of the original problem carry integers \(z_i>0,d_i>0\) with
\[
A_i(n)=d_i z_i^e,\qquad e\ge2.
\]
Assume the branch's scalar ledger proves
\[
\frac{\prod_i d_i}{c}=b^e\quad\text{for a specified }b\in\mathbb Q_{>0}.
\tag{1.3}
\]
Then
\[
G(n)=\left(b\prod_i z_i\right)^e
\]
is an integer \(e\)-th power. Indeed, the displayed root is rational, and a rational number whose positive integer power is an integer is itself integral: in lowest terms, its denominator's \(e\)-th power would divide its numerator's \(e\)-th power.

Suppose, additionally, a separate elementary proof classifies every integer endpoint of this auxiliary equation in a finite list \(E\). Then every original solution in the branch has \(n\in E\). Rechecking the original equation at every endpoint in \(E\) is a complete final step. An auxiliary point need not lift back automatically; the retained original parameters and all inverse filters must be tested.

**Proof of completeness.** The stipulated covering map supplies (1.2)–(1.3) for every original solution. The displayed root proves membership in the auxiliary equation. Its complete classification therefore contains the original endpoint. Conversely, final substitution tests accept only genuine original solutions. No additional endpoint is lost by using a necessary auxiliary cover. This proves the compiler statement.

The lemma does **not** assert that the scalar relation (1.3) is forced on every covering branch, or that every auxiliary equation has a finite elementary classification.

### 1.3. Application to the three anchors

The positive integers \(n,n+1,2n+1\) are pairwise coprime. Their canonical square allocations are
\[
n=d_1 a^2,\qquad n+1=d_2 b^2,\qquad 2n+1=d_3 c^2,
\tag{1.4}
\]
with positive squarefree, pairwise coprime \(d_i\). Thus
\[
d_1d_2d_3=\operatorname{sf}(A(n)).
\]
When this product is 6, the new witness is explicit:
\[
\boxed{S_2(n)=(abc)^2.}
\tag{1.5}
\]
This is the promised second discrete hit. It comes from the **whole allocation**, including the third anchor. If only the first two equations of (1.4) are retained, one has a Pell superset, not (1.5).

In ghost language, the state retains \((k,m,n,y,d_1,d_2,d_3,a,b,c)\). The edge to the auxiliary moment adds the exact witness \(z=abc\) and the equation \(S_2(n)=z^2\); it does not discard \(k,m,y\). Its inverse acceptance is substitution in the original sum. This is a change of equation with a power witness, not the false assertion that a smaller prefix sum is automatically a perfect power.

The branching condition is essential. For example,
\[
S_5(13)=1001^2,
\qquad\operatorname{sf}(13\cdot14\cdot27)=546\ne6.
\]
No unconditional replacement of (1.3) by an arbitrary power-sum hypothesis is being made.

## 2. Two elementary descents for the even endpoint branch

All factor allocations in this section use ordinary unique factorization in \(\mathbb Z\). No quadratic-order factorization is required.

### 2.1. A positive integer right triangle cannot have square area — PROVED

Suppose one exists, and choose one with least positive hypotenuse. It is primitive. Otherwise divide its sides by their common gcd \(g>1\). The smaller integer triangle has area equal to a rational square, namely the old square area divided by \(g^2\); its area is an integer, so that rational square root is integral. This contradicts minimality.

A primitive right triangle has one even leg. Write its legs and hypotenuse as
\[
r^2-s^2,\quad2rs,\quad r^2+s^2,
\]
where \(r>s>0\), \(\gcd(r,s)=1\), and \(r,s\) have opposite parity. To justify this parametrization, choose the odd leg \(x\), the even leg \(y\), and the hypotenuse \(z\). The two coprime positive integers \((z+x)/2,(z-x)/2\) have product \((y/2)^2\); they must be squares. This gives the displayed parametrization and its coprimality/parity conditions.

The area is
\[
rs(r-s)(r+s).
\]
Its four factors are pairwise coprime: the only possible extra common divisor of \(r-s\) and \(r+s\) is 2, and both are odd. Since their product is a square, there are positive integers \(a,b,c,d\) with
\[
r=a^2,\quad s=b^2,\quad r-s=c^2,\quad r+s=d^2.
\]
Here \(c,d\) are odd, \(d>c\), and
\[
d^2+c^2=2a^2,\qquad d^2-c^2=2b^2.
\]
Odd squares are 1 modulo 8, so the second identity makes \(b\) even. The positive integers
\[
\frac{d+c}{2},\quad\frac{d-c}{2},\quad a
\]
now form a right triangle, with area
\[
\frac{d^2-c^2}{8}=(b/2)^2.
\]
Its hypotenuse \(a\) is smaller than the old \(r^2+s^2=a^4+b^4\). This contradicts the chosen minimum. ∎

The primitive reduction above uses the fact that an integer right triangle has integer area: both odd legs are impossible modulo 4, so one leg is even.

### 2.2. The equation \(X^2=2Y^4+1\) — PROVED

Its nonnegative solutions have \(Y=0,X=1\).

Suppose \(Y>0\), and choose a solution with the least \(X>0\). Since \(X\) is odd, write \(X=2s+1\), where \(s\ge1\). Then
\[
Y^4=2s(s+1).
\tag{2.1}
\]
If \(s\) is odd, the coprime factors \(s\) and \(2(s+1)\) are fourth powers:
\[
s=u^4,\qquad2(s+1)=v^4.
\]
Here \(u\) is odd and \(v\) even, so \(v^4=2(u^4+1)\) is 4 modulo 16, a contradiction.

If \(s\) is even, the coprime factors \(2s,s+1\) are fourth powers. Write
\[
2s=u^4,\quad s+1=v^4,\quad u=2w,
\]
with \(w\ge1\) and \(v>1\) odd. Thus \(s=8w^4\) and \(v^4=8w^4+1\). Put
\[
a=(v^2-1)/2.
\]
This is a positive even integer, and
\[
a(a+1)=(v^4-1)/4=2w^4.
\]
Coprimality now gives
\[
a=2b^4,\quad a+1=c^4
\]
for positive integers \(b,c\). Hence
\[
(c^2)^2=2b^4+1
\]
is another solution of the original equation. Its first coordinate is smaller:
\[
c^2=\sqrt{(v^2+1)/2}<v<s+1<2s+1=X.
\]
All comparisons follow by squaring positive quantities or comparing integer powers. This contradicts minimality. ∎

### 2.3. The equation \(X^2=8Y^4+1\) — PROVED

Its nonnegative solutions have \((X,Y)=(1,0),(3,1)\).

For a positive solution write \(X=2s+1\); then
\[
s(s+1)=2Y^4.
\]
If \(s\) is even, coprimality gives \(s=2u^4,s+1=v^4\). Therefore \((v^2)^2=2u^4+1\), and Section 2.2 forces \(u=0\), the zero case.

If \(s\) is odd, write \(s=u^4,s+1=2v^4\). Both \(u,v\) are positive odd integers and
\[
u^4+1=2v^4.
\tag{2.2}
\]
If \(u>1\), the positive integers
\[
v^2-u,\quad v^2+u,\quad u^2+1
\]
form a right triangle, because
\[
(v^2-u)^2+(v^2+u)^2
=2v^4+2u^2=(u^2+1)^2.
\]
Its area is the integer square
\[
\frac{v^4-u^2}{2}=\left(\frac{u^2-1}{2}\right)^2.
\]
The first leg is positive since \(v^4-u^2=(u^2-1)^2/2>0\). This contradicts Section 2.1. Consequently \(u=v=1\), giving \(s=1,Y=1,X=3\). Both stated endpoints work. ∎

These are complete descents, not searches for fourth powers. Their terminal possibilities are explicitly resolved.

## 3. A prime-factor-pattern obstruction from finite fifth roots

This section replaces the reciprocity step that would otherwise enter a classical shifted-Pell proof. Everything concerns finite sets, finite polynomial arithmetic, and integer prime factors.

### 3.1. Finite algebra used below

For an odd prime \(p\), the nonzero squares modulo \(p\) number \((p-1)/2\): two nonzero elements have the same square exactly when they are negatives of each other. In particular a nonsquare \(d\) exists. The pairs
\[
a+b\theta,\quad a,b\in\mathbb F_p,\qquad\theta^2=d
\]
form a field with \(p^2\) elements. The inverse of a nonzero pair is
\[
(a+b\theta)^{-1}=\frac{a-b\theta}{a^2-db^2}.
\]
The denominator cannot be zero unless both coordinates are zero, because otherwise \(d\) would be a square. Thus this assertion about a quadratic field is an explicit finite calculation.

In any finite field with \(q\) elements, multiplication by a nonzero element permutes all \(q-1\) nonzero elements. Multiplying and cancelling their nonzero product proves
\[
x^{q-1}=1\quad(x\ne0).
\tag{3.1}
\]
A degree-\(d\) nonzero polynomial over a field has at most \(d\) roots, by successive division by linear factors. The binomial coefficients \(\binom pi\), \(0<i<p\), are divisible by \(p\). Thus \((x+y)^p=x^p+y^p\), and in a field of characteristic \(p\) the roots of \(X^p-X\) are exactly its embedded \(p\) elements of \(\mathbb F_p\).

We also need a special finite group-count fact. If a finite abelian group \(G\) has order divisible by 5, it contains a nonidentity element \(g\) with \(g^5=1\). Indeed, there are \(|G|^4\) ordered five-tuples with product 1. Cyclic rotation partitions them into orbits of size 1 or 5. The fixed tuples are precisely \((g,g,g,g,g)\) with \(g^5=1\). Their number is divisible by 5, and at least one exists. Therefore at least five exist, and one is nonidentity. This argument proves the needed existence without assuming that a finite field's multiplicative group is cyclic.

### 3.2. Direct fifth-root test — PROVED

For every odd prime \(p\ne5\),
\[
5\text{ is a square modulo }p
\iff p\equiv1\text{ or }4\pmod5.
\tag{3.2}
\]

**Necessity.** From a square root of 5 obtain
\[
w=(-1+\sqrt5)/2\in\mathbb F_p,
\qquad w^2+w-1=0.
\]
The polynomial \(Z^2-wZ+1\) has a root \(\zeta\) either in \(\mathbb F_p\) or in a field of \(p^2\) elements. In the irreducible case this is precisely the quadratic construction just proved, with the nonsquare discriminant; equivalently take the quotient by that irreducible polynomial.

The root is nonzero and \(\zeta+\zeta^{-1}=w\). Multiplying the equation for \(w\) by \(\zeta^2\) gives
\[
\zeta^4+\zeta^3+\zeta^2+\zeta+1=0.
\]
Therefore \(\zeta^5=1\). It is not 1, because then the last displayed sum would equal \(5\ne0\). Its multiplicative order is 5. By (3.1) that order divides \(p^2-1\) (also in the smaller-field case). Hence \(p\equiv\pm1\pmod5\).

**Sufficiency.** If \(p\equiv1\pmod5\), the group-count argument gives an element \(\zeta\ne1\), \(\zeta^5=1\), in \(\mathbb F_p\). If \(p\equiv-1\pmod5\), use the explicitly constructed field of \(p^2\) elements and the same argument there. In the latter case
\[
\zeta^p=\zeta^{-1}.
\]
In either case \(w=\zeta+\zeta^{-1}\) belongs to \(\mathbb F_p\): in the quadratic case \(w^p=w\), and Section 3.1 identifies all such elements. The finite geometric identity for \(\zeta^5-1\) gives \(w^2+w-1=0\). Consequently
\[
(2w+1)^2=5.
\]
This proves (3.2). ∎

The construction is over finite rings; \(\sqrt5\) in the necessity argument denotes a hypothesized residue root, not a real or complex analytic function. No general reciprocity law is a dependency.

### 3.3. The required alphabet modulo 20

We first recall, with proof, the square test for \(-1\). At a prime \(p\equiv3\pmod4\), a square root of \(-1\) contradicts (3.1) after raising its square to \((p-1)/2\). At \(p\equiv1\pmod4\), pair each nonzero element with its inverse. Only \(1,-1\) are self-inverse, so \((p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p\). On the other hand
\[
(p-1)!\equiv(-1)^{(p-1)/2}\left(((p-1)/2)!\right)^2\pmod p.
\]
Here \((p-1)/2\) is even, and this supplies a square root of \(-1\).

**Lemma — PROVED.** If an odd prime \(p\ne5\) admits a square root of \(-5\), then
\[
p\bmod20\in\mathcal H:=\{1,3,7,9\}.
\tag{3.3}
\]
If \(p\equiv1\pmod4\), divide the square root of \(-5\) by one of \(-1\); this makes 5 a square, so (3.2) gives \(p\equiv\pm1\pmod5\). These simultaneous conditions give the classes 1,9 modulo 20.

If \(p\equiv3\pmod4\), 5 cannot also be square, or division would make \(-1\) square. By (3.2), \(p\equiv\pm2\pmod5\). The simultaneous classes are 3,7 modulo 20. This proves (3.3).

The set \(\mathcal H\) is closed under multiplication modulo 20: it consists of the four powers \(1,3,9,7\) of 3. Therefore:

**Composite obstruction — PROVED.** If \(Q>0\), \(Q\equiv17\pmod{20}\), then \(-5\) is not a square modulo \(Q\). If, in addition, 2 is a square modulo \(Q\), then \(-10\) is not a square modulo \(Q\).

For the first assertion, a square root would give a root modulo every prime divisor of \(Q\). None of these primes is 2 or 5. By (3.3) all belong to \(\mathcal H\), so their product with multiplicities, \(Q\), also belongs to \(\mathcal H\), a contradiction. For the second, a square root of 2 is a unit modulo odd \(Q\); dividing a proposed square root of \(-10\) by it would give a square root of \(-5\).

Primality of \(Q\) is unnecessary. Nor can the hypothesis about 2 simply be omitted from the second assertion: \(Q=37\) is 17 modulo 20, but \(8^2\equiv-10\pmod{37}\).

## 4. A moving-modulus global separator on the Pell orbit

### 4.1. Coverage, not just a generation formula

Define
\[
(U_0,V_0)=(1,0),\qquad
(U_{j+1},V_{j+1})=(2U_j+3V_j,U_j+2V_j).
\tag{4.1}
\]
Every positive integer solution of
\[
U^2-3V^2=1,\qquad U>0,V\ge0,
\tag{4.2}
\]
is exactly one of these pairs.

For \(V>1\), the inverse coordinates
\[
U'=2U-3V,\qquad V'=2V-U
\]
are positive integers: \(3/2<U/V<2\), as follows by squaring from \(U^2=3V^2+1\). Also \(U>V\), so \(V'<V\). Direct expansion preserves (4.2). At \(V=1\), \(U=2\) and the inverse is \((1,0)\); at \(V=0\), \(U=1\). The positive integer \(V\) decreases until these terminal cases. Reversing the steps proves coverage. The forward \(V_j\) strictly increases, proving uniqueness as well.

Set
\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Finite multiplication proves
\[
M^j=\begin{pmatrix}U_j&3V_j\\V_j&U_j\end{pmatrix},\qquad
U_{-j}=U_j,
\tag{4.3}
\]
where negative indices use the integral inverse. The two-by-two characteristic identity, verified by expanding four entries, gives
\[
M^{2h}-2U_hM^h+I=0,
\qquad U_{2h}=2U_h^2-1.
\tag{4.4}
\]

### 4.2. The modulus has an explicit forbidden factor pattern

For \(s\ge1\), put
\[
h_s=2^s,\qquad Q_s=U_{h_s}.
\]
Then
\[
Q_1=7,\qquad Q_{s+1}=2Q_s^2-1.
\tag{4.5}
\]
In particular \(Q_s>1\) and
\[
Q_s\equiv17\pmod{20}\quad(s\ge2),
\tag{4.6}
\]
since \(Q_2=97\) and \(2\cdot17^2-1\equiv17\pmod{20}\).
Moreover
\[
\boxed{
M^{2h_s}\equiv-I\pmod{Q_s},\qquad
(2U_{h_s/2})^2\equiv2\pmod{Q_s}.}
\tag{4.7}
\]
The first is (4.4); the second is \(Q_s=2U_{h_s/2}^2-1\). Thus Section 3.3 proves, at every \(s\ge2\), that \(-10\) is not a square modulo \(Q_s\). This is an all-index consequence of a recurrence, not a finite list of primes to sieve.

### 4.3. Complete shifted-square isolation — PROVED

For integers \(j\ge0,a\ge0\),
\[
\boxed{U_j=4a^2+3\iff (j,a)=(2,1).}
\tag{4.8}
\]
The scalar recurrence is \(U_{j+2}=4U_{j+1}-U_j\). Its values modulo 8 are the repeating list
\[
1,2,7,2.
\]
One verifies the four entries and the return of the ordered recurrence pair; induction gives the period for every index. The target \(4a^2+3\) is 3 or 7 modulo 8, so a hit must have \(a\) odd and \(j\equiv2\pmod4\).

Suppose \(j\ne2\). Choose \(\rho\in\{2,-2\}\) with \(8\mid j-\rho\). This is possible in the two classes 2,6 modulo 8. The difference is nonzero. Write
\[
j-\rho=2^{s+1}w=2h_sw,
\quad s\ge2,\quad w\text{ odd}.
\tag{4.9}
\]
The odd integer \(w\) may be negative. By (4.7) and the integral inverse,
\[
M^j\equiv-M^\rho\pmod{Q_s},
\qquad U_j\equiv-U_\rho=-7\pmod{Q_s}.
\]
The assumed hit would give
\[
(2a)^2\equiv-10\pmod{Q_s},
\]
contradicting Section 4.2. At \(j=2\), \(U_2=7\), and the target is equivalent to \(a=1\). ∎

### 4.4. The decreasing ghost rank

For an alleged hit in a class anchored at \(\rho\), initialize
\[
(w,h,Q)=((j-\rho)/8,4,97).
\]
If \(w=0\), the index is the tested anchor. For nonzero even \(w\), make the move
\[
(w,h,Q)\longmapsto(w/2,2h,2Q^2-1).
\tag{4.10}
\]
The invariant is \(j-\rho=2hw\), with \(Q=U_h\). The power witness remains in the ledger. The positive integer \(|w|\) strictly decreases at every nonterminal step. At an odd \(w\), the negative-return congruence gives the forbidden square. This is termination of a strategy on the actual integer difference, not the unjustified assumption that a growing modulus is a decreasing rank.

The separator tests a square condition at a single unknown point. It does not require neighbouring unknown square hits, a bound on the Pell index, or a logarithmic estimate.

## 5. Complete classification of the three-anchor equation

### 5.1. The two allocation branches

Suppose
\[
A(n)=6z^2,\qquad n,z>0.
\tag{5.1}
\]
Pairwise coprimality of \(n,n+1,2n+1\) means that primes other than 2 and 3 occur to even exponents in each individual factor. The full odd exponent at 2 belongs to the even member of \(n,n+1\), and the full odd exponent at 3 belongs to exactly one anchor.

If \(n\) is even, both \(n+1\) and \(2n+1\) are either a square or three times a square. Their residues modulo 3 belong to \(\{0,1\}\). The first excludes \(n\equiv1\pmod3\), the second excludes \(n\equiv2\pmod3\). Hence \(3\mid n\), and the exact allocation is
\[
\boxed{n=6a^2,\quad n+1=b^2,\quad2n+1=c^2.}
\tag{5.2}
\]

If \(n\) is odd, it is a square or three times a square, so \(n\not\equiv2\pmod3\). Its successor is twice a square or six times a square, so \(n+1\not\equiv1\pmod3\). Thus \(n\equiv1\pmod3\), and
\[
\boxed{n=a^2,\quad n+1=2b^2,\quad2n+1=3c^2.}
\tag{5.3}
\]
All roots here are positive integers. These deductions exhaust all allocations, including the prime-exponent shifts at 2 and 3.

### 5.2. Even endpoints

From (5.2), \(b,c\) are odd and coprime, and
\[
c^2-b^2=6a^2.
\]
Odd squares are 1 modulo 8, so \(a\) is even; write \(a=2t\). The positive integers
\[
r=(c+b)/2,\qquad s=(c-b)/2
\]
are coprime and satisfy \(rs=6t^2\). Allocating the only odd-exponent primes 2 and 3 leaves, in either order, just two shapes:
\[
\{r,s\}=\{6R^2,S^2\}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\{r,s\}=\{3R^2,2S^2\},
\quad R,S>0.
\tag{5.4}
\]
In both cases \(t=RS\) and \(n=24R^2S^2\).

In the first case \(b^2=(6R^2-S^2)^2=n+1\), so
\[
(6R^2-3S^2)^2-8S^4=1.
\]
Section 2.3 makes \(S=1\); then \((6R^2-3)^2=9\), and positivity makes \(R=1\). Therefore \(n=24\).

In the second case \(b^2=(3R^2-2S^2)^2=n+1\), so
\[
(3R^2-6S^2)^2-2(2S)^4=1.
\]
Section 2.2 rules this out, since \(S>0\). This proves that 24 is the only positive even endpoint of (5.1).

### 5.3. Odd endpoints: the third square creates the shifted-Pell mark

From (5.3),
\[
a^2+1=2b^2,\qquad2a^2+1=3c^2,
\qquad3c^2+1=4b^2.
\]
Define the actual integer coordinates
\[
U=6c^2+1=4a^2+3,\qquad V=4bc.
\tag{5.5}
\]
Then
\[
U^2-3V^2
=1+12c^2(3c^2+1-4b^2)=1.
\tag{5.6}
\]
By the covered orbit in Section 4.1, \((U,V)=(U_j,V_j)\) for an integer \(j\ge0\). Crucially the **same** integer \(U\) has the additional mark \(U=4a^2+3\). Section 4.3 gives \(U=7,a=1\), so \(n=1\). The recovered point has \(b=c=1,V=4\), and all original equations are satisfied.

This is the required second-hit transport on this branch. Dropping \(c^2=(2n+1)/3\) would lose (5.5)–(5.6); a gap between successive terms of the earlier \(\sqrt2\)-Pell sequence would not have supplied the shifted-square mark. The projection above is used as a necessary cover; its sole terminal point is explicitly reconstructed, so no unproved inverse integrality assertion is needed.

Finally \(A(1)=6\) and \(A(24)=6\cdot70^2\). Sections 5.1–5.3 prove both directions of (0.4). The finite terminal cases are \(Y=0,1\) in the quartic descents and \(j=2\) in the shifted-Pell test. There is no unexamined interval at large \(n\).

## 6. Complete all-k, all-m exhaustion at n = 24

### 6.1. A proved exponent period

For every odd integer \(a\), \(a^2\equiv1\pmod8\). Repeated squaring gives \(a^{32}\equiv1\pmod{128}\). For an even summand \(i\), \(i^k\equiv0\pmod{128}\) when \(k\ge7\). Therefore \(S_k(24)\pmod{128}\) is periodic in \(k\) with period 32 on \(k\ge7\).

The code checks every one of the 32 classes, by computing the exact sums at \(k=32+r\), \(0\le r<32\). These congruences are consistent with Sol-A's Bernoulli unit law, but the accepted table needs only the elementary periods just proved.

## 7. Generalizing the ghost mechanism

### 7.1. Multiplicative prime alphabets — PROVED

Let \(m_0\ge2\), let \(H\) be a multiplicatively closed subset of the units modulo \(m_0\) containing 1, and let \(Q>1\) be coprime to \(m_0\). Suppose a proposed arithmetic mark modulo \(Q\) implies
\[
p\mid Q\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p\bmod m_0\in H
\tag{7.1}
\]
for every prime divisor \(p\). Then \(Q\bmod m_0\in H\).

**Proof.** Factor the particular positive integer \(Q\) into ordinary primes, with multiplicities. Every factor belongs to \(H\), and closure preserves membership under their finite product. ∎

Thus a separately proved residue \(Q\bmod m_0\notin H\) excludes the mark. This is a factor-pattern squeeze: the forced factor alphabet has no product in the actual residue class. It is not a statistical assertion about prime densities, nor does it require factoring the growing \(Q\) in the certificate.

Section 3 supplies \(m_0=20,H=\{1,3,7,9\}\), and the mark is a square root of \(-5\). An auxiliary square root of 2 transports the square root of \(-10\) into this mark.

### 7.2. Moving-modulus isolation — PROVED compiler lemma

Let \(M\in\mathrm{GL}_d(\mathbb Z)\). Suppose a sequence of positive integer moduli \(Q_s\) and integers \(h_s=2^s\), for \(s\ge s_0\), has exact certificates
\[
M^{2h_s}\equiv-I\pmod{Q_s}.
\tag{7.2}
\]
Let a finite set of anchors \(\mathcal R\subset\mathbb Z\) cover the possible index classes modulo \(2^{s_0+1}\). At a candidate index \(j\), choose \(\rho\in\mathcal R\) with \(j\equiv\rho\pmod{2^{s_0+1}}\).

Assume the following uniform statement has been proved: the arithmetic mark at \(M^jv\), when transported through
\[
M^jv\equiv-M^\rho v\pmod{Q_s},
\]
would force a prime alphabet as in (7.1), whereas the recurrence certificate for \(Q_s\) places it outside that alphabet. Then there is no marked index \(j\ne\rho\) in this anchor class.

**Proof.** Write \(j-\rho=2h_sw\) with \(w\) odd; the congruence-class hypothesis gives \(s\ge s_0\). Equation (7.2), including negative odd powers, gives the displayed negative return. Its forbidden terminal mark is a contradiction. Equivalently, divide the nonzero integer \((j-\rho)/2^{s_0+1}\) by 2 until it is odd; its absolute value decreases at each step. This proves termination and complete coverage of the non-anchor indices in that class. ∎

Every anchor still needs an exact test. Every index class not covered by the hypotheses remains unclassified. The prime-alphabet statement is a substantive arithmetic input, not something automatically supplied by a matrix recurrence.

In Section 4, a scalar period modulo 8 supplies the two anchor classes represented by \(\rho=2,-2\), and \(s_0=2\). The single unknown square hit supplies the forbidden terminal residue; no second unknown hit is assumed.

### 7.3. An infinite trace-family evaluation of the same alphabet

The following shows that the terminal alphabet is not tied to one numerical Pell equation.

**PROVED.** Let \(a\ge2\) be even with \(a\equiv2\) or \(3\pmod5\). Define
\[
R_0=1,\quad R_1=a,\quad R_{j+2}=2aR_{j+1}-R_j.
\]
For every positive multiple \(j\) of 8,
\[
\boxed{R_j-9\text{ is not a square}.}
\tag{7.3}
\]

Use
\[
M_a=\begin{pmatrix}a&a^2-1\\1&a\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det M_a=1.
\]
Its powers have equal diagonal entries \(R_j\), and the same finite characteristic calculation proves
\[
R_{2h}=2R_h^2-1,\qquad
M_a^{2h}\equiv-I\pmod{R_h}.
\]
Put \(Q_s=R_{2^s}\). Since \(a\) is even and \(a^2\equiv4\pmod5\),
\[
Q_1=2a^2-1\equiv3\pmod4,\quad Q_1\equiv2\pmod5.
\]
The recurrence gives \(Q_s\equiv17\pmod{20}\) for \(s\ge2\), and \((2R_{2^{s-1}})^2\equiv2\pmod{Q_s}\).

Write the positive \(j\) as \(2^{s+1}w\), \(w\) odd, \(s\ge2\). The negative return gives \(R_j\equiv-1\pmod{Q_s}\). A square \(R_j-9\) would therefore be \(-10\) modulo \(Q_s\), impossible by Section 3.3. This proves (7.3) at every such index. It asserts no classification of the other index classes.

### 7.4. Where the first and second mechanisms join

The moment-contraction lemma supplies a **new exact equation** on an eligible allocation branch. A decreasing inverse proves coverage of that equation by an explicit orbit. The moving-modulus lemma then isolates its additional square mark. Finally, terminal values of the original parameter family are checked by a finite exponent certificate.

Schematically, in this answer the actual implications are
\[
\begin{gathered}
\text{original power witness + label 6}
\ \Longrightarrow\ S_2(n)=z^2\\
\Longrightarrow\
\begin{cases}
\text{two fourth-power descents},&n\text{ even},\\
U_j=4a^2+3\text{ on a completely covered Pell orbit},&n\text{ odd},
\end{cases}\\
\Longrightarrow\ n\in\{1,24\}
\Longrightarrow\ \text{the exact list (0.3).}
\end{gathered}
\]
The two branches have different terminal mechanisms. Neither treats a failed local test as a global cover, and neither promotes a bounded numerical orbit experiment to an all-index theorem.

## 8. Answers to the other openings and exact residual sets

### 8.1. The Bernoulli envelope still needs an arithmetic rejecter

Sol-A D2–D3 prove finite signed midpoint bounds. Their one-candidate conclusion requires the **width test D10**, in addition to positivity; \(n\ge k\) by itself is used there to prove positivity and control the truncations. It should not be silently substituted for the displayed root-width condition.

Once an interval contains at most one possible integer root, a valuation or unit law can reject that particular candidate. This is useful computationally. It does not give the missing uniform conclusion that the candidate always has the wrong unit. The unrestricted candidate may be in an allowed residue class, and the packets provide no theorem making that impossible. Section 6 succeeds because the global contraction has already reduced the endpoint to 24; the finite arithmetic of that endpoint is completely periodic in \(k\) above a proved threshold.

No new assertion here says that Bernoulli coefficient sign alternation forces every unrestricted candidate to fail. Increasing the number of finite envelope terms without such an arithmetic implication narrows a real interval, but does not add a second power witness.

### 8.2. What is now closed uniformly in the two-Pell problem

Sol-B P10.3 kept two of the three anchor squares and therefore obtained two infinite Pell supersets. On the actual product label, their third square conditions are not optional. Keeping those conditions supplies Sections 5.2–5.3 and leaves only \(n=1,24\), independently of \(u\).

\boxed{n>1,\quad m\ge2,\quad\operatorname{sf}(A(n))\ne6,}

together with all previously proved restrictions. This is not a proof that the set is empty.

For \(k=u^2-1,u\ge3\), the same residual condition holds. The already completed individual fibres stay completed by their existing proofs; this answer does not replace their all-branch allocation work with an unproved uniform assertion.

### 8.3. Exact residual for the general problem

Within the set (0.2), there is no residual: (0.3) is complete for every \(k,m\). Outside it, this answer supplies no new full endpoint classification. In particular, any still-unknown nontrivial nonexceptional solution must have

### 8.4. Why the summation identities have not become descents by renaming them

Reflection carries the same polynomial value to a negative endpoint; it does not supply another positive hit of the kind required by an equally spaced-hit theorem. Pascal and binomial inversion preserve linear relations between sums, not the individual perfect-power marks. A Pell move preserving only the first two equations in (1.4) need not preserve the third equation. These distinctions are retained, not bypassed.

What works here is different: the multiplicative scalar relation in (1.3) is exact and produces the explicit integer root \(abc\) of a lower moment. A subsequent transformation, (5.5), retains two simultaneous square equations and produces the shifted-square condition on the covered \(\sqrt3\) orbit. These are the actual power-witness transports used. They do not establish a same-equation descent for unrestricted \(S_k\).
