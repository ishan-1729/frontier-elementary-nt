## 0. The change of emphasis, and the strongest conclusions

I agree with the correction of emphasis. The object to build is a theory of **certified arithmetic orbit intersections**. Power sums, Fibonacci equations, Thue equations and hyperelliptic curves are evaluations of that theory. A method that settles another isolated equation without improving coverage, observables, local lifting, or global termination is a useful application, but is not automatically a theoretical advance.

Here is my assessment of the design.

1. **Keep covers, explicit orbits, arithmetic properties, descent and gluing as separate proof obligations.** Add a certificate for the kernel/stabilizer of the orbit parametrization and for the way every change of coordinates transports the actual arithmetic property.
2. **Replace the proposed rational-leading-term dividing line.** It is false as a necessary-and-sufficient criterion. Dominance, signs, conjugate norms, rational-lattice separation, and Padé determinants are different elementary Archimedean mechanisms. Several competing exponentials do not logically force the use of Baker's theorem.
3. **Keep the module and symmetry idea, with an explicit jet hypothesis.** An irreducible finite-field norm can isolate arbitrarily many ghost coordinates with one sufficiently high-degree singular marker. Equivariance alone does not make a marker's leading form a norm.
4. **Replace the universal tau conjecture by a return-chart and gauge theorem.** For homogeneous bilinear recurrences there is an exact quadratic gauge identity, and a finite return certificate gives first- and second-order valuation formulas. This is proved below. Laurentness helps make returns regular; it does not prove primitive divisors.
5. **Keep parametric ghosts, but distinguish parameter specialization from a new group index.** Below I prove, for every integer a>=16, a complete two-unit parametrization of the units of the explicit order in Thomas's cubic family, without the unit theorem. That is a genuine uniform ghost-coverage theorem. It is not a proof that the remaining one-marker equation has only trivial solutions.

The most useful new statements in this answer are:

- a finite Archimedean/congruence covering theorem with the missing global hypothesis stated explicitly;
- a valuation-initial-form theorem and a norm-leading isolation theorem in arbitrarily many ghost variables;
- an exact gauge identity and local lifting theorem for bilinear tau recurrences, including a construction that tracks raw tau values correctly;
- an elementary uniform unit-generation theorem for Thomas's family;
- an elementary determinant approximation lemma that supplies a second Archimedean engine beyond ordinary Runge.

## 1. Q1: the natural mathematical home

### 1.1. An arithmetic orbit presentation

DEFINITION. Fix an explicit arithmetic ring R, such as Z, Z[1/S], or an order with a specified finite Z-basis and multiplication table. Let X be a solution variety, together with specified integrality, sign, and other arithmetic conditions on its points.

An arithmetic orbit presentation consists of the following data.

**Cover data.** A finite list of maps pi_d:Y_d -> X and a proof that every admissible point of X lifts to at least one Y_d with explicitly controlled denominators. Ramification, exceptional fibers and points outside the displayed affine charts must be included in that proof.

**Orbit data.** For each d, a finitely generated group Gamma_d with explicit generators, relations, a computable action on an ambient space U_d, and finitely many seeds z_(d,j). In the principal commutative setting the group is a finite torsion group times Z^r. The orbit map is

    theta_(d,j)(gamma)=gamma.z_(d,j).

There must be a proof that every relevant lifted point belongs to one of these specified orbits. It is not enough that a computation found several plausible generators.

**Property data.** An exact predicate P_(d,j)(gamma) expressing which orbit points are admissible lifts. It may involve polynomial equalities, inequalities, valuations, integrality, membership in a power class, or the existence of specified algebraic witnesses. The predicate must be transported exactly through the cover and every normalization.

**Effectivity data.** Explicit evaluation formulas, denominator controls, finite local models, and an exhaustive way to treat bounded height. These are what turn a geometric description into a proof that can be checked by integer arithmetic.

The solution set is then the image of

    {gamma in Gamma_d : P_(d,j)(gamma)}

over the finitely many d,j. Equality of this image with the desired solution set is the coverage assertion. If different indices represent the same point, record the stabilizer or work modulo it. A locally invisible kernel can otherwise produce false claims about isolation of indices.

A proof of finite generation of the entire ambient unit group or rational-point group is not part of this definition. Under your rules it must be replaced, where needed, by an explicit coverage argument for the particular subgroup and particular points that occur.

### 1.2. How an expert would recognize it

BELIEF ABOUT TERMINOLOGY. In the commutative algebraic setting, the natural home is effective integral orbit-intersection on torsors and subvarieties of algebraic groups, especially tori, abelian varieties and their extensions. The familiar surrounding theories are S-unit equations, integral Mordell–Lang, descent on covers, Skolem-type interpolation, Chabauty and the Mordell–Weil sieve. The dynamical formulation also accommodates explicitly invertible recurrences without first identifying an abelian variety.

The adelic orbit closure is a useful organizing space, but an intersection with that closure is a necessary local condition, not automatically a characterization of the original integer orbit. This distinction is central in the classical sieve as well: see [Bruin–Stoll, The Mordell–Weil sieve](https://www.mathe2.uni-bayreuth.de/stoll/papers/MWsieve.pdf), especially Sections 2–4. I am using that theory for orientation, not invoking its global hypotheses in an elementary proof.

Your distinctive contribution should be sought in **explicit coverage certificates and a reusable certificate language**, not in claiming that orbit-intersection or p-adic interpolation is new. It would be valuable to make a proof consist of independently checkable pieces: multiplication identities, bounded reductions, rational recurrence identities, congruence tables, initial forms, and exact real inequalities.

### 1.3. What a ghost variable is, and is not

The primary ghost is a discrete arithmetic coordinate: an element of Gamma or of a known quotient of Gamma. For commutative finitely generated Gamma, its underlying coordinates are still finitely many integers and finite torsion data.

Additional structure can be substantial:

- An action of an order O on Gamma records endomorphisms, complex multiplication, or Galois symmetries. It can force useful identities and organize norm forms.
- A Z[G]-module structure can separate invariant, anti-invariant, and relative-norm directions. One must still prove that the proposed decomposition and the relevant integral indices are correct.
- A profinite completion or p-adic Lie closure is a **testing space** for the discrete ghost. Its points need not be actual integer indices.
- A point of a parameter or moduli space is a **base parameter**, unless a separate group action supplies an orbit interpretation. Calling it another ghost index does not provide the missing group law or additional equations.

There are three different dimensions to record: the Z-rank of Gamma, the dimension of its p-adic analytic image, and the dimension of the ambient algebraic group. They need not coincide.

PROVED LOCAL LIMITATION. Suppose the pullbacks of k analytic markers to an r-dimensional p-adic chart have Jacobian rank k at a zero, with k<r. The p-adic implicit-function theorem gives a local zero set of dimension r−k, so that zero is not isolated in the p-adic chart. This does not prove the existence of another integer ghost on it: a positive-dimensional p-adic locus may have very few integer points. It proves that a certificate asserting isolation of the whole p-adic germ cannot work there.

### 1.4. Completion is a hypothesis, not an automatic construction

PROVED COUNTEREXAMPLE. The orbit n -> p^n in Q_p does not extend continuously from nonnegative integer n to Z_p. The indices n_j=p^j tend to 0 p-adically, but p^(p^j) tends to 0 rather than to p^0=1.

Thus “for every place the orbit extends to the completion of Gamma” is false without a compactness/unit hypothesis. For multiplicative orbits, split the discrete valuation directions from the unit directions. For rational dynamics, give regular local charts. For unit matrices or translations in a formal-group neighborhood, passage to a finite-index subgroup often supplies the needed analytic extension; the recurrence theorem below proves a usable version.

At the real place, “ends of Gamma” should mean specified escape directions or cones in Gamma tensor R, together with possible phase data. It should not mean ends of a finitely generated group in the topological group-theoretic sense. In particular Z^r has only one such group-theoretic end when r>=2, which loses the cancellation directions that matter here.

## 2. A correct gluing theorem, and what it does not prove

### 2.1. A finite cone-and-congruence certificate

PROVED THEOREM. Let Gamma=Z^r; a finite torsion factor can be handled by finitely many copies. Let A be the set of admissible ghosts, and let B be a specified finite set of known admissible ghosts. Suppose the following finite data are supplied.

1. An integer M>=1, finitely many closed cones C_i covering R^r, and a bound H>=0.
2. For every residue class c modulo M and every cone C_i, a proof of at least one of:

   (a) no t in C_i with ||t||_infinity>H and t≡c mod M belongs to A;

   (b) every t in C_i with t≡c mod M that belongs to A is in B.

Then

    A is contained in B union {t in Z^r : ||t||_infinity<=H}.

In particular, exact checking of the finite box proves the complete answer.

Proof. Every ghost outside the box has a residue class c and belongs to at least one cone C_i. Its certificate excludes it or places it in B. There are only (2 floor(H)+1)^r integer points in the box. No unproved local-to-global principle is used.

The cones can instead be any finitely described regions with a certified cover. Different local moduli can be replaced by a common multiple. First-order, higher-order, norm, sign, Runge and other inequalities can all be used as leaf certificates. The proof of the **cover** is a separate part of the certificate.

### 2.2. The compact-boundary explanation

PROVED TOPOLOGICAL FORM. Embed Z^r diagonally in

    closed radial compactification of R^r  times  profinite completion of Z^r.

For instance, the radial coordinate is t/(1+||t||). Let K be the closure of the diagonal image. Its points lying over the interior of the radial compactification are precisely the discrete integer points: any convergent sequence with bounded real coordinates has only finitely many possible integer values. Let K_infinity be the part of K lying over the radial boundary.

Suppose that each point of K_infinity has an open neighborhood U with a sound proof that all admissible integer ghosts in U belong to a fixed finite set B. Compactness of K_infinity gives finitely many such neighborhoods. Their complement in K is compact and disjoint from the boundary, so its integer points are bounded and finite. Therefore the admissible ghosts form a finite set.

This gives a precise interpretation of your proposed adelic gluing. The effective version needs the neighborhoods, their bounds, and their finite covering relation to be explicit, as in the preceding theorem.

UNPROVED IN GENERAL. It does not follow from the existence of local Taylor expansions that these neighborhoods cover the boundary. Nor does compactness manufacture a finite cover by neighborhoods for which no exclusion has been proved. An adelic limit of large ghosts can satisfy every selected local condition while a real cancellation direction remains untreated.

This is the central global proof obligation. It should be recorded as a theorem to prove, rather than hidden inside the phrase “isolation and gluing.”

## 3. Structured ghost variables and higher-order isolation

### 3.1. Finite primitive tests for homogeneous leading forms

PROVED THEOREM. Let F_1,...,F_k be convergent p-adic power series on p Z_p^r with integral coefficients and F_j(0)=0. Suppose the lowest nonzero homogeneous part of F_j has degree d_j>=1 and is H_j. Let N>=1. Suppose that, for every vector v modulo p^N with at least one coordinate a p-unit, at least one H_j(v) is nonzero modulo p^N.

Then 0 is the only common zero of the F_j in p^N Z_p^r.

Proof. If t is a nonzero vector in that ball, write t=p^a v, with a>=N and v primitive. Expansion by homogeneous degree gives

    F_j(t)=p^(a*d_j) [H_j(v)+p^a R_j(v)],

where the bracketed remainder is integral. By the finite test, for some j,

    v_p(H_j(v))<N<=a.

The higher terms cannot cancel that leading value. Thus F_j(t)!=0.

For fixed homogeneous H_j, the existence of some such N is equivalent to absence of a common nonzero zero over Q_p. To prove the nontrivial direction, restrict to the compact set of primitive vectors in Z_p^r. At each vector one H_j is nonzero, so it remains nonzero at some finite precision on a neighborhood. A finite subcover gives a uniform N. This is an existence argument; an effective application must produce and check N.

The theorem allows different marker degrees. Known p-power factors in the markers can be divided out before applying it. It does not claim that the first homogeneous parts are sufficient whenever the full analytic zero is isolated.

### 3.2. A norm-leading theorem with one marker and many indices

PROVED THEOREM. Let f(U) be a monic polynomial in Z_p[U] of degree d whose reduction modulo p is irreducible. Let

    O=Z_p[U]/(f),

and define its norm by the determinant of multiplication in the basis 1,U,...,U^(d−1). Let L:Z_p^r -> O be a Z_p-linear map whose reduction modulo p is injective. Necessarily r<=d.

Suppose an analytic scalar marker has the form

    F(t)=p^b [ Norm(L(t)) + p*A_d(t) + sum_(j>d) A_j(t) ],

where b>=0, A_j is homogeneous of degree j with integral coefficients, A_d has degree d, and the series converges on p Z_p^r. Then for every nonzero t in p Z_p^r,

    v_p(F(t))=b+d*min_i v_p(t_i).

In particular, F has no zero there except t=0, despite having only one scalar marker and possibly many indices.

Proof. Write t=p^a v, a>=1, v primitive. The reduction of O is the field F_(p^d). Injectivity of L modulo p gives L(v)!=0 in this field. Multiplication by a nonzero field element is invertible, so its determinant, the reduced norm, is nonzero. By homogeneity,

    F(t)/p^(b+ad)
      =Norm(L(v))+p*A_d(v)+sum_(j>d) p^(a(j−d))*A_j(v),

whose reduction modulo p is a nonzero norm. This proves the valuation formula.

Everything is certifiable: irreducibility of f modulo p, the rank of L modulo p, the exact disappearance of lower terms, and the coefficient divisibilities of the remainder. “p is inert” can be replaced by precisely this finite irreducibility test.

This is a useful generalization of the quadratic symmetric lemma. The price for isolating more directions with one equation is a higher-order singular zero. It does not evade the smooth dimension obstruction.

CAUTION. A module structure or an equivariant marker does not by itself imply the displayed norm-leading identity. That identity is the substantive hypothesis. Taking a norm of an already given marker usually repackages its vanishing condition; it does not create independent information. One must compute the actual first surviving jet on the actual orbit.

### 3.3. Beyond homogeneous jets: valuation initial forms

PROVED THEOREM FOR POLYNOMIAL MARKERS. Let F_1,...,F_k be polynomials in Q_p[t_1,...,t_r]. Fix a coordinate stratum in which specified coordinates are zero and the remaining coordinates are nonzero. Substitute the zero coordinates and discard zero polynomials. For a vector of integer valuations a_i on the nonzero coordinates, write t_i=p^(a_i)u_i, with u_i in Z_p^*.

For a surviving monomial c_alpha*t^alpha, its valuation weight is

    w_alpha=v_p(c_alpha)+sum_i alpha_i*a_i.

For each F_j, let w_j be the minimum weight. Reduce p^(−w_j) F_j(p^a u) modulo p, retaining exactly the monomials with that minimum. Call the resulting polynomial in unit variables the initial form I_(j,a)(u).

If, for every permitted valuation vector a, the corresponding initial forms have no common zero in (F_p^*)^s, then the original markers have no common zero on that coordinate stratum.

Proof. An actual common zero has some valuation vector a and some unit residues u. Reduction of the zero identities after division by p^(w_j) would make all I_(j,a)(u) zero, contradicting the hypothesis.

There are finitely many possible collections of minimum monomials. Their valuation regions are defined by explicit affine-linear equalities and inequalities. Thus a finite list of these regions and their unit-residue tests can be a complete certificate, provided its coverage of the permitted integer valuation vectors is proved. Zero-coordinate strata must also be treated; they cannot be omitted by dividing by a variable.

For convergent series, the same argument applies after proving that the omitted terms always have strictly larger valuation on the region in question. That tail bound is essential.

Example. Over Q_3, the equation

    x^2+y^4=0

has only (0,0). If either coordinate is zero, so is the other. Otherwise let a=v_3(x), b=v_3(y). If 2a!=4b, the term of smallest valuation cannot cancel. If 2a=4b, divide by 3^(4b). The two remaining unit terms are each 1 modulo 3, so their sum is 2 modulo 3. Again cancellation is impossible.

Here the ordinary lowest-degree form is just x^2, which has a nontrivial zero direction. A weighted valuation analysis succeeds where that one homogeneous test does not.

BELIEF. This finite initial-form calculus is a stronger general direction than adding ever more Taylor terms indiscriminately. It connects the ghost method to Newton polygons and tropical initial degenerations, while its proof is still the elementary assertion that a uniquely smallest valuation cannot cancel. Its real-place counterpart is dominance of exponential weights. The difficult cases are precisely the initial degenerations that still have admissible zeros.

## 4. Q4: arithmetic properties, witnesses, and why a single “property sheaf” is misleading

### 4.1. Keep properties as explicit predicates with witnesses

A useful property record should contain:

1. its exact global meaning;
2. an integral model or an explicit witness equation;
3. necessary local conditions and the topology in which they are open or closed;
4. its behavior under covers, group actions, and gauge changes;
5. the certificate that connects the local tests back to the global property.

The same orbit can be tested against many such predicates. They need not all be vanishing equations on the same affine space.

| Property | Elementary representation | Local behavior and main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Vanishing markers | Explicit polynomial or analytic equalities | Jets and initial forms can isolate singular zeros; smooth positive-dimensional germs remain. |
| Being an m-th power | Add a witness w with w^m=f(z), or track power classes | Near a nonzero local m-th power, this is an open condition. Merely adding w does not create a missing equation. |
| S-integrality | Choose a model and check coordinate valuations outside S | This is a condition at infinitely many primes; finitely many local checks need a global denominator theorem. |
| Subgroup membership | Explicit generators, an isogeny image, or a lattice congruence | Membership in a local closure is weaker than membership in the discrete global subgroup. |
| Norm condition | Determinant norm equation with explicit coordinates for a witness | Local norms can be open conditions; a norm-one restriction can reduce the ghost rank only if its global kernel is actually parametrized. |
| Signs and positivity | Real embeddings and exact inequalities | Can exclude entire cones without any p-adic argument. |
| Frobenius or Galois restrictions | Factorization or matrix action modulo a stated good prime | Useful finite tests; ramification and bad reduction must be excluded explicitly. |
| Primitive denominator/divisor conditions | A reconstructed coordinate identity plus a gcd bound | Neither an arbitrary tau value nor Laurentness alone gives this interpretation. |

PROVED LOCAL FACT. If an analytic equation is

    f(t)=w^m

and w0!=0 at a Q_p-point, its derivative in w is m*w0^(m−1)!=0 in Q_p. On a sufficiently small neighborhood, w is an analytic function of t. Thus the perfect-power property has a local branch, not an isolated ghost. When p does not divide m and w0 is a unit, the usual unit Hensel argument already proves this directly.

At a zero or a valuation boundary, power conditions can be useful. For example, if a primitive-direction calculation proves

    v_p(f(p^a v))=b+d*a,

then an m-th power requires m|(b+d*a), and its normalized unit must be an m-th power in the appropriate finite unit quotient. If gcd(d,m) does not divide b, this excludes every a. If it does divide b, it usually restricts a to a congruence class rather than isolating it.

### 4.2. A sheafification can erase the intended obstruction

PROVED DIAGNOSIS. On a ring in which m is invertible, every unit becomes an m-th power after passage to an appropriate finite étale cover: adjoin a root of T^m−u. The root is a unit and the derivative m*T^(m−1) is invertible there.

Consequently the presheaf “units that already are m-th powers in the original ring” has étale sheafification equal to all units. A literal sheaf of that property forgets the global root-existence obstruction.

The correct object is the cover of possible roots, or the groupoid of its lifts and transition data, together with the question whether it has a global section. In expert language this leads toward torsors and Kummer classes. In an elementary proof it is enough to retain the witness equations and their exact compatibility conditions. A norm property should be treated similarly.

For equality conditions an ideal sheaf is natural. For all the properties in the table together, I would use **arithmetic predicates and witness covers**, not claim that one universal closed subvariety or one elementary “property sheaf” captures them all.

### 4.3. Every transformation must transport the actual property

PROVED WARNING. If tau_n is replaced by tau_n/C(n), a p-unit C(n) preserves the p-adic valuation and the condition tau_n=0. It need not preserve tau_n=1, tau_n=±1, or a global perfect-power condition. A local lifting statement about the normalized value is not automatically a lifting statement about the raw value.

The tau section below gives both a counterexample and an explicit augmented map that fixes this problem. This is not a cosmetic issue: it determines which marker must be differentiated and which symmetry must be checked.

## 5. Section 4 of the design: descents beyond a 2-isogeny

### 5.1. Separate orbit coverage from preservation of the solution property

PROVED ABSTRACT DESCENT PRINCIPLE. Let V be a set with an integer-valued height H>=0 having finite sublevel sets. Suppose a finite set of explicit transformations has the following property: every v outside a specified finite base has at least one applicable transformation v -> v' with H(v')<H(v), and the transformation has a stated exact reconstruction relation. Iteration reaches the base after finitely many steps. Reversing the recorded transformations proves coverage of V by the generated constructions.

This does not prove that V itself is finite: reverse constructions may create infinite orbits. To classify a smaller solution set, the arithmetic property must either be preserved during descent or be tracked as part of the state. Normalizing a unit into a small logarithmic region, for example, need not preserve a coordinate marker. The Thomas-family proof below deliberately uses such normalization for unit coverage, and does not mislabel it a descent preserving the Thue solutions.

### 5.2. Endomorphism descent is a sound extension

TARGET. Replace multiplication by 2 or a 2-isogeny by an explicitly given endomorphism phi of degree greater than 1, including 1−omega in appropriate CM settings. Supply:

- explicit cover equations and a finite list of arithmetic branches;
- an exhaustive lifting proof for the relevant points;
- exact formulas for the inverse descent steps;
- an explicit height inequality, with exceptional points and denominators handled.

A sufficient inequality is

    h(P)>=d*h(Q)−C,  d>1,

whenever P=phi(Q)+R and R belongs to a fixed finite seed list. For h(P)>C/(d−1), this gives h(Q)<h(P). If h is integer-valued and its bounded sublevel set is explicitly enumerable, the preceding descent principle applies.

The inequality itself should be proved from the actual rational maps and gcd/resultant bounds; it should not be imported as an unexamined canonical-height theorem. Likewise, finiteness of the seed quotient must be proved for the arithmetic points under consideration. A finite quotient of a subgroup whose generators are already known does not establish coverage of all relevant points.

### 5.3. Non-UFD rings: bounded gcd and power extraction are distinct

PROVED ALGEBRAIC TOOL. If P,Q in Z[X] admit an explicit Bezout identity

    A(X)P(X)+B(X)Q(X)=C,

with A,B in Z[X] and nonzero integer C, then gcd(P(n),Q(n)) divides C for every integer n. Resultants provide such identities after denominator clearing.

This gives a finite bad-prime set without any algebraic-number-ring unique factorization. It is often enough for endpoint splittings and some norm equations.

UNPROVED WITHOUT EXTRA DATA. In a general order, the assertion that an element with all relevant prime valuations divisible by m is an m-th power up to one of finitely many multipliers does not follow from a bounded gcd alone. The missing principalization/power-extraction information is exactly where a class-group argument would normally enter.

Under your rules, replace that black box by an operational certificate: an explicit localization with a proved division/reduction algorithm, or a finite list of module/factorization types with explicit transition identities and a decreasing integer norm. The proof must show that the list is exhaustive. Calling it a “finite class certificate” is acceptable only if the verification does not assume the class-group or ideal-theoretic theorem that was meant to be avoided.

No universal localization theorem is being asserted here. A localization that works in one ring has to be proved to work, rather than inferred from the ring being a number-field order.

### 5.4. Hensel towers and vertical parameter changes

PROVED DISTINCTION. Increasing p-adic precision is not a height-decreasing descent. An infinite compatible Hensel tower may converge to a perfectly valid p-adic point that is not a global solution. A tower proves global exclusion only when it forces an impossible valuation, forces a fixed integer ghost to be divisible by every p^N, or is combined with a height bound that makes a sufficiently deep congruence decisive.

Similarly, an expansion in a family parameter a is not a descent a -> a'. A vertical descent needs an explicit map taking every relevant solution in the a-fiber to a relevant solution in a strictly smaller fiber, together with all exceptional fibers and reconstruction data. This is a promising target where an identity supplies such a map, but it is not an automatic consequence of having a family over Z[a].


## 6. Q2: Archimedean isolation is arithmetic separation, not a rationality dichotomy


The assertion “an elementary archimedean argument works exactly when its dominant term is rational, whereas two independent dominant exponentials require Baker” is not a mathematical criterion. Rationality is neither necessary nor sufficient. The useful questions are:

1. Is there a certified absence of cancellation in the relevant real cone?
2. If cancellation occurs, does a discrete arithmetic quantity have an explicit lower bound larger than the analytic remainder?
3. Can one construct explicit rational approximants strong enough to control the cancellation locus?

The third route includes Padé/hypergeometric constructions. Such constructions are particularly relevant to the very simplest-cubic family proposed in Q5: Chen, Lettl–Pethő–Voutier, and Wakabayashi give a documented non-logarithmic-forms route. Their existence directly refutes the proposed dichotomy.

### 6.1 PROVED: domination on a cone

Let Gamma=Z^r. Fix nonzero complex numbers c_1,...,c_s and real linear forms ell_1,...,ell_s on R^r. Set

f(t)=sum_j c_j exp(ell_j(t)).

Let C be any subset of Gamma, let H:C -> [0,infinity), and suppose there are an index j0, delta>0, and B>=0 such that

ell_j0(t)-ell_j(t) >= delta H(t)-B

for every t in C and every j != j0. Then

|f(t)| >= |c_j0| exp(ell_j0(t)) [1-exp(B-delta H(t)) sum_(j != j0) |c_j/c_j0|].

In particular, f(t) != 0 whenever

delta H(t) > B + log(sum_(j != j0) |c_j/c_j0|).

If s=1 the conclusion is immediate and no logarithm of an empty sum is used.

Proof. Factor out c_j0 exp(ell_j0(t)), apply the reverse triangle inequality, and apply the assumed upper bound to every ratio. This proves the displayed inequality and the conclusion.

Negative real units can be handled after a finite parity decomposition. Polynomial coefficient factors can be allowed if their ratios have explicit bounds; their logarithms are then added to the displayed error bound. Complex phases require actual control of the phases, not merely the real growth rates.

No rationality appears in this statement. For example, alpha=3+2 sqrt(2) and beta=2+sqrt(3) are multiplicatively independent algebraic units and alpha>beta>1. Thus alpha^n-beta^n>0 for every positive integer n by order alone. Their independence is also elementary: if alpha^r=beta^s, the common value lies in Q(sqrt(2)) intersect Q(sqrt(3))=Q. The only rational elements among powers of either positive norm-one unit are 1, as applying the quadratic conjugation gives the inverse; hence r=s=0. More generally, alpha^r+beta^s is positive throughout the nonnegative quadrant, including directions in which the two exponential terms have comparable size. Independent exponential terms do not themselves force a logarithmic-forms argument.

What remains difficult is a cancellation wall: two or more terms have comparable size, their signs or phases permit cancellation, and the resulting small quantity has no sufficiently strong explicit arithmetic lower bound. Such walls are the natural location of small-divisor problems. They may be handled by a norm identity, factorization, an explicit approximation construction, a descent, or congruences in special cases. There is no theorem saying they must be handled by Baker.

Rationality is not sufficient either: f(n)=2^n(1-(-1)^n) has rational dominant terms and infinitely many zeros. A parity decomposition identifies an identically zero component; rational leading data cannot replace a nondegeneracy hypothesis. Even after eliminating such an elementary degeneracy, a rational leading approximation is useful only when its remainder is smaller than a certified arithmetic gap.

### 6.2 PROVED: an arithmetic-gap certificate

Suppose an admissible orbit point t must satisfy w(t) in lambda(t)+Delta(t) Z, where Delta(t)>0. Suppose a real approximation q(t) satisfies

|w(t)-q(t)| <= E(t).

If

distance(q(t), lambda(t)+Delta(t) Z) > E(t),

then t is not admissible.

Proof. Every point of the required lattice has distance at least the displayed distance from q(t), contradicting the approximation bound.

The familiar rational polynomial-part Runge argument is a special case: if q(t) has denominator dividing D and w(t) is an integer, then a nonzero difference w(t)-q(t) has absolute value at least 1/D. An error smaller than 1/D forces equality; one must then analyse that equality, rather than automatically declare a contradiction.

### 6.3 PROVED: the conjugate norm-gap certificate

Let K have degree d, given by an explicit irreducible polynomial, and let A be a nonzero element of an explicitly given order in K. Let sigma_1,...,sigma_d be all complex embeddings, counting a pair of complex conjugates separately. If

|sigma_1(A)| <= E,

|sigma_j(A)| <= M_j for j>=2,

and E product_(j>=2) M_j < 1,

then no such A exists.

Proof. Multiplication by A on the order has an integer matrix and nonzero determinant. This determinant is the product of the conjugates. Its absolute value is at least 1, whereas the displayed estimates make it less than 1.

Equivalently, any nonzero A satisfies

|sigma_1(A)| >= 1 / product_(j>=2) |sigma_j(A)|.

If A initially has a denominator, apply the statement to D A for an explicit integer D clearing that denominator. This gives a height-dependent arithmetic gap without any rationality of the distinguished embedding. It is often weaker than the gap needed on a cancellation wall, but it is a rigorous additional archimedean mechanism.


### 6.4. A determinant certificate for explicit irrationality bounds


This is the elementary component the programme should extract from Padé constructions. It genuinely extends arithmetic-gap certificates beyond rational polynomial parts.

#### Theorem 6.4

PROVED.

Let alpha be real. Suppose integers p_j,q_j, indexed by j>=0, and explicit constants C_Q>0, C_E>=1, Q>1, E>1 satisfy

0<|q_j| <= C_Q Q^j,

|q_j alpha-p_j| <= C_E E^(-j),

p_j q_(j+1)-p_(j+1) q_j != 0

for every j>=0. Put

kappa = log(Q)/log(E),

K = 2 C_Q Q^2 (2 C_E)^kappa.

Then for every p in Z and q>=1,

|alpha-p/q| >= 1 / [K q^(1+kappa)].

The hypotheses themselves imply that alpha is irrational.

Proof. Choose n to be the least nonnegative integer with C_E E^(-n)<=1/(2q). Since C_E>=1 and q>=1,

n=ceil(log(2 C_E q)/log(E)).

The rational p/q cannot equal both p_n/q_n and p_(n+1)/q_(n+1), because their cross determinant is nonzero. Choose j in {n,n+1} for which p q_j-q p_j !=0. This is a nonzero integer, so

1 <= |p q_j-q p_j|

<= |q_j| |p-q alpha| + q |q_j alpha-p_j|

<= |q_j| q |alpha-p/q| + 1/2.

Consequently

|alpha-p/q| >= 1 / (2 q |q_j|)

>= 1 / (2 C_Q Q^(n+1) q).

The ceiling inequality gives

Q^(n+1) <= Q^2 (2 C_E q)^kappa.

Substitution gives the claimed lower bound. If alpha were rational, taking p/q=alpha would contradict that positive bound.

#### Corollary 6.5: use against a degree-d Thue approximation

Under the theorem's hypotheses, suppose an integer solution with denominator q>=1 also implies

0<|alpha-p/q| <= C_T/q^d.

If 1+kappa<d, then

q^(d-1-kappa) <= K C_T.

Thus

q <= (K C_T)^(1/(d-1-kappa)).

Proof. Compare the upper and lower bounds and multiply by the positive denominators.

For cubic equations, the critical requirement is kappa<2. Parameter-dependent Q(a), E(a), C_Q(a), C_E(a), and C_T(a) are allowed; all constants and the resulting cutoff must be explicit. The hard lemma is construction of integer approximants with the three certified properties. The determinant argument itself is entirely elementary.

This should be included in the ghost toolbox as an independent archimedean property: a certified approximation sequence bounds the permitted ghost height. It is not a form of first-order p-adic isolation and does not depend on a rational dominant branch.


## 7. Q3: A recurrence-only lifting theorem, with exact gauges and raw-marker transport

### 7.1. Corrections to the proposed scope


KNOWN, primary source checked. Generic Somos-6

tau_(n+6) tau_n = alpha tau_(n+5) tau_(n+1) + beta tau_(n+4) tau_(n+2) + gamma tau_(n+3)^2

with alpha*beta*gamma != 0 is not an ordinary cluster-algebra mutation recurrence. Fedorov–Hone explicitly say this: it belongs to the broader class of Laurent-phenomenon algebras. Their paper constructs the genus-2 Jacobian translation and sigma-function solution for generic nonsingular data. Therefore “period-1 cluster recurrence” is too narrow to include the desired generic genus-2 example. Separate three hypotheses:

1. A recurrence with a proved Laurent presentation.
2. An explicit realization, when available, as translation on an elliptic curve, Jacobian, or another algebraic group.
3. A good local return chart and a nondegenerate observable.

The local theorem below needs (3), and sometimes uses (1) to certify it; it does not need (2). Abelian geometry is useful for finding and proving the returns, controlling the divisor of the observable, and proving global coverage.

The phrase “quadratic-exponential interpolation for every abelian tau function” needs a normalization and a precise definition of tau. A tau value is commonly a value of a section of a line bundle, not an intrinsic function on the abelian variety. Changing trivializations changes the scalar cocycle. One cannot infer a normalization-independent valuation formula for raw tau_n from “attached to an abelian variety.”

### 7.2. An exact quadratic gauge identity from the bilinear recurrence alone

PROVED THEOREM (quadratic gauge).

Let K be a field of characteristic zero. Fix d>=2, coefficients a_j in K, and a bilinear recurrence

tau_(n+d) tau_n = sum_j a_j tau_(n+j) tau_(n+d-j),

where every index j lies between 1 and d-1 and the right-hand side is not the zero polynomial. Repeated summands or a middle square cause no difficulty. Let R be its rational state-shift map on states x=(x_0,...,x_(d-1)). This map is birational: the same recurrence recovers the discarded coordinate from the shifted state. For A,B in K*, define

G_(A,B)(x)_i = A B^i x_i.

Fix an integer N>=1 and set

H = G_(A,B)^(-1) R^N.

Then, as rational maps,

R^(Nt) = G_(A^t B^(N*binom(t,2)), B^t) H^t

for every integer t, wherever the relevant maps are defined. Thus, for a state z=(tau_c,...,tau_(c+d-1)),

tau_(c+Nt) = A^t B^(N*binom(t,2)) (H^t z)_0.

Proof.

Every summand in the recurrence has ordinary degree 2 and index weight d. Therefore

R G_(a,b) = G_(ab,b) R,

and, by induction,

R^N G_(a,b) = G_(a b^N,b) R^N.

Since the G maps commute with one another,

H G_(a,b) = G_(a b^N,b) H.

Now R^N=G_(A,B)H. In the t-fold product, moving each H past the next G changes the first gauge component by B^N. Consequently the first components multiply to

A^t B^(N*(0+1+...+(t-1))) = A^t B^(N*binom(t,2)),

and the second components multiply to B^t. This proves the identity for t>=0. Taking inverses proves the negative cases as well. The expression binom(t,2)=t(t-1)/2 is an integer for every integer t.

This exact identity is a useful answer to the request for an “explicit factor.” The quadratic factor is not a conjectural consequence of sigma functions here: it follows from the two-dimensional scaling symmetry of a homogeneous bilinear recurrence.

The same claim does not apply to every period-1 cluster recurrence. General exchange monomials can have other degrees or index weights. The relevant grading torus and the linear action of the shift on its character lattice must first be computed. Quadratic exponents arise from the size-two unipotent block implicit in (a,b)->(ab,b); other grading actions need not produce quadratic factors.

### 7.3. A local analytic interpolation theorem with proof

Let R_p=Z_p and let R_p< x_1,...,x_s > denote restricted power series with integral coefficients: coefficients tend to zero p-adically, so the series converge on Z_p^s.

PROVED THEOREM (near-identity return).

Let H be an analytic map given by s restricted integral power series. Assume coefficientwise

H(x) = x + p^e K(x),

where K has integral restricted coefficients, e>=1 if p is odd, and e>=2 if p=2. Let z in Z_p^s and f in Z_p< x >. Then f(H^t z), initially for integer t, extends to a restricted analytic function h(t) on Z_p. It has an expansion

h(t) = sum_(r>=0) a_r t^r,

with

v_p(a_r) >= e*r - v_p(r!) for r>=1.

Proof.

Let U act on restricted series by U(g)=g composed with H, and put Delta=U-I. Substitution and the binomial theorem show

Delta(g) is in p^e Z_p< x >

for every integral restricted g. By induction Delta^j maps integral series into p^(ej) times integral series.

The bounded operator

L=log(I+Delta)=sum_(j>=1) (-1)^(j+1) Delta^j/j

converges. Moreover L takes integral series into p^e times integral series, since

ej-v_p(j)>=e,

and the left side tends to infinity with j. Thus L^r f belongs to p^(er) Z_p< x >.

Define

h(t)=sum_(r>=0) t^r (L^r f)(z)/r!.

The stated coefficient bound follows. It tends to infinity with r because e>1/(p-1); hence this is a restricted analytic series. The formal identities exp(log(1+T))=1+T and exp(t log(1+T))=(1+T)^t, now valid for the convergent operator Delta, show that h(t)=f(H^t z) for nonnegative integer t.

The map H is an analytic bijection of Z_p^s: solving x+p^e K(x)=y by x_(r+1)=y-p^e K(x_r) is a strict contraction, as integral restricted K is 1-Lipschitz. Its inverse has integral restricted coefficients, obtainable by the same contraction in the Tate algebra. Thus the identity also holds for negative integers.

This is a standard p-adic interpolation principle; Poonen gives a short Mahler-difference proof with the optimal threshold e>1/(p-1). The proof above supplies the coefficient estimate needed for the lifting statements.

### 7.4. First-order lifting, fully explicit

PROVED THEOREM.

Under the hypotheses in Section 7.3, assume

v_p(f(H z)-f(z))=e.

Then for every nonzero t in Z_p,

v_p(h(t)-h(0))=e+v_p(t).

In particular, h(t)=h(0) only for t=0.

Proof.

For every r>=2,

er-v_p(r!)>=e+1.

For odd p this follows from v_p(r!)<=(r-1)/(p-1) and integrality; for p=2 and e>=2 it follows from v_2(r!)<=r-1. Hence all coefficients a_r with r>=2 are divisible by p^(e+1).

Since h(1)-h(0)=a_1+sum_(r>=2) a_r has valuation e, a_1 has valuation e. If v_p(t)=a>=0, the linear term has valuation e+a, while all higher terms have valuation at least e+1+2a. The smallest valuation is unique, proving the formula.

This uses one finite drift check modulo p^(e+1), not a mysterious global property of the recurrence.

### 7.5. Second-order lifting at an exact symmetry state

PROVED THEOREM.

Continue with H,z,f as above. Suppose there is an analytic involution sigma preserving the domain such that

sigma(z)=z,
sigma H sigma=H^(-1),
f composed with sigma=f.

Assume also

v_p(f(H^2 z)-2f(H z)+f(z))=2e.

Then, for every nonzero t in Z_p,

v_p(h(t)-h(0))=2e-v_p(2)+2v_p(t).

Thus h(t)=h(0) only for t=0.

Proof.

For integer t, the symmetry gives h(-t)=h(t). Continuity and density of the integers imply this on Z_p. Therefore a_r=0 for odd r.

Set d0=2e-v_p(2). For even r>=4 the coefficient estimate gives

v_p(a_r)>=d0+1.

For odd p, this follows from

e(r-2)-v_p(r!) >= r-2-(r-1)/2 = (r-3)/2,

whose integer lower bound is at least 1. For p=2 and e>=2, using v_2(r!)<=r-1 gives

er-v_2(r!)-(2e-1) >= (e-1)(r-2)>=2.

Now

f(H^2 z)-2f(H z)+f(z)
=2a_2+sum_(even r>=4) (2^r-2)a_r.

Dividing by 2 shows that a_2 agrees, modulo p^(d0+1), with half of the displayed second finite difference. The latter has valuation d0 by hypothesis. Hence v_p(a_2)=d0.

For v_p(t)=a>=0, the quadratic term has valuation d0+2a, and each higher term has strictly greater valuation. This proves the formula.

The factor v_p(2) matters at p=2. A formula 2e+2v_2(t) cannot be asserted without an additional evenness/divisibility check on the quadratic coefficient.

Example showing the p=2 normalization is real, not an artifact: with e=2,

H(x,y)=(x+4y+8, y+4),
z=(0,0),
f(x,y)=x,
sigma(x,y)=(x,-y).

Then H=I+4K, sigma H sigma=H^(-1), and h(t)=8t^2. Its valuation is 3+2v_2(t), exactly 2e-1+2v_2(t).

### 7.6. Applying the theorem to the gauged tau sequence

Suppose the normalized return from Section 7.2 is represented on a p-adic chart by a map H satisfying Section 7.3. Assume A,B belong to Z_p^*. Let iota map chart coordinates to recurrence states, send the chart base point z to (tau_c,...,tau_(c+d-1)), and intertwine H with the normalized state return. Suppose its first coordinate has the form

iota_0(x)=tau_c+p^b f(x),

where b>=0, f has integral restricted coefficients, and f(z)=0.

Put

C(t)=A^t B^(N*binom(t,2))

for integer t. Then the exact gauge identity gives

tau_(c+Nt)-C(t)tau_c=p^b C(t) f(H^t z).

Since C(t) is a p-unit, the first-order hypotheses imply

v_p(tau_(c+Nt)-C(t)tau_c)=b+e+v_p(t),

and the symmetric second-order hypotheses imply

v_p(tau_(c+Nt)-C(t)tau_c)=b+2e-v_p(2)+2v_p(t).

These are recurrence-only lifting lemmas with the requested explicit factor. They apply to Somos-4 and to the bilinear Somos-6/8 instances whenever the finite chart, return, gauge and drift certificates are supplied. They do not claim that those certificates hold at every state or every prime.

For interpolation of the raw factor C(t) itself on all Z_p, its finite-order unit components must be removed by restricting the integer index to suitable residue classes. At p=2 one may need an additional index restriction to make the exponential series converge coefficientwise. This issue is avoided entirely in the displayed integer-index valuation statements: C(t) is an explicit p-unit for every integer t, and the normalized sequence is the analytic object.

### 7.7. How to certify the good return chart from finite data

PROVED local construction.

Suppose a recurrence block map F admits a Taylor expansion in x-z with integral coefficients, convergent on the state residue disc z+p Z_p^s, satisfies F(z)=z mod p, and its inverse admits such an integral Taylor expansion there as well. (Integral Laurent expressions at a unit seed ensure this hypothesis.) Write x=z+p w. The rescaled map is

F_tilde(w)=[F(z+p w)-z]/p.

Modulo p it is the affine map

w -> b+Jw,

where b=(F(z)-z)/p mod p and J=DF(z) mod p. Terms of degree >=2 in w carry a factor p after rescaling. The integral inverse implies det J !=0 mod p.

The affine map is a permutation of the finite set F_p^s. Choose its finite order M. Then

F_tilde^M(w)=w mod p

coefficientwise. For odd p, this gives e=1 in Section 7.3. At p=2, square this near-identity map once more: if K=I+2U, direct substitution gives K^2=I mod4, so e=2 suffices. In the following formulas, replace M by 2M at p=2 to include this extra squaring.

Applied to a normalized gauged return H, passing to H^M remains a normalized return for R^(NM). Indeed Section 7.2 gives new constants

A'=A^M B^(N*binom(M,2)),
B'=B^M.

Thus the required deeper return and its quadratic factor are still explicit.

An elementary finite certificate can use H(z) mod p^2, DH(z) mod p, a bound on denominators on the disc, and the order of the resulting affine map. One must not infer coefficientwise H=I mod p merely from H(z)=z mod p: the derivative and the higher chart coefficients must be addressed.

### 7.8. What Laurentness does and does not prove

PROVED consequences of a Laurent presentation.

If every tau_n lies in Z[coefficients, x_0^(+-1),...,x_(d-1)^(+-1)], then integer coefficients and initial seed coordinates equal to +-1 give integer tau_n. More generally, S-integral coefficients and seed coordinates that are S-units give S-integral tau_n.

Integer initial coordinates alone do not suffice. In Somos-4 with alpha=beta=1 and initial seed (3,1,1,1), the next term is 2/3.

A useful local consequence is often overlooked: if the recurrence coefficients are p-integral and the starting seed coordinates are p-units, every Laurent iterate is analytic and integral on its residue disc. A block return can therefore be evaluated by its cancelled Laurent expression even if a naive step-by-step recurrence calculation encounters a denominator that is zero modulo p. If the returned seed is again a unit seed and negative iterates have Laurent expressions, the inverse block map is also analytic and integral. This supplies the regularity required in Section 7.7.

One must actually use the Laurent expressions or equivalent polynomial identities. Numerical division by a zero residue is not a valid proof of continuation.

Laurentness does not imply a primitive-divisor theorem. For a completely explicit counterexample, take

tau_n=2^(n^2),
alpha=1,
beta=252.

Then

tau_(n+4)tau_n = tau_(n+3)tau_(n+1)+252tau_(n+2)^2,

because after division by 2^(2n^2+8n+8), this identity becomes 2^8=2^2+252. The sequence is integral, nonconstant, and satisfies a Laurent Somos-4 recurrence, but it has no prime divisor other than 2. Any primitive-divisor conclusion needs further nondegeneracy and growth hypotheses. This example may be geometrically degenerate, which is exactly the point: Laurentness does not supply geometric nondegeneracy.

Likewise a primitive-denominator lemma for reconstructed curve coordinates does not follow from Laurentness. One must prove an explicit expression such as x(P_n)=A_n/B_n^2 together with a gcd bound, outside a stated finite set of primes. For Jacobians, the relevant divisors and numerator cancellations need their own certificate; an arbitrary tau coordinate is not automatically the primitive denominator of a Mumford-coordinate expression.

### 7.9. What is genuinely missing for a universal elliptic/genus-2 theorem

TARGET (geometric return-certificate package).

For a specified nondegenerate family of bilinear Laurent recurrences with an explicit elliptic or genus-2 translation model, construct, at every stated good prime and for every relevant residue orbit:

1. A finite set of regular seed charts, including charts that cross tau=0 divisors.
2. A return length N and p-unit gauge constants A,B.
3. A certificate that the normalized return preserves a chart and has an integral analytic inverse.
4. Exact symmetry identities when second-order lifting is claimed.
5. A nonvanishing drift or a finite homogeneous-jet isolation certificate for the particular arithmetic markers.
6. A primitive-denominator or other global arithmetic interpretation of those markers.

Once 1–5 are supplied, Sections 7.2–7.7 prove the lifting lemma. The truly difficult universal claims are chart/cocycle control and nondegeneracy of the markers, not the analytic interpolation step itself. Item 6 is a separate global arithmetic problem. The genus-2 Jacobian translation does not make a single theta marker isolate an arbitrary rank-two orbit at a smooth zero: the local dimension obstruction remains.

### 7.10. Computations for Sol

For each test recurrence and chosen prime p:

1. Record the recurrence, coefficients, initial seed, and all excluded denominator/discriminant primes.
2. Compute a block return modulo p using Laurent expressions or certified alternative charts, not illegal division by zero.
3. Search for A,B with R^N(z)_i = A B^i z_i mod p for every seed coordinate.
4. Form H=G_(A,B)^(-1)R^N; compute its affine return on w=(x-z)/p modulo p and pass to the affine order.
5. Verify the near-identity congruence coefficientwise to the required depth.
6. For each marker f, compute Delta f(z) mod p^(e+1) and Delta^2 f(z) mod p^(2e+1). Keep the scale b introduced by chart coordinates.
7. If second order is desired, first verify sigma H sigma=H^(-1), sigma(z)=z, and f sigma=f as exact rational identities. A few matching terms do not certify this symmetry.
8. Test the predicted valuations at t=1,p,p^2 and at several primitive unit t, including negative t, while regarding these tests as checks rather than proofs.
9. When the drift vanishes, compute the first nonzero homogeneous jet and test all primitive vectors modulo p^N with an explicit coefficient-tail bound. Do not presume that a rank deficiency is repaired by taking more terms.

### 7.11. Essential correction: normalized lifting does not isolate a raw fixed tau value

PROVED CAUTION.

The conclusion

tau_(c+Nt)-C(t)tau_c != 0 for t!=0

does not imply

tau_(c+Nt)-tau_c !=0 for t!=0.

The first compares the normalized tau value with its initial normalized value. A raw equation tau_(c+Nt)=tau_c becomes a moving level equation

(H^t z)_0=C(t)^(-1)tau_c.

The gauge factor must either preserve the desired property or be included in the marker. It preserves tau=0, and a p-unit gauge preserves v_p(tau). It does not preserve tau=1 or tau=+-1 in general. It preserves a global m-th-power property only when the relevant factors are global m-th powers (or when the admissible power class is tracked explicitly).

An explicit counterexample is available within the same homogeneous bilinear recurrence class. Let p be an odd prime and set

A=1/(1+p),
tau_n=A^n(1+pn).

Then tau_0=tau_1=1. Yet the normalized sequence h(n)=A^(-n)tau_n=1+pn satisfies

v_p(h(n)-1)=1+v_p(n)

for every nonzero integer n. Moreover tau satisfies the Somos-4 recurrence with alpha=4, beta=-3. Indeed, putting s=1+pn and dividing by A^(2n+4), the recurrence becomes

s(s+4p)=4(s+p)(s+3p)-3(s+2p)^2,

and both sides equal s^2+4ps. Thus normalized first-order isolation coexists with two raw occurrences of the value 1.

### 7.12. An explicit augmented near-identity map for the raw tau property

PROVED THEOREM.

Assume the exact gauge identity of Section 7.2 and a chart in which H=I+p^eK satisfies Section 7.3, with the chart intertwining the state return as in Section 7.6. Assume A,B belong to Z_p^*, let iota_0(x) be the original first state coordinate, an integral restricted series on the chart, and fix a desired raw value w in Z_p. Write C(t)=A^t B^(N*binom(t,2)) for integer t. Choose an integer E>=1 with

A^E = B^E = 1 mod p^(2e),

and put L=2E. Such an E exists because the unit group modulo p^(2e) is finite. For each integer b with 0<=b<L, define

z_b=H^b z,
C_b=A^b B^(N*binom(b,2)),
D_b=A^L B^(N*(Lb+binom(L,2))),
Q=B^(N L^2).

Then D_b and Q are 1 modulo p^(2e). On the augmented polydisc with variables (x,s,r), define

T_b(x,s,r)
= ( H^L(x), s*(1+p^e*r), Q*r+(Q-1)/p^e ).

This is an integral restricted analytic map satisfying

T_b=I mod p^e

coefficientwise. Start it at

(z_b, 1, (D_b-1)/p^e).

If its t-th iterate is (x_t,s_t,r_t), then, for every integer t,

tau_(c+N*(b+Lt))=C_b*s_t*iota_0(x_t).

Consequently the raw fixed-value equation tau_(c+N*(b+Lt))=w is the zero of the single integral analytic marker

M_b(x,s,r)=C_b*s*iota_0(x)-w.

The first- and second-order lifting theorems can now be applied to this augmented map and this actual marker, provided their nondegeneracy and symmetry hypotheses are checked for it.

Proof.

Since L=2E,

binom(L,2)=E(2E-1).

Every exponent of A or B in D_b and Q is divisible by E, so D_b=Q=1 modulo p^(2e). The map H^L remains I modulo p^e. The difference between the second coordinate and s is p^e sr. The difference between the third coordinate and r is

(Q-1)r+(Q-1)/p^e,

which is divisible coefficientwise by p^e. Thus T_b has the required near-identity form.

Its explicit iterates satisfy

1+p^e r_t=D_b Q^t,
s_t=D_b^t Q^binom(t,2),
x_t=H^(b+Lt)z.

Finally,

C(b+Lt)/C(b)
=A^(Lt) B^(N*[Lbt+(L^2t^2-Lt)/2])
=D_b^t Q^binom(t,2).

Substitution into the exact gauge identity proves the tau formula. This also works for negative integers by invertibility and the same identities.

There are two advantages. No assertion that the raw quadratic factor is already analytic on all Z_p is needed, and there is no Teichmuller ambiguity: it is removed by finitely many residue classes modulo L. The price is two additional state coordinates. These are bookkeeping coordinates, not independent integer ghost variables.

An involution for the normalized H and normalized observable does not automatically yield an involution fixing M_b for T_b. To use the second-order theorem for a raw value, certify a reversing involution on the augmented system or certify the vanishing of the odd Taylor coefficients by another exact identity. The rank and jet tests must be recomputed for the actual raw marker.

### 7.13. General grading torus: when quadratic gauges are justified

PROVED THEOREM.

Let a torus T=(K*)^r act by transformations G_a, and suppose a birational recurrence shift R satisfies

R G_a = G_(phi(a)) R,

where phi is a monomial torus automorphism. Its exponent matrix M belongs to GL_r(Z). Fix an integer N>=1 and a in T, and put H=G_a^(-1)R^N. Then

R^(Nt)=G_(a * phi^N(a) * ... * phi^(N(t-1))(a)) H^t

for t>=0, with the corresponding inverse identity for negative t.

This follows by the same commuting-gauge calculation as Section 7.2. The exponents in the accumulated gauge are the entries of

I+M^N+...+(M^N)^(t-1).

If M^N=I+U with U^s=0, then this matrix is exactly

sum_(j=0)^(s-1) binom(t,j+1) U^j.

Indeed, expand (I+U)^i and use sum_(i=0)^(t-1) binom(i,j)=binom(t,j+1). Thus the gauge exponents are polynomials of degree at most s. The bilinear Somos gauge has s=2, giving the quadratic formula. Larger unipotent blocks give higher-degree polynomial exponents. If M has eigenvalues that are not roots of unity, no finite-index restriction makes it unipotent, and this argument gives no polynomial-in-t gauge at all.

Therefore quadratic-exponential behavior is a consequence of a specific grading/cocycle structure, not of Laurentness alone and not of the mere phrase “tau function.”

### 7.14. Audit of the analytic inverse and finite-return claims

The analytic inverse argument is valid on the full unit polydisc: integral restricted K is 1-Lipschitz both for points and for the Gauss norm on bounded restricted series. The iteration X_(j+1)(y)=y-p^e K(X_j(y)) is a contraction in that Banach algebra, so its limit is an integral restricted analytic inverse of H. No unproved inverse-function claim is needed.

The finite-return construction requires an integral Taylor expansion in the unscaled local variables x-z. Integrality merely in the rescaled variables w=(x-z)/p would not suffice to discard higher terms modulo p. For example, an expression containing (x-z)^2/p has integral values on the residue disc, but after rescaling its quadratic term need not vanish modulo p. Section 7.7 now states the stronger correct hypothesis. Laurent expressions at a p-unit seed supply it.

Finite evaluations at sample points do not in general prove a coefficientwise congruence. The certified Taylor-integrality statement proves that all terms of degree >=2 in the rescaled return are divisible by p, so only the constant term and Jacobian need checking. The reduction is then affine, and its finite order proves coefficientwise identity, not just identity as a function on F_p^s.


The theorem covers the particular Somos-8 instances for which its return-chart hypotheses are certified; the name Somos-8 by itself is not a Laurent, nonsingularity, or Jacobian-translation certificate. The argument is independent of the dimension of the certified local chart.

## 8. Q5: Parametric ghosts and a complete elementary unit-coverage theorem


This is a full elementary theorem, not a literature quotation. It proves a uniform ghost parametrization for a positive-rank family, without Dirichlet's unit theorem or a class-group computation. Its cutoff 16 is deliberately convenient rather than optimal.

### 8.1. A general certificate principle for orders of arbitrary signature

PROVED. Let R be an explicitly given order of degree d with an explicit Z-basis. Choose the r_1 real embeddings and one embedding from each of the r_2 complex-conjugate pairs. Write w_i=1 for a real place and w_i=2 for a complex place. Suppose r_1+r_2-1 explicit units u_j have logarithm vectors (log|sigma_i(u_j)|)_i spanning the hyperplane sum_i w_i v_i=0. Let H be the subgroup generated by these units and any additional explicitly listed units.

Define M_i=exp((1/2) sum_j |log|sigma_i(u_j)||). Suppose every element delta of R with norm ±1 and |sigma_i(delta)|<=M_i for all i has a certified expression in H. Then R^×=H.

Proof. For an arbitrary unit epsilon, its logarithm vector belongs to the stated hyperplane because its norm has absolute value 1. Express that vector in the displayed basis of logarithm vectors, round each real coefficient to a nearest integer, and divide epsilon by the corresponding product of the u_j. The remaining element delta has all embedding magnitudes bounded by the M_i. The hypothesis puts delta in H, and hence epsilon in H. Conversely H consists of units by construction.

The hypothesis about bounded delta is a finite test. If b_1,...,b_d is the given Z-basis, invert the d-by-d matrix of all embeddings of the b_j, including both complex-conjugate embeddings. The bounds on the embedding magnitudes give an explicit bound on every integer coefficient of delta. One can enumerate that finite box, calculate each norm by an integer determinant, and certify the norm ±1 elements. This proof assumes no unit theorem. In a parameter family, the box may grow with the parameter; the real advance is to replace enumeration by uniform inequalities proving that all possible coefficients fall into a small symbolic list. The proof below does exactly that.

### 8.2. Uniform unit-generation theorem

PROVED.

Let a be an integer with a>=16. Define

f_a(z)=z^3-(a-1)z^2-(a+2)z-1,

F_a(x,y)=x^3-(a-1)x^2 y-(a+2)xy^2-y^3.

Let theta be the positive real root of f_a, and let R_a=Z[theta]. Then every unit epsilon in R_a has a unique expression

epsilon = s theta^A (theta+1)^B,

where s is 1 or -1 and A,B are integers.

In particular, every integer solution of |F_a(x,y)|=1 is represented by

x-y theta = s theta^A (theta+1)^B.

This concerns the order R_a. It makes no assertion that R_a is the maximal order, and it does not yet classify all pairs (A,B) for which the theta^2 coordinate vanishes.

### 8.3 Polynomial, root, and inverse identities

The rational-root test shows that f_a is irreducible: its possible rational roots are 1 and -1, while

f_a(1)=-2a-1,

f_a(-1)=1.

For a>=2,

f_a(a+d)=-2a-1+(a^2+a-2)d+(2a+1)d^2+d^3.

Hence

f_a(a+1/a)=-a+1/a^2+1/a^3<0,

f_a(a+2/a)=1+4/a+4/a^2+8/a^3>0.

For z>=a, the derivative f_a'(z) is positive: f_a'(a)=a^2+a-2>0, and f_a''(z)=6z-2(a-1)>0. Thus there is exactly one root in that range and

a+1/a < theta < a+2/a < a+1

for a>2.

The identity

(z+1)^3 f_a(-1/(z+1)) = -f_a(z)

is obtained by multiplying out. Therefore the other two roots are

theta_1=-1/(theta+1),

theta_2=-(theta+1)/theta.

They satisfy

theta>0>theta_1>-1>theta_2.

In particular all three roots are real and distinct. The following inverse identities follow by using f_a(theta)=0:

theta^(-1)=theta^2-(a-1)theta-(a+2),

(theta+1)^(-1)=-theta^2+a theta+2.

Thus theta and theta+1 are units of R_a. The conjugate formulas can also be written within R_a:

theta_1=theta^2-a theta-2,

theta_2=-theta^2+(a-1)theta+(a+1).

Their norms are

Norm(theta)=1,

Norm(theta+1)=-1.

The product formula

Norm(x-y theta)=(x-y theta)(x-y theta_1)(x-y theta_2)=F_a(x,y)

follows by expanding the monic polynomial f_a. None of these identities uses reciprocity, ideal theory, or a unit theorem.

### 8.4 An elementary small-solution lemma

Write t=a-1 and

G_t(x,y)=x^3-tx^2y-(t+3)xy^2-y^3.

For t>=1, suppose x,y are integers with

y>0, -y/2<x<=y, |G_t(x,y)|=1.

Then either (x,y)=(0,1), or y>=t+3.

Proof. If x=0, G_t(0,y)=-y^3, so y=1.

If 1<=x<=y, use x^3<=xy^2 to obtain

G_t(x,y) <= -t x^2 y -(t+2)xy^2-y^3 <= -(2t+3)<-1.

This case is impossible.

If x<0, set b=-x and c=y. Then b>=1 and c>2b, hence c>=3. We have

G_t(-b,c)=tbc(c-b)+3bc^2-b^3-c^3.

The following two estimates are explicit:

b(c-b)-(c-1)=(b-1)(c-b-1)>=0,

3bc^2-b^3-(3c^2-1)=(b-1)[3c^2-(b^2+b+1)]>=0.

For the second inequality, b<c/2 and b>=1 imply b^2+b+1<=3b^2<3c^2. Thus if c<=t+2,

G_t(-b,c) >= t c(c-1)+3c^2-1-c^3

= c [c(t+3-c)-t]-1.

The function h(c)=c(t+3-c)-t is concave. On the interval [2,t+2], its endpoint values are

h(2)=t+2,

h(t+2)=2.

Since t>=1, h(c)>=2 throughout that interval. Therefore

G_t(-b,c)>=2c-1>=5,

again impossible. It follows that c=y>=t+3, proving the lemma.

### 8.5 Reduction to the small-solution cone

Define

R(x,y)=(y,-x-y).

Direct expansion gives

R^3=I,

F_a(R(x,y))=F_a(x,y).

Simultaneous negation changes the sign of F_a and hence preserves |F_a|.

Every nonzero integer pair can be transformed by one of the six maps ±R^j, j=0,1,2, into a pair (x',y') satisfying

y'>0, -y'/2<x'<=y',

y'<=|x|+|y|.

Here is a proof including the boundary cases. Let

H=max(|x|,|y|,|x+y|)>0.

The second coordinates of ±R^j(x,y) run through ±x, ±y, ±(x+y), so choose one with second coordinate H. The quantities |x|,|y|,|x+y| are permuted by these maps, and thus for the resulting pair (u,H),

|u|<=H, |u+H|<=H.

These inequalities imply -H<=u<=0. If u>-H/2, the pair already lies in the required cone. If u<=-H/2, apply -R^2:

-R^2(u,H)=(u+H,-u).

Now 0<=u+H<=-u, and 0<-u<=H, so this pair lies in the cone as well. In either case its second coordinate is at most H<=|x|+|y|. This proof handles y=0 and equality u=-H/2 without an omitted boundary.

Consequently, for a>=2, any solution of |F_a(x,y)|=1 with |x|+|y|<a+2 must lie in the six-element orbit

(1,0), (-1,0), (0,1), (0,-1), (1,-1), (-1,1).

Indeed its normalized second coordinate is less than a+2=t+3, so the small-solution lemma forces the normalized pair to be (0,1).

### 8.6 Reducing an arbitrary unit using only two explicit logarithm vectors

Let epsilon be any unit of R_a. Its three conjugates epsilon_0,epsilon_1,epsilon_2 are nonzero reals. Multiplication by epsilon on R_a is represented by an integer matrix whose inverse is also integral, so its determinant is ±1. Hence

log|epsilon_0|+log|epsilon_1|+log|epsilon_2|=0.

Put L=log(theta)>0 and M=log(theta+1)>L. The logarithm vectors of the two explicit units are

U=(L,-M,M-L),

V=(M,L-M,-L).

They belong to the real plane with coordinate sum zero. Their first-two-coordinate determinant is

L(L-M)+M^2=L^2-LM+M^2>0.

Thus U,V form a real basis of that plane. This is ordinary real linear algebra. It does not assert or use a general theorem about unit groups.

There are therefore real numbers s,t with

(log|epsilon_0|,log|epsilon_1|,log|epsilon_2|)=sU+tV.

Choose integers A,B nearest to s,t, and put

delta=epsilon theta^(-A)(theta+1)^(-B).

This is still a unit of R_a. Its logarithm vector is alpha U+beta V, with

|alpha|<=1/2, |beta|<=1/2.

It follows immediately that

|delta_0|<=sqrt(theta*(theta+1))<theta+1,

|delta_1|<=(theta+1)/sqrt(theta),

|delta_2|<=sqrt(theta+1).

For example, the second bound follows from

log|delta_1|=-alpha M+beta(L-M)

<= [M+(M-L)]/2=M-L/2.

The third follows from

log|delta_2|=alpha(M-L)-beta L<=M/2.

### 8.7 The reduced unit has no theta^2 coordinate

Write uniquely

delta=c_0+c_1 theta+c_2 theta^2,

with c_0,c_1,c_2 integers. The Lagrange interpolation formula for the degree-at-most-two polynomial c_0+c_1 z+c_2 z^2 yields

c_2 = delta_0/f_a'(theta) + delta_1/f_a'(theta_1) + delta_2/f_a'(theta_2).

The root separations satisfy

theta-theta_1=theta+1/(theta+1)>theta,

theta-theta_2=theta+1+1/theta>theta+1,

theta_1-theta_2=1+1/[theta(theta+1)]>1.

Consequently,

|c_2| < (theta+1)/[theta(theta+1)]

          + [(theta+1)/sqrt(theta)]/theta

          + sqrt(theta+1)/(theta+1)

< 1/theta + 2/sqrt(theta) + 1/theta^(3/2).

Since theta>a>=16,

|c_2| < 1/16+2/4+1/64=37/64<1.

As c_2 is an integer, c_2=0.

### 8.8 The remaining coordinates are small

We now have delta_i=c_0+c_1 theta_i. Subtracting the two small-conjugate equations and using theta_1-theta_2>1 gives

|c_1| < (theta+1)/sqrt(theta)+sqrt(theta+1)

< 2sqrt(theta)+2/sqrt(theta).

The last inequality follows from sqrt(theta+1)<sqrt(theta)+1/sqrt(theta), whose square on the right is theta+2+1/theta>theta+1.

Since |theta_1|=1/(theta+1),

|c_0| <= |delta_1|+|c_1|/(theta+1)

< sqrt(theta)+1/sqrt(theta)+2/sqrt(theta)

=sqrt(theta)+3/sqrt(theta).

Here the exact simplification used is

[2sqrt(theta)+2/sqrt(theta)]/(theta+1)=2/sqrt(theta).

Therefore

|c_0|+|c_1|<3sqrt(theta)+5/sqrt(theta)

<3sqrt(a+1)+5/sqrt(a)

<=3(a+1)/4+5/4

<a+1.

The penultimate inequality holds because a>=16 gives sqrt(a)>=4 and sqrt(a+1)<=(a+1)/4; the last inequality is equivalent to a+1>5.

Set x=c_0 and y=-c_1. The norm identity gives |F_a(x,y)|=1, and the preceding bound gives |x|+|y|<a+1<a+2. The small-solution result therefore gives

delta in {1,-1,theta,-theta,theta+1,-theta-1}.

Absorbing the indicated extra factor into A,B,s proves that every epsilon has the expression claimed in the uniform theorem in Section 8.2.

Uniqueness follows from the independence of U,V. If theta^A(theta+1)^B is ±1, its logarithm vector is zero, so A=B=0. The value is then 1, fixing the sign as well. This completes the full elementary proof.

### 8.9 Exact ghost matrices

In the basis (1,theta,theta^2), multiplication by theta is

M_a = [[0,0,1], [1,0,a+2], [0,1,a-1]].

Multiplication by theta+1 is I+M_a. Both lie in GL_3(Z); their determinants are 1 and -1, respectively. They commute. The inverse identities above become

M_a^(-1)=M_a^2-(a-1)M_a-(a+2)I,

(I+M_a)^(-1)=-M_a^2+aM_a+2I.

Let e_0=(1,0,0)^T and let mu(c_0,c_1,c_2)=c_2. Then, for every a>=16, the integer solutions of |F_a(x,y)|=1 are exactly the vectors

s M_a^A (I+M_a)^B e_0

with A,B in Z, s=±1, and mu=0; the first two coordinates are x,-y.

This is genuine uniform ghost data over Z[a]: every matrix entry, inverse identity, and marker is polynomial in a. The coverage proof is the uniform elementary argument just given. The remaining isolation problem has two ghost indices and one vanishing marker. At a smooth p-adic zero this is still one equation in two variables, so the theorem must not be advertised as a solution of the full Thue family.

## 10. What “parametric ghosts” should mean

### 10.1. A family of certified presentations

DEFINITION. An effective parametric ghost presentation over an explicitly specified set of integer parameters A consists of the following data, with every dependence on a in A stated:

1. Integral equations for X_a and finitely many cover equations Y_(a,d) -> X_a, with all exceptional specializations listed or characterized by explicit equations.
2. Explicit groups Gamma_a, actions, seeds, arithmetic predicates and coverage proofs as in Section 1.1. The number of generators and branches may be constant on a stated parameter stratum; if it varies, its variation must be controlled.
3. Identities over the parameter ring that prove the coordinate maps and reconstruction formulas after specialization.
4. Local charts and congruence certificates on stated parameter residue classes, including their denominator and bad-reduction conditions.
5. Real estimates with explicit dependence on a, together with a proof that their ranges cover all the allowed parameters and all unbounded ghost directions.

Uniformity means that one finite proof or a precisely bounded certificate-generating procedure treats the whole parameter set. It does not mean that a computer has verified many values of a.

There are useful intermediate levels of uniformity:

- **Uniform formulas:** the same algebraic maps and markers specialize correctly.
- **Uniform coverage:** every relevant solution in every fiber belongs to the displayed orbits.
- **Uniform local certificates:** finitely many parameter congruence cases have proved lifting or exclusion rules.
- **Uniform global completion:** those rules and real bounds cover every admissible ghost in every fiber.

The Thomas-family theorem in Section 8 reaches the second level, with exact matrices and two independent generators for every a>=16. Section 9 gives a precise additional certificate that would reach global completion for the Thue equation.

CAUTION. A formal module over Z[a] is normally infinitely generated as a Z-module. It cannot simply be substituted for the finite-rank Gamma in the compactness or local-rank statements. One can instead specialize an order O_a of fixed degree, specify a finite-rank module over each O_a, and prove the required basis and index statements uniformly. The base parameter and the group coordinates should remain separate in the notation.

Likewise, all power-sum polynomials S_k at once do not form a fixed-degree polynomial family in Z[k,n]. Their degree in n grows with k. For fixed n there is a finite exponential-polynomial presentation in k, but its number of summands grows with n. Any common finite-dimensional ghost model for unbounded n and k would itself be a substantial new theorem.

### 10.2. Why expansions in a alone do not classify unbounded exponents

PROVED LIMITATION OF A FIXED TRUNCATION. Suppose a positive unit or conjugate has been approximated by

    alpha(a)=alpha_N(a)*(1+epsilon(a)),  |epsilon(a)|<=1/2.

For an arbitrary integer exponent A, the exact ratio is

    alpha(a)^A/alpha_N(a)^A=(1+epsilon(a))^A.

The elementary inequality |log(1+x)|<=2|x| for |x|<=1/2 gives

    |(1+epsilon(a))^A−1| <= exp(2|A|*|epsilon(a)|)−1.

Proof of the logarithmic inequality: integrate 1/(1+t) between 0 and x, using |1/(1+t)|<=2 on that interval. The exponential bound follows from |exp(s)−1|<=exp(|s|)−1 for real s.

Thus a remainder epsilon(a)=O(a^(−N)) is not uniformly small after exponentiation over all A in Z. For a fixed a with epsilon(a)!=0, one sign of A makes the ratio grow without bound. With several units, the same issue involves sums of |A_i| times the corresponding logarithmic errors.

This explains precisely why a few excellent expansions of theta(a), theta(a)+1, or their logarithms do not by themselves solve the two-index marker equation. One needs at least one of:

- an independently proved bound on the exponent vector;
- an approximation order chosen as a function of its height, with a proved determinant/denominator separation, as in Section 6.4;
- a sign or dominance argument valid throughout the relevant cone;
- a norm or arithmetic-lattice gap that survives the actual growth of the other conjugates;
- a descent or a complete local covering that excludes the unbounded indices.

The obstacle is the lack of a uniform separation estimate, not a theorem saying “Baker is necessary.” The Padé route demonstrates why the latter assertion would be too strong.

### 10.3. Finite parameter congruences: what is enough

PROVED CERTIFICATE PRINCIPLE. Suppose all matrices, maps and marker expressions used in a fixed local certificate have coefficients integral at p after substituting a, and their reductions to the required finite precision depend only on a modulo p^h. Suppose the denominator nonvanishing and all higher-series tail estimates are proved uniformly on that parameter disc. Then checking the finite algebraic and residue conditions for each of its finitely many parameter residues proves that local certificate for every integer specialization in those discs.

Proof. The required finite reductions are identical on each stated residue class; the separately proved uniform tail estimate justifies discarding every higher term. The proof of the local lifting lemma therefore specializes without change.

This does not say that checking finitely many sample a-values verifies a real inequality for all a. Such an inequality needs a symbolic factorization, monotonicity argument, interval estimate with a proved tail, or another genuine proof on the unbounded parameter interval. Nor does it say that a fixed prime works at every specialization: prime divisors of a discriminant or denominator polynomial can vary with a.

TARGET. For each proposed family, produce an explicit finite partition of the parameter set into real ranges and congruence classes, and show that every part has the required coverage and separation certificates. A proof that a residue class is always nonsingular is useful; a proof that it contains no admissible orbit points is a different assertion.

## 11. Several commuting ghost directions: a joint interpolation theorem

This is the direct bridge from the one-index recurrence engine to structured multi-index ghosts. It does not require that the dynamics already be recognized as a torus or Jacobian.

PROVED THEOREM. Fix a prime p. Let H_1,...,H_r be commuting analytic maps on the closed unit polydisc Z_p^s, given by integral restricted power series, such that coefficientwise

    H_i(x)=x+p^(e_i) K_i(x),

where K_i has integral restricted coefficients, e_i is an integer, and e_i>1/(p−1). In particular e_i>=1 suffices for odd p, and e_i>=2 suffices for p=2. Let z be in Z_p^s, and let f be an integral restricted power series.

Then there is an integral restricted power series h in r variables satisfying

    h(t_1,...,t_r)=f(H_1^(t_1)...H_r^(t_r) z)

for every integer vector t. Here the inverse iterates are well defined. The resulting extension to Z_p^r is unique among continuous functions agreeing with the integer orbit.

More precisely, let U_i act on functions by U_i f=f composed with H_i, and put L_i=log U_i. If nu=(nu_1,...,nu_r) is a multi-index, the coefficient of t^nu is

    (L_1^(nu_1)...L_r^(nu_r) f)(z)/(nu_1!...nu_r!),

and has valuation at least

    sum_i [e_i*nu_i−v_p(nu_i!)].

Proof. Integral restricted series are 1-Lipschitz in the p-adic maximum norm. Solving H_i(x)=y by the contraction x=y−p^(e_i)K_i(x) gives an analytic inverse, as justified in Section 7.14. The operators U_i−I send integral restricted series into p^(e_i) times that ring. Therefore the operator logarithms

    L_i=sum_(j>=1) (−1)^(j+1) (U_i−I)^j/j

converge, and L_i increases coefficient valuations by at least e_i. Commutativity of the H_i implies commutativity of the U_i and L_i. The product of their exponential series is

    exp(t_1 L_1)...exp(t_r L_r)f,

with the displayed coefficients. Since v_p(n!)<=n/(p−1), the coefficient valuations tend to infinity with total degree under the strict inequality on e_i. Thus this is an integral restricted series. The convergent log/exp identities give U_i^(t_i) at every nonnegative integer t_i; the inverse identities give all integer t_i. Evaluation at z proves the formula. Finally Z^r is dense in Z_p^r, so continuity gives uniqueness.

COROLLARY WITH PROOF. Let f_1,...,f_k be markers vanishing at z. Their joint orbit pullbacks have the convergent expansions supplied by the theorem. If their actual lowest homogeneous parts pass the finite primitive test of Section 3.1, the zero is isolated on a specified p-adic ball. If one pullback has the norm-leading expansion of Section 3.2, it has the exact valuation asserted there. These statements follow by applying the proved jet theorems to the pullbacks; no unit theorem or rational-point finite-generation theorem is involved.

If an analytic involution sigma fixes z, satisfies sigma H_i sigma=H_i^(−1) for every i, and fixes every f_j, then each pullback satisfies

    h_j(−t)=h_j(t).

Proof. Apply the conjugation identity at integer indices and then use continuity. Comparing power-series coefficients shows that every term of odd total degree vanishes. An anisotropic quadratic or higher even-degree leading system can now be tested, but symmetry alone does not prove anisotropy. A nonzero kernel in the analytic orbit map remains a possible obstruction.

This formulation makes the roles clear: commutativity gives joint interpolation; symmetry removes specified degrees; module structure can constrain the surviving forms; a primitive-direction calculation proves isolation; a global cover proves completeness. None of those implications should be silently reversed.

### 12.1. The top theorem, in a form suitable for a paper

PROVED CONDITIONAL CERTIFICATE THEOREM: GAUGED-RETURN ARITHMETIC LIFTING.

Let an invertible homogeneous bilinear recurrence have state map R and diagonal gauges G_(A,B) as in Section 7.2. Fix an orbit state z_state=(tau_c,...,tau_(c+d−1)), a prime p, a block length N, p-units A,B, and an explicit regular p-adic chart iota sending a chart base point z to z_state and intertwining the normalized state return

    G_(A,B)^(−1) R^N

with a chart map H=I+p^e K having integral restricted coefficients, where the integer e satisfies e>=1 for odd p and e>=2 for p=2. Assume all displayed state evaluations are defined, and the chart contains the stated orbit.

Then:

1. The exact scalar gauge is C(t)=A^t B^(N*t*(t−1)/2), and the original first coordinate at index c+Nt equals C(t)*iota_0(H^t z). In particular the observable is the original state coordinate pulled back through the chart, not an arbitrarily chosen chart coordinate.
2. For an integral analytic observable f, the first finite-difference condition v_p((f composed with H−f)(z))=e gives the exact valuation e+v_p(t) for f(H^t z)−f(z), for every nonzero t in Z_p.
3. If an exact reversing involution fixes z and f, and v_p((U−I)^2 f(z))=2e, then the exact valuation is 2e−v_p(2)+2v_p(t).
4. A coordinate factor p^b adds b to these valuations. The precise coordinate formula and its integrality are part of the input.
5. For a raw property such as tau_(c+Nt)=w with w in Z_p and iota_0 integral restricted, one may pass to the finitely many residue classes and explicit augmented maps of Section 7.12. The raw equality is then an integral analytic marker on a near-identity map. Its own first or symmetric second finite difference, if it meets the preceding hypotheses, gives the corresponding exact valuation.
6. Several commuting certified returns admit the joint interpolation of Section 11 and the primitive-direction tests of Section 3.

The proof is supplied in Sections 7.2–7.7, 7.11–7.14 and 11. The conclusion is a theorem about any recurrence admitting these finite certificates, whether elliptic, genus 2, or not yet assigned an algebro-geometric interpretation. It does not assert that every periodic-quiver tau sequence admits them.

THE HARDEST APPLICATION LEMMA. For a proposed recurrence and arithmetic property, construct a finite collection of regular charts and gauged return maps covering every relevant residue branch, and prove that the observable used in the lifting calculation is exactly the original arithmetic condition. If some branches cannot be isolated, prove a different exclusion or a real bound for them. This is the point at which a generic interpolation theorem becomes a complete Diophantine method.

For primitive-denominator applications there is a second independent hard lemma: prove the reconstruction identity and gcd bound that force the relevant tau or theta-divisor quantities to divide a fixed integer. No amount of local recurrence interpolation supplies that arithmetic interpretation automatically.

### 12.2. Computations for Sol: exact inputs and stopping conditions

I would ask for five certificate-producing tasks, in the following order. Numerical experiments should be labelled experiments; successful tests become proofs only after the indicated identities or exhaustive finite enumerations are supplied.

**Task A: one recurrence, with the raw property retained.** Start with a known Somos-4 example from your completed proofs, then an elliptic Somos-5 example, then a genus-2 Somos-6 example with an explicitly verified translation model. Use a Somos-8 example only after its particular Jacobian model or regular return data has been supplied; an order-eight recurrence by itself does not certify genus 2.

For each, record the exact recurrence, rational seed, rational observable and reconstruction formula. At selected primes, output the block return, the gauges A,B, the order of the affine map after rescaling, and the coefficientwise near-identity congruence. Report the first and second finite differences of the actual marker at the required precision. A list of scalar recurrence values is insufficient to prove the return-map congruence.

At a modular zero, evaluate a cancelled Laurent block formula or change charts. Do not divide by a zero residue. For tau=±1 or tau dividing a fixed integer, carry the gauge via Section 7.12 and recompute the raw drift. Test the proposed involution by a rational identity, not by agreement on a handful of iterates.

Deliverables: a rational identity file, finite residue tables, the exact modulus needed, and a list of branches not yet covered. Stopping condition: every branch is either excluded, isolated with the correct arithmetic property, or explicitly marked as needing another engine.

**Task B: a genuinely multi-index singular example.** For two commuting unit matrices, translations or explicit endomorphisms, compute the actual orbit-marker expansion through its first surviving degree. First compute its Jacobian rank. If a smooth zero has fewer independent markers than analytic directions, label the branch NON-ISOLATED; do not search indefinitely for an anisotropic higher-order correction that contradicts the implicit-function theorem.

At singular symmetric states, factor the leading form modulo candidate primes. Look for the determinant norm of a finite-field extension, or produce valuation-initial-form cells with no common unit zero. Output the polynomial defining the extension, its finite irreducibility certificate, the linear map L and its rank, or the complete finite list of valuation cells and residue tests. Test zero-coordinate strata separately.

Deliverables: an explicit instance of Section 3.2 or 3.3 that was inaccessible to the old quadratic one-index lemma. This is a clearer theoretical success than a larger table of primes that fails to finish a smooth branch.

**Task C: Thomas-family coverage and the missing separator.** Verify symbolically the identities in Sections 8.3 and 8.9 for M_a and I+M_a. The mathematical coverage theorem is already proved for every a>=16; computation should now check transcription and identify useful local parameter classes.

For each chosen prime p, classify a modulo the precision needed for the matrices and marker, compute the finite orders of both unit matrices, and identify the local rank of the theta^2 marker at the known solutions. Preserve the two exponent coordinates. An auxiliary prime killing a particular residue branch is valuable; a large residue table with a persistent smooth branch is not a proof of global failure or success.

In parallel, extract explicit Padé numerator and denominator formulas from the cited hypergeometric method. Check their recurrence or finite-sum identity, determinant nonvanishing, denominator-clearing factors and real remainder bounds. The decisive numerical-symbolic target is exactly

    kappa(a)<2,
    [2K(a)/(a−1)]^(1/(2−kappa(a)))<a+2

for all a>=a0, with a proved bound on a0. If the estimates fail, report which of denominator growth, remainder decay or constants prevents the inequality. Do not compensate by unproved extrapolation from sampled parameters.

**Task D: one descent in an order without unique factorization.** Choose the ring and equation first. Give a Z-basis, multiplication table, norm, explicit bad integer C from Bezout/resultant identities, and a proposed finite reduction list. Ask the computation to produce transition identities and a decreasing integer height. The certificate must prove that every relevant factorization type reaches the finite list. A list of likely multipliers with no exhaustive reduction proof is not the requested theorem.

An alternative is one explicit non-2 endomorphism descent: output the rational maps, kernel and exceptional-point handling, every cover branch, and the exact height inequalities. The sought improvement is a new verified coverage mechanism, not a change of notation from 2 to m.

**Task E: audit the global cover.** For each finished application, maintain a finite graph of cases: cover branch, seed, real cone, residue class, local chart, arithmetic property, and certificate used. Verify every parent case is the union of its children. Terminal leaves must be known solutions, finite bounded checks, or sound exclusions. An unexplained surviving leaf is an unfinished proof, however many other leaves have been eliminated.

The output should distinguish three possibilities: an actual integer solution; a compatible local/adelic survivor; or a branch whose covering or precision proof is missing. These have very different mathematical meanings.

## 13. How to evaluate the theory across test equations

The strongest evaluation suite would deliberately require different modules.

| Evaluation | Capability being tested | What counts as progress in the theory |
|---|---|---|
| A completed rank-one elliptic example | Exact gauges, primitive denominators and raw-marker lifting | Recover the earlier proof as an instance of the return-chart theorem, with fewer special lemmas |
| A rank-two or totally real unit example | Joint interpolation and the smooth-rank obstruction | Exhibit a real separator, a new cover with a proved global lift, or a singular initial-form certificate; merely adding redundant markers does not count |
| A genus-2 Jacobian translation | Regular charts, higher-dimensional observables and theta-divisor denominators | Prove the recurrence/translation/reconstruction identities and one complete finite cover |
| Thomas's parametric cubic order | Uniform positive-rank orbit coverage and Archimedean approximation | Section 8 supplies coverage; a fully reproduced Section 9.4 certificate would supply a uniform complete application |
| An equation in a non-UFD order | Finite arithmetic branches and explicit power extraction | An exhaustive reduction certificate replacing the usually hidden ideal/class argument |
| A power-sum family | Variable degree, local parameter conditions, Runge and non-Runge ends | A proved uniform bound or exclusion family, with the residual parameter region stated exactly |

PROVED GEOMETRIC CAUTION. Passing to a finite cover that is étale near a given point does not change local dimension: its local branches map analytically isomorphically after choosing a lifted point over the relevant local field. Therefore a smooth positive-dimensional solution germ cannot become an isolated germ merely because the same equations were rewritten on that finite cover. A successful tower must bring genuinely new arithmetic restrictions, a different ambient orbit with a proved coverage relation, or ramified/singular behavior that is explicitly analyzed. An increased count of displayed equations is not by itself an increased count of independent restrictions.

The same caution applies to multiple isogenous curves. If their markers are rational pullbacks of one existing marker and have no independent arithmetic content, their simultaneous vanishing has the same germ. Separate coverings can be useful because global liftability, power classes and denominator conditions differ; that gain must be identified and proved.

BELIEF. The difficult region across these examples is the intersection of an unbounded real cancellation direction with a positive-dimensional local admissible germ and no available arithmetic height bound. It is here that the next mechanism must do one of three things: force a smaller discrete orbit by descent, produce a quantitative arithmetic gap by a determinant/norm construction, or prove that another globally necessary cover condition fails. More accurate local interpolation alone does not address that combination of obstructions.
