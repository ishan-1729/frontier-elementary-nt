## 0. What the proposed tower accomplishes

The square extraction over the Eisenstein integers is valid, but its coefficient is not automatically drawn from a fixed finite list: it contains a factor of the **variable** linear form `3x-y`. Keeping that coefficient, rather than discarding it, does yield an additional global inequality.

For a primitive solution
\[
F_7(x,y)=x^3-6x^2y-9xy^2-y^3=\epsilon,\qquad \epsilon=\pm1,
\]
with \(y\ne0\), put \(B=x-2y\). Define the **actual escaping part**
\[
H=\prod_{\substack{p^e\parallel |y|\\p\ne3,\ B\not\equiv\epsilon\ (\bmod p)}}p^e,
\qquad D=|y|/H.                                                   \tag{0.1}
\]
Every prime of \(H\) is \(1\pmod3\), but not every \(1\pmod3\) prime of \(y\) belongs to \(H\): a split prime can choose the rational branch. These definitions retain that distinction.

**PROVED:**
\[
\boxed{|y|\le6H^2,\qquad |y|<100000D^6.}                          \tag{0.2}
\]
The first inequality sharpens the rational-sign argument with the actual branch label. The second is the complementary result: it controls a solution when the part **not** escaping to the Eisenstein factors is small. It uses the full split-prime norm ledger, a nonzero integer coefficient, and a quadratic equation. No logarithmic estimate is used.

In particular the case \(D=1\), in which every prime-power block of \(y\) escapes, is completely classified by **68 quadratic equations**: it has only the trivial solutions. Thus the purely escaping case is no longer an open case of this contact method. What remains is a mixed allocation, not simply the name of a congruence class of primes.

More generally, with \(H_0=10^{100}\), an exact finite certificate proves:

> **PROVED regional Thomas theorem.** If one of the three cyclic presentations of a solution has \(y\ne0\) and \(\min(D,H)\le H_0\), then the solution is one of
> \(\pm(1,0),\pm(0,1),\pm(1,-1)\).

The new complementary inequality has an explicit uniform-in-\(a\) version in §3. It is a theorem for every parameter, not a completed classification of the whole family.

For S-003, the two rational factors of the quadratic contact already suffice to collect both sorts of split primes. Let \(D_a,H_a\) denote the **actual two sign parts**, defined in §5, of \(p=u-aw\), for \(a=0,1,2\). Then
\[
\boxed{|p|<4C_a\min(D_a,H_a)^3,\quad
(C_0,C_1,C_2)=(150,834,11422).}                                  \tag{0.3}
\]
The exact box calculation proves the known six points whenever one nonzero transverse coordinate has \(\min(D_a,H_a)\le10^{100}\). This includes arbitrarily many \(1\pmod4\) primes all choosing the previously escaping sign. Gaussian factorization refines that sign into two conjugate orientations; it does not force them to agree with the other rational sign.

Two additional conclusions explain precisely where the straightforward towers stop.

* **PROVED neutral return:** the bounded-factor cubic alternative for Thomas is
  \[
  (3+\omega)(x-y\omega)^3
   =21xy(x+y)+3\epsilon+\epsilon\omega.
  \]
  Factoring the associated equation \(Z^2+3=28Q^3\) and returning to this branch recovers the **same cubic form** and the **same norm** \(Q=x^2+xy+y^2\). It is a cover, not a descent in that norm.
* **PROVED restricted Gaussian obstruction:** the S-003 quartic has the explicitly defined coefficient invariant \(J=112750\ne0\). Consequently it is not a rational linear change of the real or imaginary coefficient of a single fixed Gaussian multiple of a fourth power. This obstructs that particular direct fourth-power analogue, not arbitrary nonlinear covers.

For S13, §6 proves two distinct difficulties. The quadratic order naturally attached to the residual norm is **not a unique-factorization domain**, with a concrete irreducible nonprime element. Independently, the residual polynomial has primes of exponent one outside every fixed finite set, even while the endpoint ledger \(4X+9=(2n+1)^2\) is retained. Splitting those primes does not turn them into cubes or fix their allocation between \(Y-A\) and \(Y+A\).

All assertions marked PROVED have elementary proofs below and exact certificates where finite enumeration is used. **UNPROVED** means an outstanding mathematical step, not a claim that the equation cannot be solved. No historical novelty is claimed for quadratic-ring factorization, covariants, or continued fractions. The particular complementary bounds and certificates are the outputs of this answer.

## 1. Quadratic-ring arithmetic used in the cover

### 1.1. Division, gcds, and unique factorization, proved here

Use the pairs \((a,b)\) to represent \(a+b\omega\), with \(\omega^2+\omega+1=0\). Then
\[
(a,b)(c,d)=(ac-bd,ad+bc-bd),\quad
\overline{(a,b)}=(a-b,-b),\quad N(a,b)=a^2-ab+b^2.
\]
For Gaussian integers replace the middle product term by \(ad+bc\), conjugate by \((a,-b)\), and use \(N=a^2+b^2\). Expanding four products proves multiplicativity of both norms. The norms are positive integers on nonzero integral pairs.

For nonzero \(\beta\), the quotient \(\alpha/\beta=\alpha\bar\beta/N\beta\) has rational coordinates. Round each to a nearest integer. The error coordinates \(r,s\) satisfy \(|r|,|s|\le1/2\). Thus
\[
r^2-rs+s^2\le3/4<1
\quad\hbox{or}\quad r^2+s^2\le1/2<1.
\]
Multiplying back by \(\beta\) gives an integral remainder of norm strictly less than \(N\beta\). This proves Euclidean division in both rings, without a geometric or analytic theorem.

Successive division terminates because the positive integer norm decreases. Back substitution gives a gcd as a linear combination. If an irreducible \(\pi\) does not divide \(a\), its gcd with \(a\) is a unit; a Bezout identity then shows that \(\pi\mid ab\) implies \(\pi\mid b\). Thus irreducibles are prime. Repeated proper factorization terminates by decreasing norm, and primality proves uniqueness by cancellation. This proves every unique-factorization assertion used below.

Solving \(N=1\), by completing a square, gives the six Eisenstein units and the four Gaussian units. Units can always be absorbed in the displayed coefficient \(\delta\); they must not be silently dropped.

### 1.2. Splitting detected by an actual residue root

Suppose an odd prime \(p\ne3\) and an integer \(B\) satisfy
\[
B^2+\epsilon B+1\equiv0\pmod p,
\qquad \epsilon=\pm1.
\]
Then \(\epsilon B\) has order three modulo \(p\), so \(p\equiv1\pmod3\). Here Fermat's congruence follows by multiplying the permutation of the nonzero residues induced by a unit. Bezout applied to exponents shows that an element of order three forces \(3\mid p-1\); no primitive-root theorem or reciprocity is needed.

There is an Eisenstein prime of norm \(p\) dividing \(B-\epsilon\omega\). Compute
\[
\pi=\gcd(p,B-\epsilon\omega).
\]
Evaluation \(\omega\mapsto\epsilon B\) modulo \(p\) is compatible with multiplication and kills both arguments, so a Bezout identity rules out a unit gcd. The gcd is not associate to \(p\), because the coefficient \(-\epsilon\) is not divisible by \(p\). Its norm divides \(p^2\), and hence is \(p\). In particular \(\pi\bar\pi=p\).

The two conjugate factors are relatively prime away from three:
\[
(B-\epsilon\omega)-(B-\epsilon\omega^2)
 =-\epsilon(\omega-\omega^2),\quad N(\omega-\omega^2)=3.             \tag{1.1}
\]
Thus a prime above \(p\ne3\) dividing one does not divide the other. This is the exact orientation needed for the square extraction.

Likewise, for an odd prime dividing \(Z^2+4\), the residue \(Z/2\) squares to \(-1\), forcing \(p\equiv1\pmod4\); the gcd of \(p\) and \(Z-2i\) has norm \(p\). The conjugate gcd is supported at two because their difference is \(4i\).

## 2. Thomas a=7: an exact cover and a complementary global bound

### 2.1. Separate the actual branches before extracting squares

Let \(F_7(x,y)=\epsilon\), \(y\ne0\), and put
\[
B=x-2y,\quad L=3x-y=3B+5y,\quad Y=|y|.
\]
Homogeneity shows \(\gcd(x,y)=1\). Direct expansion gives
\[
B^3-\epsilon=7y^2L,
\qquad F_7=B^3-21By^2-35y^3.                                  \tag{2.1}
\]
Use \(D,H\) from (0.1). They are positive, coprime, and \(DH=Y\).

If \(p\ne3\) divides \(y\), then \(B\) and \(L\equiv3B\pmod p\) are units. At the rational branch \(B\equiv\epsilon\pmod p\), the factor \(B^2+\epsilon B+1\) is a unit modulo \(p\), so
\[
p^{2v_p(y)}\mid B-\epsilon.
\]
At any other branch, (2.1) forces \(B^2+\epsilon B+1\equiv0\pmod p\), and §1.2 applies. In fact its valuation is exactly
\[
v_p(B^2+\epsilon B+1)=2v_p(y)+v_p(7).                           \tag{2.2}
\]

At three, if \(e=v_3(y)\ge1\), then \(B\equiv\epsilon\pmod3\) and
\[
B^2+\epsilon B+1=3(1+3\epsilon z+3z^2)
\quad\text{when }B=\epsilon+3z.
\]
The right side has valuation one. Since \(3\mid L\), (2.1) gives \(v_3(B-\epsilon)\ge2e\). This explains the compulsory assignment of three to \(D\).

Consequently
\[
\boxed{D^2\mid B-\epsilon,
\qquad H^2\mid B^2+\epsilon B+1.}                              \tag{2.3}
\]
Every prime of \(H\) is split, but a split prime on the rational branch belongs to \(D\). The distinction is essential to the new bound.

For each \(p^e\parallel H\), choose the oriented prime \(\pi_p\) of norm \(p\) dividing \(\alpha=B-\epsilon\omega\). Equation (2.2) and the conjugate gcd imply \(\pi_p^{2e}\mid\alpha\), including \(p=7\). Set
\[
\gamma=\prod_{p^e\parallel H}\pi_p^e,
\quad N\gamma=H,
\quad\delta=\alpha/\gamma^2\in\mathbb Z[\omega].                 \tag{2.4}
\]
This is an extraction of the content contributed by \(y\), not necessarily the maximal square extraction of \(\alpha\). Write
\[
d=N\delta=\frac{B^2+\epsilon B+1}{H^2}\in\mathbb Z_{>0},
\qquad c=\frac{B-\epsilon}{D^2}\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Taking norms and cancelling \(y^2=D^2H^2\) in (2.1) gives the additional equation
\[
\boxed{c d=7(3B+5y).}                                        \tag{2.5}
\]
The factor \(d\) is not disposable. Equation (2.5) is precisely the variable linear debt which a proposed bounded-factor cover must retain.

### 2.2. The complete inverse ledger

One exact polynomial cover is the following. Choose \(\epsilon,\eta\in\{\pm1\}\), \(D\ge1\), \(\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb Z[\omega]\), \(\gamma\ne0\), and \(c\in\mathbb Z\), satisfying
\[
\begin{aligned}
&H=N\gamma,\quad d=N\delta,\quad
\delta\gamma^2=B-\epsilon\omega,\\
&B-\epsilon=cD^2,\qquad cd=7(3B+5\eta DH).
\end{aligned}                                                  \tag{2.6}
\]
Recover
\[
y=\eta DH,\qquad x=B+2y.                                      \tag{2.7}
\]
Multiplying the norm of the first equation by the second gives
\[
B^3-\epsilon=(B-\epsilon)(B^2+\epsilon B+1)
 =7y^2(3B+5y).
\]
Thus (2.1) proves \(F_7(x,y)=\epsilon\). Conversely every original solution with \(y\ne0\) has the lift just constructed. Coprimality and the prime-orientation restrictions select canonical branches but are not needed for the soundness of this inverse implication.

This is a complete algebraic cover, **not yet a finite list of equations with coefficients from a fixed bounded set**. The quantitative next step tells exactly when its coefficient set becomes finite.

### 2.3. The norm coefficient is bounded in terms of D

**Lemma.** Every solution with \(y\ne0\) satisfies
\[
Y<|B|<6Y.                                                       \tag{2.8}
\]
If \(|B|\le Y\), the last term of (2.1) dominates:
\[
|F_7|\ge35Y^3-21|B|Y^2-|B|^3\ge13Y^3>1.
\]
If \(|B|\ge6Y\), divide the other two terms by \(|B|^3\). Their ratios decrease with \(|B|/Y\), and at six the lower bound is
\[
(216-126-35)Y^3=55Y^3>1.
\]
Both cases are impossible.

Since \(B,Y\) are integers, (2.8) implies
\[
Y^2<B^2+\epsilon B+1<36Y^2.
\]
Dividing by \(H^2\) yields
\[
\boxed{D^2<d<36D^2.}                                         \tag{2.9}
\]
If \(\delta=s+t\omega\), the inequality
\(s^2-st+t^2\ge3s^2/4,3t^2/4\) further gives
\[
|s|,|t|<7D.                                                     \tag{2.10}
\]
Thus bounding \(D\) gives an actual finite coefficient cover. There is no appeal to a class group or unit theorem in this assertion.

The rational marker is nonzero: if \(B=\epsilon\), (2.1) gives \(3B+5y=0\), impossible in integers. Also \(|B-\epsilon|\le6Y\). Therefore (2.3) gives
\[
D^2\le6Y,
\qquad\boxed{Y\le6H^2.}                                      \tag{2.11}
\]

### 2.4. The new complementary bound

Put \(s=D^2\). Equation (2.5), after multiplication by \(D^2\), becomes
\[
(d-21s)B-d\epsilon=35s y.                                     \tag{2.12}
\]
The norm relation is
\[
d y^2=s(B^2+\epsilon B+1).
\]
Eliminate \(y\) using these two exact identities:
\[
A_2 B^2+A_1 B+A_0=0,                                         \tag{2.13}
\]
where
\[
\begin{aligned}
A_2&=d(d-21s)^2-1225s^3,\\
A_1&=-\epsilon\{2d^2(d-21s)+1225s^3\},\\
A_0&=d^3-1225s^3.
\end{aligned}
\]

**The leading integer coefficient cannot vanish.** If it did, \(d/s\) would be a rational root of the monic polynomial
\[
f(Z)=Z^3-42Z^2+441Z-1225.
\]
A rational root of a monic integer polynomial is integral, by reducing its numerator and denominator. But
\[
f(m)\equiv m^3+m+1\equiv1\pmod2
\]
for every integer \(m\). Hence \(A_2\ne0\), and \(|A_2|\ge1\).

From (2.9), \(s<d<36s\) and \(|d-21s|<20s\). Thus
\[
|A_1|<53065s^3,
\qquad |A_0|<45431s^3.
\]
Since \(B\ne0\), (2.13) gives
\[
|B|\le |A_1|+|A_0|<98496s^3<100000D^6.
\]
Together with (2.8), this proves
\[
\boxed{|y|<100000D^6.}                                        \tag{2.14}
\]

This is an elementary global step beyond the one-sided contact in P17. It uses the **integrality** of the norm coefficient \(d\) and the exact reconstruction relation (2.5). Just bounding the absolute value of the Eisenstein square factor would not give it.

The degree-six exponent is not claimed optimal. The argument proves this explicit bound with no unproved prime-distribution, approximation, or rank input.

### 2.5. The purely escaping case is closed by 68 quadratics

If \(D=1\), (2.9) gives \(d=2,\ldots,35\). For each of these 34 integers and each \(\epsilon\), solve (2.13) by its integer discriminant. The leading coefficient is nonzero by the preceding parity argument. Test both possible integral roots, the divisibility in (2.12), and the original form.

The complete output is:

| d | epsilon | B | x | y |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 7 | -1 | 3 | 1 | -1 |
| 7 | -1 | -2 | 0 | 1 |
| 7 | 1 | 2 | 0 | -1 |

This finite certificate contains every integral root of every possible quadratic, not a search to a guessed bound. The omitted \(y=0\) branch has \(x=\pm1\). Hence no nontrivial solution can have all the prime-power content of a nonzero cyclic coordinate escaping from the rational branch.

### 2.6. A larger complete infinite region

The cyclic map \((x,y)\mapsto(y,-x-y)\) preserves \(F_7\); applying it three times gives the identity. Its second coordinates are, up to sign, \(y,x+y,x\). A common sign change changes \(\epsilon\) and \(B\) simultaneously and leaves the branch labels unchanged.

For \(H_0=10^{100}\), if \(\min(D,H)\le H_0\) in any cyclic presentation, (2.11)–(2.14) give
\[
|y|<10^{605}
\]
in that presentation. Section 7 supplies a complete exact continued-fraction exhaustion of this box. It has only the signed trivial solutions. This proves the regional theorem in §0.

It strictly extends the type of region in P17: \(H\le b_3(y)\), so the old bounded-\(b_3\) condition is retained, but now an unbounded escaping part is also covered whenever its complementary rational-branch part \(D\) is bounded.

The remaining possible solutions must have, in every nonzero cyclic coordinate,
\[
D,H>10^{100},\qquad
(Y/100000)^{1/6}<D\le\sqrt{6Y},\qquad
\sqrt{Y/6}\le H<100000^{1/6}Y^{5/6}.                           \tag{2.15}
\]
Equivalently one can raise these inequalities to integer powers; no real root computation is a premise. This is a precise residual mixed-allocation set, not a proof that it is empty.

## 3. A uniform complementary theorem for the Thomas family

The argument is not confined to parameter seven. The constants below are deliberately coarse but completely explicit.

Let \(a\ge1\), \(F_a(x,y)=\epsilon=\pm1\), \(y\ne0\), and define
\[
\Delta=a^2+a+7,\quad l=2a+1,\quad U=3x-(a-1)y.
\]
Expansion gives
\[
\boxed{U^3-27\epsilon=\Delta y^2(3U+l y).}                     \tag{3.1}
\]
Let \(H\) contain the full prime powers \(p^e\parallel |y|\) for which
\[
p\ne3,\qquad U\not\equiv3\epsilon\pmod p,
\]
and put \(D=|y|/H\). Such primes are \(1\pmod3\).

At every rational-branch prime other than three, the cofactor
\(U^2+3\epsilon U+9\) is a unit, so \(p^{2e}\mid U-3\epsilon\).
At three, \(U=3U_0\). For \(e=1\), the quantity \(3(U-3\epsilon)\) is already divisible by \(3^{2e}\). For \(e\ge2\), \(U_0\equiv x\equiv\epsilon\pmod3\), and dividing (3.1) by 27 gives
\[
v_3(U_0^3-\epsilon)\ge2e-1.
\]
The quotient by \(U_0-\epsilon\), when nonzero, has valuation one by the explicit calculation in §2.1. Hence \(3^{2e}\mid9(U_0-\epsilon)=3(U-3\epsilon)\); a zero marker is divisible as well. Thus
\[
D^2\mid3(U-3\epsilon).                                        \tag{3.2}
\]

For the escaping primes, Eisenstein orientation gives \(\gamma\) with \(N\gamma=H\) and
\[
\delta\gamma^2=3(U-3\epsilon\omega).
\]
The extra factor three pays only a fixed denominator/ramification cost. Put
\[
d=N\delta=\frac{9(U^2+3\epsilon U+9)}{H^2},\quad
c=\frac{3(U-3\epsilon)}{D^2}.
\]
Cancelling the exact product gives
\[
cd=27\Delta(3U+l y).                                          \tag{3.3}
\]
These equations, together with \(y=\eta D N\gamma\) and
\(x=(U+(a-1)y)/3\in\mathbb Z\), give the same complete reconstruction proof as §2.2. The divisibility by three in this last expression is part of the ledger.

For the constants, set
\[
\begin{aligned}
M&=9(4a+11)^2,\qquad E=27\Delta,\qquad J_0=729\Delta^2l^2,\\
K&=15M^3+6M^2E+12J_0,\\
\mathcal C(a)&=(M+E)K+3M.
\end{aligned}                                                  \tag{3.4}
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{|y|<\mathcal C(a)D^6.}                                 \tag{3.5}
\]

**Proof of the quantitative assertion.** Leading-term domination in \(F_a\) gives \(|x|<(a+3)|y|\): at \(a+3\) the difference between the leading cube and the absolute lower terms is
\[
(a+3)^3-(a-1)(a+3)^2-(a+2)(a+3)-1
=3a^2+19a+29>1,
\]
and the normalized lower-term ratios decrease thereafter. Therefore \(|U|<(4a+8)|y|\). With \(s=D^2\),
\[
0<d<Ms.
\]
Eliminating \(y\) from (3.3) and its norm relation gives
\[
A_2U^2+A_1U+A_0=0,
\]
where
\[
\begin{aligned}
A_2&=d(d-Es)^2-J_0s^3,\\
A_1&=-\epsilon\{6d^2(d-Es)+3J_0s^3\},\\
A_0&=9d^3-9J_0s^3.
\end{aligned}                                                  \tag{3.6}
\]
For clarity, the eliminated linear equation was
\[
(d-Es)U-3\epsilon d=9\Delta l s y.                            \tag{3.7}
\]

If \(A_2=0\), the positive rational number \(q=d/(9s)\) obeys
\[
q(q-3\Delta)^2=\Delta^2l^2.
\]
The denominator \(q-3\Delta\) is nonzero. Set
\(z=\Delta l/(q-3\Delta)\). Then \(z^2=q\) and
\[
z^3-3\Delta z-\Delta l=0.
\]
The identity
\[
27F_a((z+a-1)/3,1)=z^3-3\Delta z-\Delta l
\]
would give a rational root of the monic cubic \(F_a(X,1)\). Its only possible rational roots are \(\pm1\); their values are \(-2a-1\) and 1. This is impossible. Thus \(A_2\) is a nonzero integer.

If \(U\ne0\), (3.6) and the coefficient bounds give
\[
|U|\le |A_1|+|A_0|<K s^3.
\]
Equation (3.7), after using \(9\Delta l\ge1\), now gives
\[
|y|<\{(M+E)K+3M\}s^3.
\]
If \(U=0\), (3.1) says \(\Delta l y^3=-27\epsilon\). For \(a\ge2\), \(\Delta l>27\), impossible; for \(a=1\), \(|y|=1\), which satisfies (3.5). This completes the proof.

This is a uniform non-Runge content theorem. A fixed bound on \(D\) produces a finite coefficient cover and a finite endpoint bound for each \(a\). It does not bound \(D\) uniformly over all solutions or complete the family. No finite table in \(a\) is substituted for that missing statement.

## 4. Where the Eisenstein tower stops

### 4.1. Maximal square extraction leaves a variable factor

Instead of the \(y\)-directed extraction, factor \(\alpha=B-\epsilon\omega\) completely and extract its maximal square:
\[
\alpha=\delta_0\gamma_0^2,
\]
where \(\delta_0\) is squarefree as an Eisenstein element, with its unit included. Since no rational prime divides both coefficients of \(\alpha\), an inert rational prime cannot divide \(\alpha\). By (1.1) a split rational prime contributes only one orientation. At three the norm \(B^2+\epsilon B+1\) has valuation zero or one. Thus
\[
d_0=N\delta_0
\]
is an ordinary squarefree positive integer.

For an actual Thomas solution,
\[
\boxed{d_0\mid 7|3x-y|.}                                     \tag{4.1}
\]
At a prime other than three this follows from (2.1): if the prime occurs in the quadratic factor, it does not occur in \(B-\epsilon\), so an odd residual exponent must come from \(7L\), not \(y^2\). If three occurs, then \(B\equiv\epsilon\pmod3\); reducing the second equation of (2.1) modulo three forces \(3\mid y\), hence \(3\mid L\).

Equation (4.1) is not a fixed finite support statement. It is exactly the point where the proposed expression “bounded factor” needs an additional theorem.

Write \(\delta_0=s+t\omega\), \(\gamma_0=m+n\omega\). Comparing the two coefficients gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
B&=s m^2-2t mn+(t-s)n^2,\\
-\epsilon&=t m^2+2(s-t)mn-s n^2.
\end{aligned}                                                  \tag{4.2}
\]
The second binary quadratic has discriminant
\[
4(s^2-st+t^2)=4d_0.
\]
It is a Pell-type equation when \(d_0\) is nonsquare, not automatically a new cubic Thue equation with smaller height. The remaining linear form and the reconstruction of \(y\) still have to be enforced.

### 4.2. Constant imaginary coefficient does not bound the factor

There is no finite list of squarefree Eisenstein coefficients for all elements \(B-\omega\), even though their imaginary coefficient is fixed.

**Proof.** Given a finite set of ordinary primes, include three and form their product \(M\). The integer \(M^2+M+1>1\) is 1 modulo every prime in that set, so it has a prime divisor \(q\) outside the set. Such a prime is \(1\pmod3\). Its root is simple because a common root of \(X^2+X+1\) and \(2X+1\) would force \(q=3\). In
\[
f(M+Mq t)\equiv f(M)+Mq t f'(M)\pmod{q^2}
\]
there is exactly one forbidden residue of \(t\pmod q\) for which the valuation is at least two. Choose another. The new integer \(B\) still has no old prime dividing \(f(B)\), and \(q\) divides it to exponent one. Therefore the squarefree norm coefficient contains a prime outside the original list. This is a finite polynomial expansion and a finite choice, not a prime-distribution theorem. ∎

This does **not** assert that such \(B\)'s extend to Thomas solutions. It proves that fixed coefficient, pairwise gcd, and square extraction alone cannot justify a bounded factor on the necessary ambient locus.

Even a fixed small coefficient can leave infinitely many ambient points. Set \(\delta_0=2-\omega\), of norm seven. Then the constant-coefficient equation in (4.2) is
\[
m^2-6mn+2n^2=1.
\]
Starting at \((m,n)=(1,0)\), the integral move
\[
(m,n)\longmapsto(17m-6n,3m-n)                                 \tag{4.3}
\]
preserves it by expansion. Positive coordinates increase after the initial point. The resulting elements are
\[
(2-\omega)(m+n\omega)^2=B-\omega,
\quad B=2,653,165986,42159917,\ldots.
\]
Their norms are \(7(m^2-mn+n^2)^2\). They need not satisfy (2.5). For instance, at \((m,n)=(17,3)\), \(H=247\), \(B=653\), neither choice \(y=\pm247\) gives \(F_7=1\).

This identifies the necessary missing condition, rather than mistaking the Pell family for counterexamples to Thomas. The new bound in §2 succeeds precisely because it restores the full relation (2.5).

### 4.3. A bounded-factor cubic cover exists, but its natural return is neutral

Put
\[
P=xy(x+y),\quad Q=x^2+xy+y^2,\quad Z=42P+5\epsilon.
\]
The coefficientwise identity
\[
\boxed{(3+\omega)(x-y\omega)^3=21P+3F_7+F_7\omega}             \tag{4.4}
\]
gives, at \(F_7=\epsilon\),
\[
\frac{Z+\epsilon(1+2\omega)}2
 =(3+\omega)(x-y\omega)^3,
\qquad Z^2+3=28Q^3.                                           \tag{4.5}
\]
Here \(Z\) is odd and \(Z\equiv5\epsilon\pmod{42}\). These are parts of the original-point ledger.

The norm equation itself has a bounded-factor cubic extraction. Let
\(\alpha=(Z+\epsilon(1+2\omega))/2\). If \(3\mid Z\), then \(v_3(Z^2+3)=1\), incompatible with \(28Q^3\); hence three does not divide \(Q\). The difference of \(\alpha\) and its conjugate has norm three, so they are relatively prime. At every prime away from seven their prime-element exponents are multiples of three; at seven the nonzero orientation has exponent \(1\pmod3\). Thus
\[
\alpha=\xi\pi\gamma^3,
\quad N\pi=7,\quad N\gamma=Q,
\]
where \(\xi\) is one of six units and \(\pi\) is one of the two orientations above seven. This gives a finite list of coefficient equations, together with the inverse restrictions. It uses only §1's factorization proof.

But the branch explicitly furnished by every original point is \(\pi=3+\omega\), \(\xi=1\), \(\gamma=x-y\omega\). Expanding the coefficient of \(\omega\) gives
\[
[\omega](3+\omega)(m+n\omega)^3
 =m^3+6m^2n-9mn^2+n^3=F_7(m,-n).                              \tag{4.6}
\]
It is the original equation again. On that branch the recovered cube root differs from \(x-y\omega\) only by \(1,\omega,\omega^2\), since their cubes agree. All these possibilities have the identical norm \(Q\). There is no strict descent in \(Q\), and taking the positive cube root of the rational norm merely changes \(7Q^3\) back to the same old \(Q\).

The same phenomenon holds uniformly. Direct multiplication gives
\[
\boxed{((a+2)+3\omega)(x-y\omega)^3
 =\Delta xy(x+y)+(a+2)F_a+3F_a\omega.}                         \tag{4.7}
\]
Consequently
\[
\{2\Delta xy(x+y)+(2a+1)F_a\}^2+27F_a^2
 =4\Delta(x^2+xy+y^2)^3.
\tag{4.8}
\]
The coefficient of \(\omega\) in the direct cube branch is \(3F_a\). Dividing the known coefficient back out returns the same form and same norm.

This is a proved **neutral loop in the specified tower**, not a theorem that no descent through another cover can exist. To use this finite cubic cover for completion, one needs a new separator for one of its remaining cubic forms, or a mark-preserving move that changes the norm. Renaming the cubic variables is not that move.

### 5.1. The complete two-sign allocation gives a symmetric bound

If \(p=0\), the point is already \(\pm(a,1)\). Otherwise the pair \((p,q)\) is primitive. Neither \(B_a-1\) nor \(B_a+1\) can vanish: the residual would force its linear factor to vanish. The primitive residual-zero vectors, up to common sign, are

and their original norm values are respectively

130321,\quad3347663881,\quad22971045324058561921,

not one.

At an odd prime \(l\mid p\), the residue \(q\) is a unit and \(q^4=1\). Therefore \(B_a\equiv q^2=\pm1\pmod l\). Assign the full \(l^{v_l(p)}\) to \(D_a\) for the plus sign, and to \(H_a\) for the minus sign. The minus sign requires \(l\equiv1\pmod4\). Assign the entire binary part to \(D_a\). Then
\[

and
\[
\boxed{D_a^3\mid4(B_a-1),\qquad H_a^3\mid4(B_a+1).}             \tag{5.1}
\]
For odd primes this follows from the contact product and the fact that its two factors have gcd dividing two. For the prime two, write \(e=v_2(p)\ge1\); \(q\) and the coefficient of \(pq\) are odd. If \(e=1\), then \(B_a\equiv3\pmod4\), so \(v_2(4(B_a-1))=3\). If \(e\ge2\), then \(B_a\equiv1\pmod4\), while the product has valuation at least \(3e+2\), so \(v_2(B_a-1)\ge3e+1\). This proves the fixed binary cost in every case.

At the stated \(K_a\), the leading fourth power exceeds the sum of the absolute lower terms by at least two. Dividing by \(|q|^4\) proves the domination for every larger ratio. The displayed \(C_a\)'s are exactly

### 5.2. What a literal Gaussian square cover produces

Write \(B_a=q^2+c_apq+d_ap^2\), and define \(Z=2q+c_ap\). Completing the quartic, not just its leading term, gives
\[
16F(aq+p,q)=Z^4-p^2G_a(p,Z),
\]
where

| a | c_a | G_a(p,Z) |
|---:|---:|---|
| 0 | -3 | 170Z^2+480pZ+295p^2 |
| 1 | 11 | 650Z^2-8800pZ+33095p^2 |
| 2 | -41 | 10730Z^2+604960pZ+9592295p^2 |

Thus, at a solution,
\[
(Z-2)(Z+2)(Z-2i)(Z+2i)=p^2G_a(p,Z).                           \tag{5.3}
\]
The pairwise Gaussian gcds of the four factors are supported at two; their differences are fixed Gaussian integers with norms powers of two. This is a genuine factor cover with bounded common content.

Extract a maximal square from \(Z-2i\):
\[
Z-2i=\delta\gamma^2,
\quad\delta=s+ti,\quad\gamma=m+ni.
\]
For odd primes, only one orientation occurs; the coefficient two rules out any odd inert prime. The exponent of \(1+i\) in \(Z-2i\) is zero when \(Z\) is odd, two when \(Z=2r\) with \(r\) even, and three when \(r\) is odd. It follows that \(d=N\delta\) is an ordinary squarefree integer and
\[
\boxed{d\mid2|G_a(p,Z)|.}                                    \tag{5.4}
\]
For odd primes this follows from
\((Z^2-4)(Z^2+4)=p^2G_a\) and \(\gcd(Z^2-4,Z^2+4)\mid8\). The binary part costs at most the single factor two in the squarefree norm.

Here \(G_a\ne0\) on a nonzero integral pair: its discriminants as a quadratic form are
\[
29800,\quad-8607000,\quad-45724699800.
\]
The first lies strictly between \(172^2\) and \(173^2\), and the other two are negative. Thus there is no nonzero rational zero.

The coefficient equations are
\[
Z=s(m^2-n^2)-2tmn,
\quad t(m^2-n^2)+2smn=-2.                                    \tag{5.5}
\]
Their discriminant is \(4(s^2+t^2)=4d\). The full inverse ledger also requires (5.3),
\[
Z-c_ap\equiv0\pmod2,\qquad q=(Z-c_ap)/2,\qquad u=aq+p.
\]
With these retained, the cover is exact. Without them, (5.5) is only a Pell-type relaxation with a variable coefficient whose prime support comes from \(G_a\).

For a concrete calibration, \(\delta=1+2i\) has norm five. The equation
\[
m^2+mn-n^2=-1
\]
has the infinite integer orbit
\[
(m,n)=(0,1),\quad(m,n)\mapsto(m+n,m+2n).
\]
Expansion preserves the equation and the coordinates grow. Along it
\[
(1+2i)(m+ni)^2=Z-2i,
\quad Z=-1,-11,-76,-521,\ldots.
\]
These points do not automatically solve (5.3) for an integral \(p\). They prove that the constant imaginary coefficient in the Gaussian cover is not by itself a discrete squeeze excluding large points.

As in §4.2, the squarefree coefficient has unbounded prime support on the ambient family \(Z-2i\): take \(Z=2M\) with \(M\) divisible by any prescribed finite set of primes and by two. A prime of \(M^2+1\) is outside that set and is \(1\pmod4\); a finite simple-root adjustment makes its valuation exactly one. Again the statement is about the ambient factorization, not the actual Thue solution set.

### 5.3. Why a direct fixed Gaussian fourth-power coefficient cannot replace this cover

For
\[
f(X,Y)=aX^4+bX^3Y+cX^2Y^2+dXY^3+eY^4,
\]
define the explicit cubic coefficient expression
\[
J(f)=72ace+9bcd-27ad^2-27b^2e-2c^3.                            \tag{5.6}
\]
Two finite algebra facts suffice:

1. \(J(f\circ M)=(\det M)^6J(f)\) for a rational two-by-two matrix \(M\).
2. The real or imaginary coefficient of \((r+si)(X+iY)^4\) has \(J=0\).

Here is a proof not importing invariant theory. Under diagonal substitution the five coefficients acquire factors \(\lambda^4,\lambda^3\mu,\lambda^2\mu^2,\lambda\mu^3,\mu^4\); every monomial in (5.6) has weight \(\lambda^6\mu^6\). Swapping \(X,Y\) interchanges \(a,e\) and \(b,d\), leaving (5.6) unchanged. Under \(X\mapsto X+tY\) the coefficients are
\[
(a,b+4at,c+3bt+6at^2,d+2ct+3bt^2+4at^3,
 e+dt+ct^2+bt^3+at^4).
\]
Substitution in (5.6) leaves \(J\) unchanged. For complete finite certification of this last identity, the difference is of degree at most three in each of \(a,b,c,d,e\), and at most twelve in \(t\). The verifier checks the entire grid
\[
(a,b,c,d,e)\in\{0,1,2,3\}^5,\qquad t\in\{0,\ldots,12\}.
\]
Successive use of the polynomial root bound proves the identity from all 13,312 exact evaluations. This is an exhaustive polynomial-identity certificate, not a sample of parameters. Elementary row elimination decomposes every invertible rational matrix into diagonal matrices, swaps, and these shears (lower shears are conjugated by a swap). The singular case follows as a polynomial identity, or is unnecessary for the application.

For the second fact the real coefficient has vector
\[
(r,-4s,-6r,4s,r).
\]
Direct substitution gives zero; the imaginary coefficient is another choice of \(r,s\). The accompanying finite cubic-degree grid verifies this polynomial identity as well. Multiplying the form by a rational scalar preserves the assertion \(J=0\).

But for S-003,
\[
J(1,-6,-29,54,-19)=112750\ne0.                                 \tag{5.7}
\]
Therefore no nonsingular rational linear change of \((u,w)\) turns its form into one coefficient of a fixed Gaussian multiple of a fourth power. Unlike (4.4), that simple fourth-power return cannot be the whole equation. A successful construction must add a variable coefficient, another equation, denominators with an exact ledger, or a genuinely nonlinear cover.

This obstruction concerns exactly the stated ansatz. It is not an impossibility theorem for all Gaussian approaches or all contact towers.

## 6. S13: the quadratic residual and the precise extraction obstruction

The accepted two-anchor presentation is
\[
X=(n-1)(n+2),\quad Y=630r,
\]
\[
A(X)=517X^2+1050X+630,\quad
C(X)=113400X^2+205211X+328020,
\]
\[
Y^2-A(X)^2=X^3C(X),\qquad
630\mid Y,\quad4X+9=(2n+1)^2.                                 \tag{6.1}
\]
Its universal identity is \(3780P(X+2)-A^2=X^3C\), with \(P\) the quintic in the earlier S13 cover. The new script rechecks every coefficient, the fixed gcd Bezout certificate, and
\[
453600C(X)=(226800X+205211)^2+K,
\quad K=106678317479=53\cdot23663\cdot85061.                    \tag{6.2}
\]

### 6.1. The natural quadratic order is not a Euclidean UFD

Put \(\lambda=(1+\sqrt{-K})/2\). Since \(K\equiv7\pmod8\),
\[
\lambda^2-\lambda+c=0,\qquad c=(K+1)/4=26669579370
\]
with \(c\) even. In the order \(\mathcal O=\mathbb Z[\lambda]\),
\[
N(a+b\lambda)=a^2+ab+cb^2
 =(a+b/2)^2+Kb^2/4.                                          \tag{6.3}
\]
There is no element of norm two: for \(b\ne0\) the last bound exceeds two, while for \(b=0\) the norm is an integer square. Therefore the element 2, whose norm is four, is irreducible. Any factorization into nonunits would require two elements of norm two.

However
\[
\lambda(1-\lambda)=c\in2\mathbb Z,
\]
and neither \(\lambda\) nor \(1-\lambda\) is divisible by 2, as is immediate from their two integer coefficients. Thus 2 is **irreducible but not prime**. This directly disproves unique factorization in this order, and hence the Euclidean strategy of §1 cannot be transferred to it.

There is also an explicit failure of the norm division test: the imaginary part of \(\lambda/2-(a+b\lambda)\) has absolute value at least \(\sqrt K/4\), so its norm is at least \(K/16>1\) for every integral \(a,b\). No quotient when dividing \(\lambda\) by 2 has a smaller-norm remainder.

Passing to the usual half-integral order has already been done here. In fact \(K\) is squarefree (the three factors above are prime, checked by trial division), and this is the full quadratic integer ring. A finite denominator proof is available: write an integral element as \((a+b\sqrt{-K})/d\) in primitive integer coordinates. Its monic quadratic minimal polynomial has integer trace and norm coefficients by the elementary primitive-polynomial lemma. Thus \(d\mid2a\) and \(d^2\mid a^2+Kb^2\). An odd prime of \(d\) would divide both \(a,b\), using squarefreeness of \(K\), a contradiction. If \(4\mid d\), the same contradiction follows modulo four. Hence \(d=1\) or 2, and in the latter case \(a,b\) are odd, giving an element of \(\mathbb Z[\lambda]\). The primitive-polynomial lemma follows by reducing primitive products modulo each prime: a product of two nonzero polynomials over a field is nonzero, so a prime cannot divide every product coefficient unless it divides every coefficient of one factor. This is the usual finite content proof, not a class-number input.

An explicit multiplication-lattice allocation could replace unique factorization, as in earlier programme work. The statement here is not that such a replacement is impossible. It is that **element factorization in this actual quadratic ring is not justified by the Eisenstein/Gaussian Euclidean argument**.

### 6.2. The residual has unbounded first-power primes even on the endpoint ledger

Independently of the preceding ring obstruction, \(C(X)\) is not a bounded coefficient. Let \(v=n-1\), so \(X=v(v+3)\), and define
\[
g(v)=C(v(v+3))
 =113400v^4+680400v^3+1225811v^2+615633v+328020.
\]
The following exact integer Bezout identity certifies its simple-root property:
\[
U(v)g(v)+V(v)g'(v)=187917163267100349,                          \tag{6.4}
\]
where coefficients in increasing order are
\[
\begin{aligned}
U&=(1049460936716,830330222400,276776740800),\\
V&=(-253928904387,-480659769658,-311373833400,-69194185200).
\end{aligned}
\]
The script checks this by multiplication. A formal derivative of this finite polynomial is just coefficient arithmetic.

**PROVED:** for every finite set of primes there is \(n>1\) such that \(C((n-1)(n+2))\) has a prime outside that set to exponent exactly one.

**Proof.** Enlarge the set to contain the prime divisors of the nonzero constant in (6.4) and \(c_0=328020\). Let \(M\) be its prime product and take \(v=c_0M\). Then \(g(v)/c_0\) is an integer greater than one and is 1 modulo every prime of \(M\). Choose one of its prime divisors \(p\), outside the set. Equation (6.4) makes \(g'(v)\) a unit modulo \(p\). In
\[
g(v+c_0Mp t)\equiv g(v)+c_0Mp t g'(v)\pmod{p^2},
\]
exactly one value \(t\pmod p\) gives valuation greater than one. Choose another nonnegative value. Set \(n=1+v+c_0Mp t\). The claim follows, and the endpoint relation \(4X+9=(2n+1)^2\) holds identically. ∎

For a small check, at \(n=2\), \(X=4\) and
\[
C(4)=2963264=2^6\cdot46301.
\]
Further exact factorizations are recorded in the verifier output. The proof above, not that table, proves the unbounded-support assertion.

These \(n\)'s are **not asserted to be square-sum solutions**. The theorem proves that the endpoint ledger and the residual norm identity do not themselves give a bounded support for the coefficient being allocated. A theorem excluding these primes on actual square-sum solutions would be an additional global input.

### 6.3. Splitting orientation is not the sign allocation that is missing

Outside the fixed primes dividing the common-content constant and \(328020\), a prime of \(X\) does not divide \(C(X)\). Its contribution to the product in (6.1) has exponent \(3v_p(X)\), and it can be assigned wholly to one of \(Y-A\), \(Y+A\). A good prime of \(C(X)\), by contrast, has an exponent not forced to be a multiple of three. If its exponent is one, it must remain in a residual coefficient of one of these factors after extracting cubes.

In (6.2), a good rational prime of \(C(X)\) splits between the two conjugate factors
\[
226800X+205211\pm\sqrt{-K}.
\]
That orientation depends on \(X\). It does not say whether the **rational** prime divides \(Y-A\) or \(Y+A\). Those are different allocation questions. At a prime where \(A\) is a unit, either sign \(Y\equiv\pm A\) is possible in the contact congruence. Independent choices at distinct good primes combine by the ordinary Chinese remainder construction. Earlier P17 already exhibited such a finite sign-independence certificate; the distinction remains after the residual is written as a quadratic norm.

Thus the exact obstruction to the proposed direct extraction is:

1. no Euclidean element factorization in the actual quadratic order;
2. even with a correct arithmetic cover replacing it, the residual contributes first-power primes outside every fixed set on the endpoint locus;
3. conjugate orientation of those primes does not supply the missing coupling to the signs of \(Y\pm A\).

The power \(X^3\) is not evidence for a cubic-field cover with bounded coefficient: the variable \(C(X)\) remains in the product. No rank-one conclusion, removal of a Selmer-type coset, or unrestricted S13 theorem follows from these factor identities alone.

## 7. The finite exhaustions are independent of forbidden height bounds

This section proves the finite reduction used in §§2.6 and 5.1. It neither imports the earlier logarithmic completions nor assumes their height cutoffs.

### 7.1. Exact algebraic root intervals

For
\[
f_3(t)=t^3-6t^2-9t-1,
\quad f_4(t)=t^4-6t^3-29t^2+54t-19,
\]
integer/rational signs and IVT give one root in each of
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
f_3:&(-2,-1),\ (-1/8,-1/9),\ (7,8),\\
f_4:&(-5,-4),\ (0,1/2),\ (1,2),\ (8,9).
\end{array}                                                    \tag{7.1}
\]
Their number is the degree, so these are all the roots and each is simple. The rational-root test, at \(\pm1\) and \(\pm1,\pm19\) respectively, excludes rational roots.

For a real number \(z\), choose a nearest root \(\beta\). Its distance from every other root is at least half that other root's distance from \(\beta\), by the triangle inequality. The interval gaps in (7.1) give products of these other distances at least \(14/9\) for the cubic and \(15/8\) for the quartic. The verifier checks every row of these finite rational products.

At a solution, therefore,
\[
|x/y-\beta|\le9/(14|y|^3),
\qquad |w/u-\beta|\le8/(15|u|^4),                              \tag{7.2}
\]
respectively. For denominators at least two, both are strictly less than \(1/(2q^2)\). Primitivity of each fraction follows from the original homogeneous norm being one in absolute value. Denominator-one and zero cases are treated directly.

### 7.2. The finite continued-fraction criterion

For completeness, if \(\alpha\) is irrational, \(\gcd(p,q)=1\), \(q>0\), and \(|\alpha-p/q|<1/(2q^2)\), then \(p/q\) is a convergent.

Define the complete quotients by floor, subtraction and reciprocation, and define \((p_j,q_j)\) by their finite two-term recurrence. Induction gives adjacent determinant \(\pm1\), unbounded denominators (\(q_{j+2}\ge q_{j+1}+q_j\)), and opposite signs of adjacent errors \(\delta_j=p_j-q_j\alpha\). The sign assertion follows by reversing the finite substitutions in
\[
\alpha=(p_j\alpha_{j+1}+p_{j-1})/(q_j\alpha_{j+1}+q_{j-1}).
\]
Choose the last \(j\) with \(q_j\le q<q_{j+1}\). If \(p/q\ne p_j/q_j\), write
\[
(p,q)=A(p_j,q_j)+B(p_{j+1},q_{j+1})
\]
in the adjacent unimodular basis. The integers \(A,B\) are nonzero and have opposite signs: equal positive signs contradict \(q<q_{j+1}\), and equal negative signs contradict \(q>0\). Hence \(|p-q\alpha|\ge|\delta_j|\).

But the proposed approximation is strictly better in that error than any distinct fraction \(r/s\), \(0<s\le q\). Otherwise
\[
1\le|ps-qr|\le s|p-q\alpha|+q|r-s\alpha|
 <(s+q)/(2q)\le1,
\]
a contradiction. Apply this to \(p_j/q_j\). This proves the criterion with finite identities and inequalities. No analytic continued-fraction theorem is used.

It subtracts that integer and reverses endpoints on reciprocation. Thus every emitted quotient is certified for the actual root. It stops only after a denominator exceeds the required box. Insufficient precision causes a complete restart at doubled precision; no such restart was needed here.
