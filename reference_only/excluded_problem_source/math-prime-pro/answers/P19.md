## 0. Results, and the distinction that matters

A growing denominator is a **cost**, not an advantage by itself. It makes the rational grid finer. What can make it useful is an arithmetic orbit on which the approximation error shrinks faster than that cost, or a cover which first removes the nonsquare leading coefficient. A second, different use of an orbit is to cancel its leading growth between **two** putative hits, leaving an integer norm defect. Both mechanisms produce results below.

The strongest completed evaluations in this answer are these. Write
\(T=n(n+1)\), and let \(\operatorname{sf}(v)\) be the positive squarefree kernel of a positive integer \(v\), so \(v=\operatorname{sf}(v)a^2\).

   \operatorname{sf}(T-2)=14,

   \]
   A deliberately coarse, independently proved Runge bound and nineteen elementary quadratic covers reduce the assertions to respectively **95,565** and **122,456** labelled orbit rows. Every row is checked exactly. These are not classifications of the complementary square classes.

## 1. Orbit squeezes with a denominator and a contact ledger

### 1.1. Integer-gap theorem on an arbitrary covered orbit

**PROVED.** Suppose a covering construction sends every candidate solution to an index \(s\) and an integer \(w\ge0\) with
\[
w^r=N_s,\qquad r\ge2.
\]
For that index, suppose \(D_s,P_s,L_s\) are positive integers, and exact transport of the arithmetic marker proves
\[
L_s\mid D_sw-P_s.
\tag{1.1}
\]
Define
\[
E_s=D_s^rN_s-P_s^r.
\]
If
\[
0<|E_s|<L_sP_s^{r-1},
\tag{1.2}
\]
that index contains no solution.

**Proof.** The nonzero integer \(D_sw-P_s\) is a multiple of \(L_s\). Factoring the difference of powers gives
\[
|E_s|=|D_sw-P_s|
\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}(D_sw)^iP_s^{r-1-i}
\ge L_sP_s^{r-1},
\]
contrary to (1.2). ∎

There is no requirement that \(D_s,P_s\), or \(N_s\) be polynomials in \(s\), or that the denominator be bounded. There **is** a requirement to prove the integer membership and (1.1). Without a contact divisor, use \(L_s=1\).

A sharper signed version is often preferable. If \(P_s>L_s\), (1.1) holds, and
\[
(P_s-L_s)^r<D_s^rN_s<P_s^r,
\tag{1.3}
\]
then the integer \(D_sw\), constrained to \(P_s+L_s\mathbb Z\), cannot occupy that interval. The analogous upper interval is equally valid. Non-strict bounds leave the appropriate endpoint equations; they cannot be discarded.

The bounded-candidate version is also exact. If \(|E_s|<K L_sP_s^{r-1}\), then
\[
D_sw-P_s=jL_s,\qquad |j|<K.
\]
There are finitely many values of \(j\) **per index**. This does not make an unbounded set of indices finite.

### 1.2. The precise denominator-versus-error test

Let \(q_s=P_s/D_s>0\), and suppose finite identities and inequalities prove
\[
|N_s^{1/r}-q_s|\le A\rho^s,
\quad D_s\le B\sigma^s,
\quad L_s\ge C\tau^s,
\]
with positive constants and \(\rho\sigma/\tau<1\). Assume also that the difference is nonzero, and that (1.1) is retained at any solution. Then every index with
\[
(AB/C)(\rho\sigma/\tau)^s<1
\tag{1.4}
\]
is excluded.

Indeed \(|D_sw-P_s|/L_s<1\), contrary to its being a nonzero integer. When the constants are rational, the first index satisfying (1.4) can be found by successive rational multiplication. This terminates: for \(0<t<1\), finite Bernoulli gives \(t^{-j}\ge1+j(t^{-1}-1)\), which eventually exceeds any specified positive rational. Algebraic constants can instead be replaced by certified rational bounds preserving the strict rate inequality.

The relevant quantity is therefore **error times denominator divided by the proved contact divisor**. Increasing the denominator without improving anything else makes the test worse. If the displayed rate is at least one, this particular estimate fails; that is not a theorem that no better estimate or centre can exist.

### 1.3. An algebraic lattice requires all its embeddings

If a marker lies in an explicitly given degree-\(d\) integral order, a nonzero multiplication determinant is a nonzero integer. Consequently a product of bounds on **all** its conjugates that is strictly less than one excludes it. A small value at one real embedding is not enough.

This assertion needs no theorem about ideals. In an integral basis closed under multiplication, multiplication by an integral element is an integer matrix. Its determinant is nonzero in a field, and is the product of its conjugate values; alternatively that determinant itself can be bounded directly. Section 7 exhibits the exact constant determinants that prevent the simplest algebraic centres from giving the desired contradiction.

### 1.4. Finite cancellation of leading orbit modes

**PROVED finite-annihilator lemma.** Let \(z_{s+i}\) be integers at a set of alleged hits, \(a_{s+i}\) real comparison values, and \(c_0,\ldots,c_h\) integers. If
\[
\sum_{i=0}^h c_i a_{s+i}=0,
\qquad
\sum_{i=0}^h|c_i|\,|z_{s+i}-a_{s+i}|<1,
\]
then \(\sum_i c_i z_{s+i}=0\).

The sum is an integer of absolute value less than one. To turn this equality into exclusion, a further argument must prove that the **actual** root values cannot satisfy it. Sol-A2 N12 does this by strict curvature and Rolle after cancelling an affine leading term with coefficients \((1,-2,1)\). Sections 5–6 use a different cancellation: an exact quadratic defect between two orbit values.

A solution at one index does not provide neighbouring hits. Neither N12 nor a cyclic gap-product theorem can be applied unless the cover proves all the required hits or the actual closed cycle. This is a significant constraint on attempts to turn a single Thue solution into a squeeze cycle.

### 1.5. Coverage and contact remain separate obligations

A complete ghost/squeeze certificate consists of: an elementary covering or descent with its inverse conditions; a transported integer or order-valued marker; the signed gap, including its nonzero condition; and every remaining finite index or exceptional coefficient case.

Contact and sign synchronization can enlarge \(L_s\) in (1.1). However, if different prime-power blocks go to different signs, their product is not automatically a divisor of one marker. Carry the complete sign ledger until an identity actually synchronizes those signs. A quadratic-field factorization can change the lattice in which the marker lives, but then §1.3, rather than the single real lattice gap, is the relevant test.

## 2. A growing-grid theorem for every base and every power degree at least three

This extends the supplied [exponential-product grid][products]. Its original complete twenty-pair classifications also use valuation and periodic covers outside \(q\mid k\); those other covers are not generalized here.

### 2.1. Uniform theorem

**PROVED.** Let \(a\ge2,q\ge3,2\le b\le a^q\) be integers such that
\[
b^{q-1}\ge q^{q-2}a^q.
\tag{2.1}
\]
Then for every integer \(s\ge1\),
\[
(a^{qs}-1)(b^{qs}-1)
\]
is not a \(q\)-th power of an integer. The integer \(q\) need not be prime.

Put
\[
X=a^{qs},\quad Y=b^s,\quad
D=q a^{(q-1)s},\quad P=Y(qX-1),\quad
N=(X-1)(Y^q-1).
\]
Then
\[
D^q=q^qX^{q-1},
\]
and the exact residual is
\[
\begin{split}
E&=P^q-D^qN\\
 &=Y^qF(X)+q^qX^{q-1}(X-1),\\
F(X)&=(qX-1)^q-q^qX^{q-1}(X-1).
\end{split}
\tag{2.2}
\]
Let \(C=\binom q2q^{q-2}\). For \(0<u<1\),
\[
1-qu<(1-u)^q<1-qu+\binom q2u^2.
\tag{2.3}
\]
One finite proof is Taylor's theorem of order one with its Rolle remainder: the second derivative is positive and strictly less than \(q(q-1)\) at a point strictly between zero and \(u\). Taylor's finite remainder follows by subtracting a quadratic with the same endpoint value and applying Rolle twice. Thus no integral or infinite expansion enters. Taking \(u=1/(qX)\) yields
\[
0<F(X)<CX^{q-2}.
\tag{2.4}
\]

The base conditions imply
\[
Y\le X,\qquad
Y^{q-1}\ge q^{q-2}X.
\tag{2.5}
\]
For the latter, raise (2.1) to \(s\), and use \(q^{(q-2)s}\ge q^{q-2}\). Equations (2.2)–(2.5) give
\[
0<E<(C+q^2)X^{q-1}Y^{q-1}.
\tag{2.6}
\]

The following constant inequality is valid for **all** \(q\ge3\):
\[
C+q^2<q(q-1/2)^{q-1}.
\tag{2.7}
\]
Indeed finite Bernoulli, strictly for exponent \(q-1\ge2\), gives
\[
(1-1/(2q))^{q-1}>1-(q-1)/(2q)=(q+1)/(2q).
\]
Multiplication by \(q^q\) gives a lower bound
\[
\frac{q+1}{2}q^{q-1}
=C+q^{q-1}\ge C+q^2.
\]
This proves (2.7), including \(q=3\).

Here \(X\ge8,Y\ge2\), so
\[
P-1\ge(q-1/2)XY.
\]
The finite binomial theorem gives
\[
P^q-(P-1)^q>q(P-1)^{q-1}.
\]
Consequently (2.6)–(2.7) imply
\[
\boxed{(P-1)^q<D^qN<P^q.}
\tag{2.8}
\]
If \(N=w^q\), its positive integer root would make \(Dw\) an integer strictly between \(P-1\) and \(P\). This proves the theorem.

### 2.2. Reach and the role of the orbit

For \(a=3,q=3\), (2.1) is simply \(b^2\ge81\), and the upper bound is \(b\le27\). Thus all nineteen bases \(9\le b\le27\) are covered for every exponent \(3s\).

## 3. A complete S13 square-class cover

### 3.1. Allocation can repair a nonsplit end

The following elementary cover lemma is a useful counterpart of Astra7's endpoint-square allocation.

**PROVED.** Let \(f(T)\in\mathbb Q[T]\) have odd degree \(d\ge1\), positive leading coefficient \(c\), and \(f(\tau)\ne0\), where \(\tau\) is rational. Let \(\delta>0\) be rational and suppose \(c\delta^d\) is a rational square. Then the integer solutions of
\[
z^2=f(\tau+\delta a^2),\qquad a\ge0,
\tag{3.1}
\]
have an effective finite upper bound on \(a\), obtainable using only finite polynomial operations and rational inequalities.

**Proof.** The right side has degree \(2d\) and rational positive leading square root. Successively matching the coefficients of degrees \(2d,2d-1,\ldots,d\) constructs \(q(a)\in\mathbb Q[a]\), of degree \(d\), such that
\[
R(a)=q(a)^2-f(\tau+\delta a^2),\qquad \deg R<d.
\]
This residual is nonzero. Otherwise the invariance under \(a\mapsto-a\) gives
\((q(-a)-q(a))(q(-a)+q(a))=0\) as a polynomial identity. Hence \(q\) is even or odd. Its degree \(d\) is odd, so it must be odd, forcing \(f(\tau)=q(0)^2=0\), a contradiction.

Clear denominators: choose \(D\ge1\) with \(Q=Dq\in\mathbb Z[a]\) and \(D^2R\in\mathbb Z[a]\). Finite leading-term bounds give an explicit cutoff beyond which
\[
Q>0,\quad D^2R\ne0,\quad |D^2R|<Q.
\]
For example bound each lower monomial by its coefficient times \(a^{e-1}\) when a polynomial has degree \(e\), and apply this to \(Q\), \(\pm D^2R\), and \(Q\pm D^2R\). At a square point,
\[
0<|D|z|-Q|
=\frac{|D^2R|}{D|z|+Q}<1,
\]
contradicting integrality. ∎

This proves a bound **on that cover**. It does not prove that every solution has the required squarefree label. That missing allocation is exactly the difference between a regional theorem and a complete fibre theorem.

Put

\[

\tag{3.2}

\[
r=2y/T\in\mathbb Z,\qquad P(T)=105r^2.

Indeed, if the rational number \(r=A/B\) is reduced, (3.3) forces \(B^2\mid105\), hence \(B=1\). Conversely, integral \(r\) and \(T=n(n+1)\) recover the integer \(y=Tr/2\). The certificate independently constructs the Bernoulli power-sum polynomial and checks every coefficient in (3.2).

\[

the leading coefficient becomes

### 3.5. Uniform reach, with its missing hypothesis explicit

For an odd exponent \(k\ge5\) with \(k\equiv1\pmod4\), the accepted integral Faulhaber lift has
\[
k+1=ab^2,\quad a\text{ squarefree},\quad
z^2=aH_k(T),\quad \deg H_k=(k-3)/2\text{ odd}.

## 4. Completing nineteen square-class regions inside the k=323 and k=399 gaps

The direct endpoint intervals are enormous, but on a fixed quadratic allocation cover there are only finitely many Pell orbit terms below a proved bound. This is an application of **coverage before squeezing/enumeration**. The following proof deliberately avoids depending on the large optimized auxiliary files for these two fibres.

### 4.1. An independent explicit bound for the even-u Runge family

**PROVED.** Let \(D=u^2\), where \(u\ge4\) is even, and put \(r=D/2\). Define
\[
Q_0=2^r(3r)!,\qquad H=(r+2)(2D)^D,
\qquad L=D!Q_0^2,
\]
\[
\boxed{B_D=2+6D+LH+4Q_0H.}
\tag{4.1}
\]
Every integer solution \(S_{D-1}(n)=y^2\), \(n\ge0\), satisfies \(n<B_D\).

This is a coarse finite bound, not a claim that its whole interval is affordable by direct enumeration.

**Finite coefficient proof.** Define \(B_0=1\) and
\[
B_j=-\frac1{j+1}\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\binom{j+1}{i}B_i.
\]
Induction gives \(|B_j|\le j!\) and \(\operatorname{den}(B_j)\mid(j+1)!\). For the first assertion the divided recurrence is at most
\(j!\sum_{v=2}^{j+1}1/v!<j!\); bound that finite sum using \(v!\ge2^{v-1}\). For the second, all earlier denominators divide \(j!\), and division by \(j+1\) gives the asserted denominator.

For completeness, the symmetry needed in the power-sum formula also has a finite proof. At a specified degree, invert the polynomial \((\sum_{i=0}^{D+1}Z^i/i!-1)/Z\) modulo the required power of \(Z\). Its inverse has coefficients \(B_j/j!\). The finite binomial identity gives \(E(Z)E(-Z)=1\) in that quotient, and hence \(B(-Z)=B(Z)+Z=E(Z)B(Z)\). Thus odd \(B_j\) vanish for \(j>1\), and \(B_j(1)=(-1)^jB_j\). Coefficient comparison with the defining recurrence gives
\(B_D(X+1)-B_D(X)=DX^{D-1}\). It follows by telescoping that
\[
f(X):=DS_{D-1}(X)
=\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}(-1)^j\binom DjB_jX^{D-j}.
\tag{4.2}
\]
All uses of \(E,B\) above are in finite quotient rings, not infinite power series.

Write
\[
f(X)=X^D\sum_{j=0}^D a_jX^{-j},\qquad a_0=1,
\]
where \(a_D=0\). Then
\[
|a_j|\le D^j,\qquad \operatorname{den}(a_j)\mid(j+1)!.
\]
In the finite quotient \(\mathbb Q[Z]/(Z^{r+1})\), take the root polynomial
\[
\beta(Z)=\sum_{j=0}^r\beta_jZ^j,
\quad\beta_0=1,\quad
\beta^2=\sum a_jZ^j\pmod{Z^{r+1}}.
\]
The triangular coefficient recurrence constructs it uniquely. Equivalently use the finite binomial polynomial through order \(r\); its square identity follows from finite Vandermonde convolution, proved first for nonnegative integer parameters and then as a polynomial identity at rational parameters.

Counting the ordered compositions of \(j\), and using \(|\binom{1/2}{\ell}|\le1\), gives
\[
|\beta_j|\le(2D)^j.
\tag{4.3}
\]
A term with composition \(i_1+\cdots+i_\ell=j\) has denominator dividing
\[
2^\ell\ell!\prod_{v=1}^{\ell}(i_v+1)!
\quad\mid\quad2^j(3j)!.
\]
The factorial divisibility follows from the integral multinomial coefficient: the factorial arguments have total \(j+2\ell\le3j\). Therefore \(Q_0\) clears all \(\beta_j\), \(j\le r\).

Set
\[
q(X)=\sum_{j=0}^r\beta_jX^{r-j},\qquad R(X)=q(X)^2-f(X).
\]
The coefficient cancellation gives \(\deg R\le r-1\). Each coefficient of \(R\) has absolute value at most \(H\): at most \(r+1\) products in the square have total reversed index at most \(D\), each bounded by \((2D)^D\), and the coefficient of \(f\) contributes at most one further such bound. Also \(LR\in\mathbb Z[X]\).

The residual is nonzero. Otherwise
\[
(q(X)-q(X-1))(q(X)+q(X-1))=DX^{D-1}.
\]
The factors have degrees \(r-1,r\) and leading coefficients \(r,2\). Since their product is a monomial, finite polynomial factorization forces them to be \(rX^{r-1}\) and \(2X^r\). Thus
\(q(X)=X^r+(r/2)X^{r-1}\). But \(f(0)=f(-1)=0\), so \(q(-1)=0\), forcing \(r=2\). This contradicts \(D\ge16\).

For \(X>1+LH\), the nonzero polynomial \(LR\) has no zero: its leading integer coefficient has absolute value at least one, and its lower terms, each at most \(LH\), are dominated by the leading term using a finite geometric sum. For \(X\ge6D\), (4.3) gives
\[
q(X)>X^r/2,
\]
since the sum of the lower-term ratios is less than \(\sum_{j=1}^r(1/3)^j<1/2\). For \(X\ge2\),
\[
|R(X)|\le2HX^{r-1}.
\]
At a square solution set \(w=u|y|\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\); then \(w^2=f(n)\). If \(n\ge B_D\), all the preceding inequalities apply and
\[
0<|w-q(n)|=\frac{|R(n)|}{w+q(n)}<\frac{4H}{n}<\frac1{Q_0}.
\]
This contradicts \(Q_0(w-q(n))\in\mathbb Z\). The bound (4.1) is proved.

## 5. S-003: cancel two shifted Fibonacci-square hits

The supplied Pell cover has already reduced the odd branch to the shifted-square markers \(4F_{6j+1}+5\) and \(4F_{6j+5}+5\). The result here is a restriction **between hits**, not another statement that the gaps between covered Pell terms are empty.

### 5.1. An exact affine-orbit defect

**PROVED identity.** For nonzero real \(L\), put
\[
V=L+L^{-1},\quad K=V^2-4,
\quad X=u+v+b,
\quad Y=uL^2+vL^{-2}+b.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{[2(X+Y)+bK]^2-4V^2XY
=(b^2-4uv)V^2K.}
\tag{5.1}
\]
Expand the two squares, use \(L^2+L^{-2}=V^2-2\), and cancel the terms in \(u^2,v^2\) and \(u+v\); the remaining coefficient is exactly the right side. `P19_orbits.py` also checks the equality as a finite Laurent-polynomial identity in four independent variables. It is not inferred from numerical orbit values.

The point of (5.1) is that \(uv\) is constant along a reciprocal exponential orbit. If both \(X,Y\) are squares and \(V,bK\) have the required integrality, the left side is a difference of integer squares with a controlled defect. This is the affine-shift version of the exact Cauchy defect emphasized by the Steele and Sol-A packets.

### 5.2. Specialization to Fibonacci values

Let \(F_0=0,F_1=1,F_{j+1}=F_j+F_{j-1}\), and put
\(\phi=(1+\sqrt5)/2\). Finite substitution in the recurrence and the first two values proves
\[
F_j=\frac{\phi^j-(-\phi^{-1})^j}{\sqrt5}.
\tag{5.2}
\]
This is an algebraic recurrence identity, not an analytic limit. Let \(n\) be odd and \(m\) a positive multiple of four. Set
\[
X=4F_n+5,\quad Y=4F_{n+m}+5,
\quad V=L_{m/2},\quad K=V^2-4,
\]
where \(L_j=\phi^j+(-\phi^{-1})^j\) is the integer Lucas sequence. Formula (5.1), with
\[
u=4\phi^n/\sqrt5,
\quad v=4\phi^{-n}/\sqrt5,
\quad b=5,
\quad L=\phi^{m/2},
\]
gives
\[
Z=2(X+Y)+5K,
\qquad
\boxed{Z^2-4V^2XY=61F_m^2=:E>0.}
\tag{5.3}
\]
Indeed \(uv=16/5\) and \(V^2K=5F_m^2\).

If both \(X,Y\) are squares, let \(W=2V\sqrt X\sqrt Y\), a positive integer. Then
\[
J=Z-W\in\mathbb Z_{>0},\qquad
J=\frac E{Z+W},\qquad
J-\frac E{2Z}=\frac{J^2}{2Z}.
\tag{5.4}
\]
We will put this integer into an impossible gap in three ranges.

### 5.3. The range m <= n-3: a direct integer gap

For \(n\ge m+3\),
\[
Z\ge8F_{n+m}\ge8F_{2m+3}>61F_m^2=E.
\tag{5.5}
\]
To justify the strict last inequality, the finite Fibonacci addition identity gives
\(F_{2m+3}=F_{m+1}^2+F_{m+2}^2\). For \(m\ge2\),
\(F_{m+1}\ge3F_m/2\) and \(F_{m+2}\ge5F_m/2\): use \(F_m\le2F_{m-1}\). Hence \(8F_{2m+3}\ge68F_m^2>61F_m^2\).

The addition identity itself follows by induction from
\(F_{a+b}=F_aF_{b+1}+F_{a-1}F_b\), with the initial cases checked directly. Thus no extra recurrence theorem is being imported. From (5.4)–(5.5), \(0<J<E/Z<1\), impossible.

### 5.4. Quantitative comparison after leading cancellation

Put \(x=\phi^n,z=\phi^m\), and
\[
c_0=\frac{61}{16\sqrt5}<2,
\qquad Z_0=\frac8{\sqrt5}xz.
\]
Using (5.2), all the following are exact:
\[
Z=\frac8{\sqrt5}(x+x^{-1}+xz+(xz)^{-1})
+5(z+z^{-1})+10,
\]
\[
E=\frac{61}{5}(z-z^{-1})^2.
\tag{5.6}
\]
Write \(Z=Z_0(1+\delta)\), with \(\delta>0\). Since \(\sqrt5<12/5\), termwise comparison gives
\[
\delta\le3/z+6/x.
\tag{5.7}
\]
For example, the first three extra terms contribute at most \(3/z\); the terms with coefficients \(5\sqrt5/8<3/2\) and \(10\sqrt5/8<3\) contribute at most \(6/x\).

If \(z\ge x\), (5.7) gives \(\delta\le9/x\). Since
\[
\frac E{2Z}=c_0\frac zx\frac{(1-z^{-2})^2}{1+\delta},
\]
we obtain
\[
0<c_0\frac zx-\frac E{2Z}
\le18\frac z{x^2}+\frac4{xz}.
\]
Also \(J<E/Z<4z/x\), and (5.4) gives
\[
0<J-E/(2Z)<3z/x^3.
\]
Consequently
\[
\boxed{\left|J-c_0z/x\right|
<21z/x^2+4/(xz)\qquad(z\ge x).}
\tag{5.8}
\]
All estimates are finite inequalities on positive real algebraic quantities.

There is one needed neighbouring range with \(z<x\): \(m=n-1\), so \(z=x/\phi\). In this case (5.7) gives \(\delta<12/x\). The same calculation replaces 21 in (5.8) by 27. This small case must not be silently covered by the hypothesis \(z\ge x\).

### 5.6. The larger difference range: a Fibonacci rational grid

Suppose

### 5.7. The all-index gap and its exact limit

Combining §§5.3–5.6 proves:

> If \(n<N\) are odd, \(N\equiv n\pmod4\), \(n\ge101\), and both \(4F_n+5\) and \(4F_N+5\) are squares, then \(N>3n-16\).

Indeed set \(m=N-n\), a positive multiple of four. The assumption \(N\le3n-16\) gives \(m\le2n-16\). Either \(m\le n-3\), or the odd difference \(m-n\) is one of the nine small cases, or it is at least sixteen and satisfies the second condition of §5.6. These cases exhaust the assumption.

\tag{5.11}
\]
This is a finite counting restriction, not a proof of finiteness over all indices.

The exact finite controls check 2,525 instances of (5.3). An independent scan of odd indices through 10,001 finds only
\((n,s)=(1,3),(5,5),(11,19)\). That scan is labelled **CHECKED** and is not the proof of (5.11) or a height cutoff. The original restriction to indices \(1,5\pmod6\) remains: the third odd class has \(F_n\equiv2\pmod4\), making \(4F_n+5\equiv13\pmod{16}\), not a square.

### 6.1. Coverage of the actual Pell sequence

Set \(U=x^2\ge1,V=|y|\). The norm equation is \(20U^2-19V^2=1\). For \(U\ge9\),

The remaining condition is exactly that \(U_j\) be square.

### 6.2. A quadratic-norm model for both index parities

Define

\binom{X_t}{Y_t}=M^t\binom10.

All these are integers, \(\det M=1\), and the first coordinate satisfies (6.2), so \(X_t=U_t\). Direct matrix multiplication, or induction, gives the exact shape

\tag{6.3}

Taking the upper-left entry of \(M^{2t}\) and \(M^tMM^t\) respectively proves

\qquad

\tag{6.4}

For the second identity the expanded entry is

Let

## 7. Which orbit grids are obstructed, and which are not

### 7.1. A fixed-expression orbit version of R12

**PROVED, with a restricted scope.** Let \(K\subset\mathbb R\) be a fixed field, let \(\lambda>1\), and let \(f(T)\in K[T,T^{-1}]\) have positive leading coefficient \(c\) and positive highest exponent \(d\). Let \(r\ge2\). Suppose either \(r\nmid d\), or \(c\notin K^r\). For every fixed rational function \(q(T)\in K(T)\), there are positive constants \(A\) and an integer \(s_0\) such that
\[
\bigl|f(\lambda^s)^{1/r}-q(\lambda^s)\bigr|
\ge A\lambda^{ds/r}\qquad(s\ge s_0).
\tag{7.1}
\]
The real root is the positive one; \(f(\lambda^s)>0\) beyond a finite cutoff.

**Proof.** A nonzero rational function has an integral leading exponent \(e\) and a nonzero leading coefficient \(b\in K\), obtained by subtracting numerator and denominator degrees. Finite coefficient bounds give its leading comparison \(bT^e\) with arbitrarily small relative error once \(T\) exceeds an explicit bound. The same finite leading-term argument gives the positive root comparison \(c^{1/r}T^{d/r}\); this can also be bounded by taking positive roots of two rationally separated coefficient inequalities.

If \(e<d/r\), the root term dominates; if \(e>d/r\), the rational function dominates. If \(e=d/r\), then \(r\mid d\), but \(b\ne c^{1/r}\) by the coefficient hypothesis, so the nonzero difference of leading coefficients dominates. Taking, for example, a quarter of the relevant positive leading separation gives (7.1). The zero rational function is immediate. All cutoffs come from finite coefficient domination and a positive leading-coefficient separation. With algebraic inputs these can be made rational by polynomial root brackets. ∎

This theorem excludes fixed finite Laurent or rational formulae in the indicated field as increasingly accurate centres. It does **not** cover arbitrary index-dependent coefficients, varying-degree approximants, a discontinuous rounding rule, or an arithmetic cover changing the field or dominant character. There is no claim here that every possible orbit-dependent grid is obstructed.

For S-003 set \(\lambda=\phi^6=9+4\sqrt5\). The two relevant sequences have the forms
\[
4F_{6j+1}+5=c_A\lambda^j+5+c_A'\lambda^{-j},
\quad c_A=2+(2/5)\sqrt5,
\]
\[
4F_{6j+5}+5=c_B\lambda^j+5+c_B'\lambda^{-j},
\quad c_B=10+(22/5)\sqrt5.
\tag{7.2}
\]
Here the prime denotes quadratic conjugation, and
\[
c_Ac_A'=c_Bc_B'=16/5.
\]
This is not a rational square. Thus neither leading coefficient is a square in \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\): the norm of a square would be a rational square.

On any fixed index phase \(j=ht+e\), an odd \(h\) retains the odd leading exponent obstruction. For even \(h\), the leading coefficient becomes \(c\lambda^e\), whose norm is still \(16/5\), so the coefficient obstruction remains. Therefore no fixed rational expression in \(\lambda^t\), with coefficients in the original quadratic field, supplies an absolute error tending to zero on a whole phase.

For S-005,
\[
U_j=c\lambda^j+c'\lambda^{-j},
\quad\lambda=39+4\sqrt{95},\quad
c=\frac12+\frac{\sqrt{95}}{20},\quad cc'=1/80.
\tag{7.3}
\]
The same phase argument applies, since \(1/80\) is not a rational square.

These obstructions do not contradict §§5–6: those arguments cancel the leading mode **between two alleged hits**, rather than approximate a single root by one of the forbidden fixed expressions.

### 7.2. The first algebraic correction has an exact saturated norm

One might enlarge the quadratic field by the square root of the leading coefficient. The most natural first correction then has a completely explicit norm, which shows why its small value at one embedding is not yet an exclusion.

Let \(K\) be a real quadratic field, let \(c,c'>0\) be conjugates with \(c\) nonsquare in \(K\), let \(x\in K\) satisfy \(xx'=1\), and let \(b\in\mathbb Q\). Suppose
\[
w^2=cx^2+b+c'x^{-2},\qquad w\in\mathbb Q.
\]
In the degree-four field \(K(\sqrt c)\), put
\[
q=\sqrt c\,x+\frac{b}{2\sqrt c}\,x^{-1}.
\]
Then finite multiplication gives
\[
w^2-q^2=\left(c'-\frac{b^2}{4c}\right)x^{-2}.
\]
Taking the product with its quadratic conjugate proves
\[
\boxed{N_{K(\sqrt c)/\mathbb Q}(w-q)
=\frac{(b^2-4cc')^2}{16cc'}.}
\tag{7.4}
\]
This is just the product of the four factors \(w\pm q\) and their quadratic conjugates. No norm theorem beyond finite field multiplication is needed. The right side is independent of the orbit index.

For the first S-003 orbit on its even phase, take \(c=c_A\), \(b=5\), \(x=\lambda^t\). Then (7.4) is
\[
N(w-q)=3721/1280.
\]
To see the integer lattice explicitly, put \(\eta=5\sqrt c\). It satisfies
\(\eta^2=50+10\sqrt5\), and
\[
q=\eta\left(\frac{x}{5}+\frac{5-\sqrt5}{16}x^{-1}\right).
\]
Thus \(80(w-q)\) lies in the explicit integral order
\(\mathbb Z[\sqrt5,\eta]\), since \(\lambda^{\pm1}\in\mathbb Z[\sqrt5]\). Its integer multiplication determinant is exactly
\[
80^4\frac{3721}{1280}=119072000,
\tag{7.5}
\]
not a number of absolute value less than one.

For S-005 on the even phase, take \(c\) from (7.3), \(b=0\), \(x=\lambda^t\). The raw norm is \(1/80\). This number being less than one is **not** a contradiction, because the raw marker has a denominator. With \(\eta=20\sqrt c\),
\(\eta^2=200+20\sqrt{95}\), and \(20(w-q)\) belongs to
\(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{95},\eta]\). Its exact integer norm is
\[
20^4/80=2000.
\tag{7.6}
\]

Equations (7.5)–(7.6) identify the new cover left by these centres: an equation of constant, nonzero norm with the original orbit condition still attached. They do not solve that cover. Adding more correction terms or using additional embeddings may lead somewhere else, but must print the improved integer norm or grid budget; the small first-embedding error alone has already been accounted for.

### 7.3. Why the exponential-product construction is genuinely different

In §2 the phase \(k=qs\) has an integral dominant \(q\)-th root, and the growth relation between \(a^s\) and \(b^s\) makes the scaled residual smaller than an adjacent integer-power gap. For S-003/S-005 the fixed quadratic orbit field retains a nonsquare dominant coefficient on every phase; adjoining its root produces the nonzero constant norms above.

This explains a precise limitation of transplanting the same formula. It does not elevate R12 to an impossibility theorem for arbitrary grids. In particular, the allocation cover of §3 is an explicit example of changing the dominant coefficient before squeezing, and the two-hit constructions show another way to avoid relying on a single root centre.

## 8. Thomas a=7: the mixed allocation remains a genuine equation

### 8.1. The integer coefficient already used by the complementary bound

Retain P18's actual branch data, not just the prime congruence classes:
\[
B=x-2y,\quad Y=|y|=DH,\quad
D^2\mid B-\epsilon,\quad H^2\mid B^2+\epsilon B+1,
\]
\[
d=\frac{B^2+\epsilon B+1}{H^2},\quad s=D^2,
\qquad s<d<36s.
\]
The exact reconstruction equation is
\[
(d-21s)B-d\epsilon=35sy,
\qquad dy^2=s(B^2+\epsilon B+1).
\tag{8.1}
\]
Eliminating \(y\) gives
\[
A_2B^2+A_1B+A_0=0,
\tag{8.2}
\]
where
\[
\begin{aligned}
A_2&=d(d-21s)^2-1225s^3,\\
A_1&=-\epsilon\{2d^2(d-21s)+1225s^3\},\\
A_0&=d^3-1225s^3.
\end{aligned}
\]
The integer \(A_2\) is nonzero: otherwise \(d/s\) is a rational root of
\(Z^3-42Z^2+441Z-1225\). A monic rational root is integral, while that polynomial is one modulo two at every integer. Thus \(|A_2|\ge1\).

The bounds \(|A_1|<53065D^6\), \(|A_0|<45431D^6\), and \(|B|>Y\), proved in [P18 §2][p18], give
\[
0<|A_2|<98496D^6/Y.
\tag{8.3}
\]
This is already an integer squeeze. It excludes the region with \(Y\) larger than the indicated sixth-power scale. Merely calling \(A_2\) a new discrete set does not make the right side smaller on the remaining mixed region.

A useful new mechanism would have to produce, from (8.1) and the oriented factor ledger, an additional divisibility of \(A_2\), a different cancelling combination with smaller growth, or an actual decreasing move. None of these is established in this answer. A rational root bracket for \(d/D^2\) by itself supplies only a growing number of integer candidates as \(D\) grows.

### 8.2. The complementary inequalities and split support cannot alone close the middle

Here is an all-parameter test of that proposed inference. For any integer \(j\ge1\), put
\[
t=9j,\quad D=t,\quad H=t^2+t+1,\quad Y=DH,
\qquad d=7D^2.
\tag{8.4}
\]
Then
\[
\gcd(D,H)=1,\quad H\equiv1\pmod9,\quad
Y\le6H^2,\quad Y<100000D^6,
\quad D^2<d<36D^2.
\tag{8.5}
\]
Every prime of \(H\) is \(1\pmod3\). Indeed three does not divide \(H\); at any other prime divisor, \(t\) is a nonzero cube root of one but is not one, since \(t\equiv1\) would make \(H\equiv3\). Fermat's congruence and division of exponents then give \(3\mid p-1\).

Both norm representations are explicit in the Eisenstein ring:
\[
H=N(t-\omega),\qquad d=N(D(3+\omega)).
\tag{8.6}
\]
Thus arbitrarily large coprime values satisfy the two size inequalities, the split support, the congruence, and these individual norm representations.

These data are **not** claimed to be Thomas solutions or a counterexample to a stronger descent. In fact, with the displayed orientations,
\[
D(3+\omega)(t-\omega)^2
=(3t^3+2t^2-2t)+(t^3-4t^2-3t)\omega,
\]
whose second coefficient is not \(-\epsilon\) for \(t\ge9\). The full coefficient equation \(\delta\gamma^2=B-\epsilon\omega\) has been lost. That is precisely the information a successful new squeeze must exploit.

The Sol-A2 prime-content moment lemma is consistent with this family. It gives a lower bound on a large prime when a bound on the total prime multiplicity is available; it does not bound the product \(H\), and no required uniform multiplicity bound has been proved for the Thomas ledger.

### 8.3. Why a cyclic-gap theorem is not yet a Thomas theorem

The signed cyclic transformations of a Thomas solution generate further presentations of the same equation. They do not, just by cyclicity, create positive equations of the form
\[
x_i^r+a_ix_{i+1}^{r-1}=z_i^r
\]
with fixed coefficients and with the positivity needed to multiply gap inequalities. Nor does a finite tree of observed residue states prove such a cycle or a lowering move.

The retained all-height regions from P18 still apply: bounding either actual branch factor \(D\) or \(H\) in one cyclic presentation gives a complete regional result. The residual mixed set has both large in every presentation, together with (8.1) and the oriented Eisenstein coefficient equations. This answer supplies no proof that the residual set is empty, for \(a=7\) or uniformly in the parameter.

## 9. Odd powers, other Schäffer covers, and S011

### 9.1. A sharper uniform dominant-term corridor

**PROVED.** Let \(n\ge2,m\ge2,r\ge1\) be integers. If
\[
m\ge n-1,\qquad (n-1)^m<n^{m-1},
\tag{9.1}
\]
then
\[
\boxed{(n^r)^m<S_{mr}(n)<(n^r+1)^m.}
\tag{9.2}
\]

**Proof.** The largest summand is \(n^{mr}\), and the remaining positive sum is at most \((n-1)^{mr+1}\). Raising the strict inequality in (9.1) to \(r\) gives
\[
S_{mr}(n)-n^{mr}
\le(n-1)^{mr+1}
<(n-1)n^{r(m-1)}
\le m n^{r(m-1)}.
\]
The last quantity is the first positive term of the finite binomial expansion of \((n^r+1)^m-n^{mr}\), whose other terms are positive. This proves (9.2).

Let \(b(n)\) be the smallest nonnegative integer with \(2^{b(n)}\ge n\). The sufficient bound
\[
\boxed{m\ge\max(2,(n-1)b(n))}
\tag{9.3}
\]
implies (9.1). For \(n>2\), finite Bernoulli gives
\[
(n/(n-1))^{n-1}>2;
\]
raising to \(b(n)\) gives a value strictly greater than \(2^{b(n)}\ge n\). Increasing the exponent to \(m\) preserves it. For \(n=2\), the imposed \(m\ge2\) gives the strict inequality directly. Also (9.3) implies \(m\ge n-1\).

This includes odd power exponents and makes no assumption that \(mr+1\) is a perfect power. For example \(S_{101r}(n)\) is not a 101st power for every \(r\ge1\) and \(2\le n\le21\). The condition \(m\mid k\) is essential to this particular comparison index \(n^{k/m}\); no assertion about the other exponents is being smuggled in.

The sharp condition (9.1) is the supplied R9 mechanism. The explicit sufficient threshold (9.3) is a different uniform way to pay for the sum, replacing a quadratic-in-\(n\) sufficient threshold by one involving the finite binary-length index where advantageous.

### 9.2. S13 valuation holes and the remaining balanced allocation

The previously proved holes at primes 3, 7 and 691 in the set of sums of two squares, and the square obstruction at five, remain sound necessary conditions. They act on prime valuations of the exact Faulhaber value; they do not force every remaining endpoint to meet such a prime. Section 3 supplies a different global leaf, based on the squarefree class of the **two-anchor product** \(T-2\).

For the cubic-contact presentation of P16/P17,
\[
Y^2-A(X)^2=X^3C(X),\qquad X=T-2,
\]
the variable polynomial \(C(X)\) and the distribution of the prime powers of \(X\) between the two factors remain. The new cover \(X=14a^2\) deals with one entire class without proving a common sign. It does not prove that the other squarefree labels are bounded, nor that their residual divisors lie in a fixed finite list.

Thus a complete hybrid solver may take the union of valuation-hole leaves, contact/cofactor leaves, the new square-class leaf, and any certified quotient/Jacobian leaves. The logical requirement is still a cover of every candidate. A large union of proved leaves is not automatically such a cover.

### 9.3. S011 and the three-hit limitation

No new unrestricted S011 classification is proved here. Its previously certified near-square base regions, fixed exponent regions and even-exponent factorization remain as stated in the supplied [priority-row proof][priority]. Sol-A2 N12 is a theorem about three equally spaced power hits with a shared constant and leading coefficient. A solution \(x^2+7=y^n\) at one base does not give the other two hits. Applying its integer second difference to unproved neighbours would be the missing step, not a use of the theorem.

The same warning applies to a supposed exponent induction for \(S_k(n)\): finite-sum recurrences transport exact values, but do not automatically transport a perfect-power witness to the other terms. The difference between a recurrence identity and a covered set of simultaneous hits must remain explicit.

### 10.2. Exact residual sets

For **S13 squares**, the new conclusion removes the entire positive class \(\operatorname{sf}(n(n+1)-2)=14\). A remaining nontrivial solution must lie in another squarefree class and satisfy the earlier valuation, contact and inverse-cover ledgers. Rank one is neither assumed nor proved.

For **k=323,399 squares**, a remaining nontrivial solution has \(\operatorname{sf}(n(n+1)-2)>30\), in addition to the previously retained finite gap intervals and local restrictions. The new packet does not exclude those complementary labels.

For **S-003**, further square hits on either odd residue class modulo four must obey the pairwise gap \(N>3n-16\) once the smaller index is at least 101. The first further hit is still unexcluded. The original eight Thue tails and their prime-support/actual-branch constraints remain available, but this answer has not proved a new synchronization between those constraints and a first-hit orbit grid.

For **S-005**, the product-gap theorem forbids close repeated square classes in either parity. It leaves the possibility of an isolated first later square term. A bound on the spacing from the anchor, or a separate one-hit obstruction, is still required.

For **Thomas a=7 and the family**, the full mixed coefficient ledger remains the stopping point. The elementary inequalities and norm representations alone admit unbounded compatible size data. This is not a proof that the exact Thue ledger admits a nontrivial point, and not a proof against a new descent.

For **S011 at unrestricted odd exponents**, no additional global cover is supplied.

### 10.3. Concrete next certificates

The most promising finite tasks are now more specific than “try a finer grid.”

1. **Allocation forcing for S13.** Use the exact equation \(P(T)=105r^2\) and the endpoint square \(4T+1\) to constrain \(\operatorname{sf}(T-2)\). A certificate forcing the label fourteen, or a finite set of further labels with matching covers, would turn §3 into a full or substantially larger classification. Without that forcing theorem, increasing a label table is only a regional extension.
2. **More power-sum allocation covers.** For k=323,399, generate explicit positive norm-one matrices for additional fixed squarefree labels, their full decreasing seed bounds, and replay to the already proved \(B_D\). The present verifier supplies a working all-height format. A uniform bound on the labels is a separate target.
3. **First-hit separation for S-003/S-005.** Search for an arithmetic consequence of one shifted-square/square hit which supplies a second hit within the forbidden index range, or a stronger integral annihilator relation. The present pairwise gaps would then become a negative ghost theorem. A generated neighbouring orbit value is not itself a second hit.
4. **Algebraic grid audit.** Any proposed centre in a larger field should include its clearing multiplier and its full multiplication determinant. Compare the resulting norm against (7.5) or (7.6). A smaller error at one embedding with an equal increase in denominator/conjugate size is not a passing certificate.
5. **Thomas mixed coefficient squeeze.** Target (8.1)–(8.2), not just the two magnitude inequalities. A useful certificate would prove extra divisibility of a nonzero coefficient combination, a finite affine contraction on an actual cycle of marked powers, or a lower-content inverse map. Its inverse integrality and branch orientations must be printed.

The combination that worked in this answer is **allocation → covered sparse orbit → finite norm/root gap → complete orbit remainder**, and, separately, **two hits → cancellation of the leading character → an integer or rational-grid defect**. These are additions to the ghost framework with explicit coverage and arithmetic transport. They are not a generic elementary solver for all the remaining equations.
