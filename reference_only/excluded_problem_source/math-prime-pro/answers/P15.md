## 1. Audit of the programme

### 1.1. The rule for accepting a finite replacement

A calculation at one modulus is not an all-precision lifting proof. A list of initial approximants is not an all-index error bound. A finite search is not exhaustive until coverage has been proved. The acceptable pattern is:

\[
\text{finite identities and inequalities}
\; +\;\text{an explicit induction or decreasing integer}
\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{the infinite assertion needed by the evaluation}.
\]

Conversely, an algorithm may discover a certificate using tools that the final proof does not need. Acceptance can consist entirely of checking the resulting integer vector, polynomial identity, rational inequality, or complete finite box. In particular, a finite determinant is not automatically a Baker-style interpolation argument. Its mathematical role and its dependencies decide the classification.

## 2. Finite real-zero budgets and polynomial end certificates

**ELEMENTARY.** The integer-index part of lifting.real_count admits a proof using only finite sums, polynomial division, sign comparisons, determinants, and induction. No exponential function at noninteger arguments, logarithm, differentiation of an exponential, Rolle theorem, limit, infinite series, or analytic zero estimate is needed.

The bases need not be algebraic for the theorem. Algebraicity matters only for making the signs, recurrence identities, and candidate zero values exactly certifiable.

### C1. The simple-mode theorem

Let
\[
0<a_1<\cdots<a_m,\qquad c_j\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\},
\qquad u_n=\sum_{j=1}^m c_j a_j^n,\quad n\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Write \(V(c_1,\ldots,c_m)\) for the number of adjacent sign changes in the ordered coefficient list. Then
\[
\boxed{\#\{n\in\mathbb Z:u_n=0\}\le V(c_1,\ldots,c_m)\le m-1.} \tag{RC1}
\]
The same bound holds on any subset of \(\mathbb Z\). Zero coefficients are removed before counting signs. Equal bases are merged before zero coefficients are removed. If every merged coefficient is zero, the sequence is identically zero and there is no finite zero budget.

This counts **distinct integer indices**. Consecutive zero values each count once, exactly as separated zeros do. No passage through a zero is needed, and no scalar recurrence division is used.

#### C1.1. A finite positive-determinant identity

For integers \(n_1<\cdots<n_m\) and positive reals \(a_1<\cdots<a_m\), set
\[
D(n,a)=\det[a_j^{n_i}]_{1\le i,j\le m}.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{D(n,a)>0.} \tag{RC2}
\]

**Proof by finite induction on \(m\).** Factoring \(a_j^{n_1}>0\) from column \(j\) reduces to \(n_1=0\) and all exponents nonnegative. The case \(m=1\) is immediate. For \(m>1\), subtract the first column from every other column and expand along the first row. Divide column \(j\), for \(j\ge2\), by the positive number \(a_j-a_1\). The remaining determinant has entries
\[
\frac{a_j^{k_i}-a_1^{k_i}}{a_j-a_1}
=\sum_{t=0}^{k_i-1}a_1^{k_i-1-t}a_j^t,
\qquad i,j=2,\ldots,m,
\]
where \(0=k_1<k_2<\cdots<k_m\) are the shifted exponents.

Put \(q=m-1\) and \(p_i=k_{i+1}-1\), so \(0\le p_1<\cdots<p_q\). This matrix is the product of the two finite matrices
\[
A_{i,t}=
\begin{cases}
a_1^{p_i-t}&0\le t\le p_i,\\
0&t>p_i,
\end{cases}
\qquad
B_{t,j}=a_{j+1}^t,\qquad 0\le t\le p_q.
\]
The finite determinant expansion gives
\[
\det(AB)=
\sum_{0\le t_1<\cdots<t_q\le p_q}
\det A_{[:,t_1,\ldots,t_q]}\,
\det B_{[t_1,\ldots,t_q,:]}. \tag{RC3}
\]
To verify this identity directly, expand the determinant after expanding each product entry. Terms with repeated intermediate indices have two equal rows in the second determinant and cancel. Grouping the other terms by their increasing index set gives exactly the displayed sum.

Every first determinant has the sign of
\[
\det[\,1_{\{t_j\le p_i\}}\,]_{i,j=1}^q,
\]
because factoring \(a_1^{p_i}\) from rows and \(a_1^{-t_j}\) from columns contributes a positive factor. Subtract each previous row from the next, working from the bottom upward. The new rows are indicators of the disjoint ordered intervals
\[
(-1,p_1],\ (p_1,p_2],\ldots,(p_{q-1},p_q].
\]
The determinant is \(1\) if exactly one selected \(t_j\) lies in each interval, and \(0\) otherwise. Indeed each column then has one \(1\), and the increasing order of the selected indices forces the nonzero permutation to be the identity. It is \(1\) for the choice \(t_i=p_i\).

Every second determinant in (RC3) is positive by the induction hypothesis, since its nodes are \(a_2<\cdots<a_m\) and its exponents are increasing nonnegative integers. Thus every summand is nonnegative and at least one is positive. Restoring the positive column factors proves (RC2). ∎

This is a finite identity even when some original \(n_i\) are negative: the initial positive column factors remove that case. Its size can be large; the proof asserts neither a fast numerical procedure nor an asymptotic approximation.

#### C1.2. Coefficient signs give the sharp budget

Put \(v=V(c_1,\ldots,c_m)\). Choose one positive separator \(\xi\) strictly between \(a_j\) and \(a_{j+1}\) at each place where the coefficient sign changes. In increasing order these are
\[
0<\xi_1<\cdots<\xi_v.
\]
Suppose, for a contradiction, that there are \(v+1\) distinct integer zeros
\(n_0<\cdots<n_v\). Form the Laurent polynomial
\[
P(X)=
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
\xi_1^{n_0}&\cdots&\xi_v^{n_0}&X^{n_0}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
\xi_1^{n_v}&\cdots&\xi_v^{n_v}&X^{n_v}
\end{pmatrix}. \tag{RC4}
\]
If \(v=0\), this simply means \(P(X)=X^{n_0}\).

For \(X>0\) different from all the \(\xi_i\), move its last column into the increasing order of the nodes. By (RC2), the reordered determinant is positive. Therefore
\[
\operatorname{sgn}P(X)=(-1)^{\#\{i:\xi_i>X\}}.
\]
Its signs at the \(a_j\) change exactly where the coefficient signs change. Multiply \(P\) by a global sign so that
\[
c_jP(a_j)>0\qquad(1\le j\le m).
\]
Consequently \(\sum_j c_jP(a_j)>0\). On the other hand, expansion along the last column gives \(P(X)=\sum_{i=0}^v d_iX^{n_i}\), and hence
\[
\sum_jc_jP(a_j)
=\sum_{i=0}^v d_i\sum_jc_ja_j^{n_i}
=\sum_{i=0}^v d_i u_{n_i}=0.
\]
This contradiction proves (RC1). No assertion about a zero between two integer indices has been used. ∎

### C2. Repeated characteristic roots

Let \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\) be distinct positive reals and
\[
u_n=\sum_{j=1}^m P_j(n)a_j^n,\qquad
P_j\in\mathbb R[n]\setminus\{0\},\qquad
d_j=1+\deg P_j,\quad D=\sum_jd_j.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{\#\{n\in\mathbb Z:u_n=0\}\le D-1.} \tag{RC5}
\]

The proof below uses formal derivatives of **finite polynomials** only. A formal derivative is the algebraic operation \(X^k\mapsto kX^{k-1}\); no derivative limit is used.

#### C2.1. Polynomial Descartes rule from weighted telescoping

For a nonzero real polynomial, let \(V(P)\) denote the sign changes in its nonzero coefficients, listed in increasing degree. For \(a>0\),
\[
V((X-a)P)\ge V(P)+1. \tag{RC6}
\]
Replacing \(X\) by \(aX\) changes no coefficient sign, so it suffices to use \(a=1\). Write \(P=\sum p_iX^i\), and \(Q=(X-1)P=\sum q_iX^i\). Then
\[
q_i=p_{i-1}-p_i,\qquad
\sum_{\ell=i+1}^{j}q_\ell=p_i-p_j. \tag{RC7}
\]
Strip initial and final zero coefficients of \(P\). The first nonzero coefficient of \(Q\) has the sign opposite to the first coefficient of \(P\); its last coefficient has the same sign as the last coefficient of \(P\). At each change from a nonzero coefficient \(p_i\) to the next nonzero coefficient \(p_j\) of opposite sign, the sum in (RC7) has the sign of \(p_i\). At least one \(q_\ell\) in that interval has this sign. These intervals are disjoint and ordered. The first coefficient of \(Q\), one such coefficient from each interval, and its last coefficient consequently give an alternating subsequence with \(V(P)+1\) changes. This proves (RC6), including internal zero coefficients.

If a positive number is a root of a polynomial, ordinary finite polynomial division factors out its linear factor. Repeating (RC6) shows that a nonzero polynomial has at most \(V(P)\) positive roots **counted with their algebraic multiplicities**. In particular a polynomial with at most \(D\) nonzero monomials has at most \(D-1\) positive roots counted with multiplicity. This is all that is needed below; neither an intermediate-value theorem nor a polynomial-root existence theorem is required.

#### C2.2. Transpose the alleged zero matrix

Suppose \(D\) distinct integers \(n_1<\cdots<n_D\) were zeros of \(u\). Form the square matrix with rows indexed by these integers and columns indexed by the pairs \((j,k)\), \(0\le k<d_j\), with entries
\[
n_i^k a_j^{n_i}.
\]
The nonzero coefficient vector of the \(P_j\) lies in its kernel, so finite linear algebra gives a nonzero vector \((b_1,\ldots,b_D)\) in the kernel of its transpose. Thus the nonzero Laurent polynomial
\[
Q(X)=\sum_{i=1}^D b_iX^{n_i}
\]
satisfies
\[
\left(X\frac{d}{dX}\right)^kQ(a_j)=0,
\qquad 0\le k<d_j. \tag{RC8}
\]
The identity in (RC8) is just the finite monomial rule
\((X\,d/dX)^k X^n=n^kX^n\), valid also for negative integer \(n\).

By induction using the product rule,
\[
(X\,d/dX)^k=X^k(d/dX)^k+
\text{a linear combination of lower formal derivative orders}.
\]
Since \(a_j\ne0\), (RC8), successively in \(k\), implies \(Q^{(k)}(a_j)=0\) for \(k<d_j\). Multiplying by a sufficiently large positive power of \(X\) makes \(Q\) an ordinary polynomial and does not alter its multiplicity at a positive root. Finite division, or the finite binomial expansion about \(a_j\), says that \(a_j\) is a root of multiplicity at least \(d_j\).

The resulting polynomial has at most \(D\) nonzero monomials but at least \(\sum_jd_j=D\) positive roots counted with multiplicity. This contradicts C2.1 and proves (RC5). ∎

No sign-change formula for the coefficients of the polynomial amplitudes \(P_j(n)\) is being asserted. The simple-mode sign budget and the repeated-root dimension budget are different statements.

### C3. Negative roots, identically zero classes, and transients

For distinct nonzero real roots \(\theta_j\), first fix \(\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}\) and write \(n=2t+\varepsilon\). Then, exactly,
\[
u_{2t+\varepsilon}
=\sum_{a>0}C_{a,\varepsilon}(t)a^t,\qquad
C_{a,\varepsilon}(t)=
\sum_{\theta_j^2=a}\theta_j^\varepsilon P_j(2t+\varepsilon). \tag{RC9}
\]
Only finitely many \(a\) occur. Merge the polynomials with the same \(a\), remove those identically zero, and apply C1 or C2:

- Constant amplitudes: at most the sign changes of the merged coefficients ordered by \(a\), hence at most one less than their number.
- Polynomial amplitudes: at most
  \[
  \sum_{a:C_{a,\varepsilon}\not\equiv0}
  \bigl(1+\deg C_{a,\varepsilon}\bigr)-1.
  \]
- If every merged polynomial is zero, the entire parity class is zero. A finite budget must not be attached to that class.

For example \(2^n-(-2)^n\) vanishes at every even index. The even merged coefficient is exactly zero; this is an algebraic identity, not a large collection of exceptional isolated zeros.

The theorem uses nonzero characteristic roots so that negative powers and backward indices are meaningful. A zero root in a nonnegative-index recurrence is a finite transient: if its multiplicity is \(e\), remove the first \(e\) indices and verify them separately, then apply the theorem to the nonzero roots. Equivalently, factor the characteristic polynomial as \(X^e g(X)\), with \(g(0)\ne0\), and note that \(g(E)u\) is killed by \(E^e\); beyond the transient the recurrence \(g(E)u=0\) holds. No claim about backward indices is made when the state map is singular.

Integer indices have no analytic multiplicity in these counting theorems. If one imposes
\[
u_n=\Delta u_n=\cdots=\Delta^{k-1}u_n=0,
\qquad \Delta u_n=u_{n+1}-u_n,
\]
the finite binomial formula for \(\Delta^j\) is triangular and shows that this is exactly the zero window \(u_n=\cdots=u_{n+k-1}=0\). Count those distinct indices once each; overlapping windows cannot be counted twice. The multiplicities in C2.1–C2.2 concern roots of an auxiliary polynomial and are fully algebraic.

### C4. Exact certificate and examples

An exact certificate for a recurrence marker contains:

1. Its recurrence or matrix-power identity, initial data, and a certified decomposition into real nonzero roots with polynomial amplitudes. This is finite linear algebra: applying \((E-\theta)\) to \(P(n)\theta^n\) lowers the degree of \(P\), and the prescribed finite initial values identify the solution. When a backward orbit is used, certify the nonzero constant coefficient.
2. The parity merge (RC9), including an exact decision whether every amplitude is zero. The real-root and ordering/sign certificates can use rational isolating intervals, polynomial division, and finite sign computations. The mode-count budget does not need signs of individual coefficients.
3. A list of distinct zero indices with exact values, whose size equals the proved budget. This is a saturated certificate and proves the list complete. If the list is shorter, record the remaining budget; finding some zeros does not exhaust it.

Examples proved by the finite budgets:

\[
4^n-5\,2^n+4=0
\iff n\in\{0,2\}\qquad(n\in\mathbb Z).
\]
There are two coefficient sign changes, and both zeros are exact.

\[
2^n+3^n-5=0
\iff n=1.
\]
The ordered coefficients for bases \(1,2,3\) are \((-5,1,1)\), with one sign change.

\[
2^n-n-1=0
\iff n\in\{0,1\}.
\]
Here the bases are \(1,2\), with polynomial amplitudes \(-n-1,1\), so \(D=3\) and the budget is two.

For any specified positive integer \(N\),
\[
(2^n-1)(2^n-2^N)=0
\iff n\in\{0,N\}.
\]
The budget is two. Certifying the zero at \(0\), even with a strong isolation statement near \(0\), leaves the other zero unaccounted for. This is the precise reason an unsaturated budget is not a completeness proof.

**Fibonacci example, all integer indices.** Define \(F_0=0,F_1=1\), extend \(F_{n+1}=F_n+F_{n-1}\) in both directions, and put
\(\phi=(1+\sqrt5)/2,\ \psi=(1-\sqrt5)/2\). Finite substitution in the recurrence and its first two values proves
\[
F_n=(\phi^n-\psi^n)/\sqrt5
\]
for all integers \(n\). No limiting formula is used. Since \(-1<\psi<0<1<\phi\), the bases on either parity are \(\psi^2<1<\phi^2\). For \(F_n-1\), the even coefficients have signs \((-,-,+)\), hence budget one, attained at \(n=2\). The odd coefficients have signs \((+,-,+)\), hence budget two, attained at \(n=-1,1\). Therefore
\[
\boxed{F_n=1\iff n\in\{-1,1,2\}.}
\]

P15_counting.py verifies the displayed saturation certificates, the parity-degenerate example, the Fibonacci recurrence values and sign intervals, finite positive-determinant identities, and the polynomial sign-variation lemma. The finite sample checks of universal identities are transcription checks; the proofs above establish them for all indices.

### C5. A finite norm-identity replacement for Runge/Padé smallness

The source checked is [isolation.auxiliary.md](https://github.com/ishan-1729/math-prime/blob/round/t009/research/proofs/methods/isolation.auxiliary.md), blob 4d7f61c3cd6f24074a7a9155a11d4a1c71c85541.

Its basic final certificate is already a finite integer-gap statement: an integer divisible by \(M\) and of absolute value less than \(M\) is zero. The new standard should inspect how its congruence and real bounds are obtained. Infinite analytic or \(p\)-adic expansions are not necessary when finite identities suffice.

Here is an entirely finite whole-end certificate. Let \(F,A,B\in\mathbb Q[X]\), let \(D\ge1\) clear the coefficients of \(A\) and \(B\), and put
\[
N(X)=B(X)^2-A(X)^2F(X).
\]
Suppose for every real \(X\ge X_0\) one has
\[
F(X)\ge0,\quad A(X)\ge0,\quad B(X)>0,\quad
0<D|N(X)|<B(X). \tag{RC10}
\]
Then there is no integer solution \(w^2=F(X)\) with integer \(X\ge X_0\).

**Proof.** Replace \(w\) by \(|w|\). The integer
\(I=D(A(X)w-B(X))\) is nonzero because \(N(X)\ne0\). But the exact identity
\[
|I|=\frac{D|N(X)|}{A(X)w+B(X)}
\le\frac{D|N(X)|}{B(X)}<1
\]
is impossible. ∎

Every hypothesis in (RC10) is a polynomial sign/inequality certificate. If \(F,A\) have positive leading coefficients, \(B\) has positive leading coefficient \(b_d\), and \(\deg N=h<d=\deg B\), a crude explicit cutoff comes from coefficient sums alone. If \(F\) instead has negative leading coefficient, its eventual negativity already excludes squares. For any polynomial \(P=p_eX^e+\cdots+p_0\), \(p_e\ne0\),
\[
X>\max\left(1,\frac{2\sum_{i<e}|p_i|}{|p_e|}\right)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{sgn}P(X)=\operatorname{sgn}p_e,\qquad
|P(X)|>\tfrac12|p_e|X^e.
\]
This follows by bounding the lower-degree terms by \(X^{e-1}\sum_{i<e}|p_i|\). Also
\[
|N(X)|\le X^h\sum_i|n_i|\qquad(X\ge1).
\]
Thus, in addition to sign cutoffs for \(A,B,F,N\), it is sufficient to impose
\[
X>\frac{2D\sum_i|n_i|}{b_d}.
\]
Since \(d-h\ge1\), this implies \(D|N(X)|<B(X)\). Taking one plus the ceiling of the maximum of these finitely many rational bounds gives an integer cutoff.

The approximants \(A,B\) may be searched for by solving a finite coefficient-cancellation system. Their acceptance uses only the identity for \(N\) and the polynomial inequalities, regardless of how they were found. A finite Hankel determinant or a finite Desnanot–Jacobi identity is elementary; a claim about infinitely many nonzero Hankel pivots needs a separate proof. Formal coefficient recurrences can be truncated to the finitely many coefficients actually used and checked by polynomial multiplication. No analytic interpretation of a full power series is required.

The card's finite-order derivative determinant bound also remains elementary under the clarified Rolle/mean-value permission, provided its derivative bounds are established for the actual finite order required. A \(p\)-adic series lemma should be replaced by a modulus-\(p^M\) polynomial or finite-binomial identity proving the exact divisibility used; a claim about an infinite series is not itself that finite certificate. None of these observations supplies an affordable uniform auxiliary or a missing global cover: those are still separate mathematical obligations.

## 3. Finite congruence lifting, finite zero counts, and local coverage

**PROVED.** The first-order and symmetric second-order conclusions used in **lifting.dynamical**, **lifting.somos**, and **lifting.tau** have proofs using only finite polynomial identities, binomial coefficients, and divisibility. For integer indices they do not require a \(p\)-adic interpolation, an infinite series, differentiation, or an analytic zero theorem. The theorem below makes no claim about noninteger \(p\)-adic indices.

### L1. The precise finite algebra

Fix a prime \(p\). Let \(\mathcal R\) consist of rational functions \(P/Q\) in finitely many state variables with integer coefficients, where \(Q\) reduces modulo \(p\) to a nonzero constant. Rational constants whose denominators are prime to \(p\) are allowed. Equality modulo \(p^N\) means that the difference belongs to \(p^N\mathcal R\). Thus every such function can be evaluated modulo every \(p^N\), at every integer state.

Suppose \(H(x)=x+p^eK(x)\), with \(K\in\mathcal R^k\), where
\[
e\ge1\quad(p\text{ odd}),\qquad e\ge2\quad(p=2).
\tag{L1}
\]
For \(U f=f\circ H\), put \(D=U-I\). Then
\[
D(p^a\mathcal R)\subset p^{a+e}\mathcal R.\tag{L2}
\]
This is ordinary finite-difference notation:
\[
D^jf(z)=\sum_{i=0}^j(-1)^{j-i}\binom ji f(H^iz).
\]
The equality is the finite binomial theorem for repeated substitutions.
Here is a proof without a Taylor expansion. For a monomial, replace its factors one at a time; the difference after substituting \(x+p^eK\) is divisible by \(p^e\). Add the finitely many monomials. For a quotient use
\[
\frac{P'}{Q'}-\frac P Q=\frac{P'Q-PQ'}{Q'Q}.
\]
The denominator remains a unit modulo \(p\), and the numerator has the required divisibility. The same argument shows that substitution preserves every congruence in \(\mathcal R\).

Fix any precision \(N\), and choose an integer \(J\) with \(eJ\ge N\). Then \(D^J=0\) modulo \(p^N\). At this finite precision,
\[
\boxed{U^t=\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\binom tjD^j\qquad(t\in\mathbb Z).}\tag{L3}
\]
For nonnegative \(t\) this is the finite binomial theorem. For negative \(t\), the coefficients are still integers:
\(\binom{-m}{j}=(-1)^j\binom{m+j-1}{j}\).
Pascal's identity gives \(S_{t+1}=(I+D)S_t\) for the displayed finite sum \(S_t\); \(I+D\) has the finite inverse \(I-D+\cdots+(-D)^{J-1}\). Starting at \(S_0=I\) proves (L3) in both directions. No infinite sum is taken.

The map \(H\) itself is a permutation of the state vectors modulo every \(p^N\). It is the identity modulo \(p\). If a preimage is known modulo \(p^n\), its possible lifts \(x+p^na\) satisfy
\[
H(x+p^na)\equiv H(x)+p^na\pmod{p^{n+1}},
\]
by the same finite difference argument. Exactly one digit vector \(a\) works. Induction proves bijectivity and compatibility of the inverses at different precisions. Hence negative powers in (L3) agree with negative finite-orbit iterates. Actual rational orbit values, when supplied by a recurrence, must be identified with these residues by the exact transport certificate; no inverse-limit object is necessary.

One may instead supply compatible finite coefficient maps at each modulus, provided the same divisibility identities and transport rules are proved uniformly by a finite algebraic rule. The proof below always runs at a single specified precision. A check at several sample precisions alone is not that uniform proof.

### L2. First order, including negative indices

Let \(z\) be a rational base state with denominators prime to \(p\), and \(f\in\mathcal R\); integer states are included. If
\[
v_p(f(Hz)-f(z))=e,
\]
then for every nonzero integer \(t\),
\[
\boxed{v_p(f(H^tz)-f(z))=e+v_p(t).}\tag{L4}
\]
This notation also applies when the left-hand side is an actual rational or integer quantity whose residues are represented by the finite orbit.

Put \(a=v_p(t)\). In (L3) the first term is \(tDf(z)\), of valuation \(e+a\). For \(j\ge2\),
\[
\binom tj=\frac tj\binom{t-1}{j-1}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v_p\!\left(\binom tjD^jf(z)\right)\ge a+ej-v_p(j).
\]
Under (L1), \(e(j-1)-v_p(j)\ge1\). For odd \(p\), \(p^{v_p(j)}\le j\) gives \(v_p(j)\le j-2\) when \(j\ge2\); for \(p=2\), \(v_2(j)\le j-1\) and \(e\ge2\) suffice. Choose \(N=e+a+1\) in (L3). Every term after the first vanishes modulo that precision. This proves the exact valuation, for both signs of \(t\).

The restriction at two is substantive: \(H(x)=3x\), \(z=1\), \(f=x-1\) has \(e=1\) and first increment \(2\), but its increment at \(t=2\) is \(8\), of valuation \(3\), not \(2\).

For commuting maps \(H_1,\ldots,H_r\), all satisfying (L1) at the same depth \(e\), the corresponding \(D_i\) commute. Applying the finite product of (L3) gives, for \(t=p^av\), with \(v\) primitive modulo \(p\),
\[
f(H_1^{t_1}\cdots H_r^{t_r}z)-f(z)
\equiv p^{e+a}\sum_i v_i\,\frac{D_if(z)}{p^e}
\pmod{p^{e+a+1}}.\tag{L5}
\]
In every term of total difference order at least two, select one nonzero binomial index \(j_i\); the same bound \(a-v_p(j_i)\), together with the total divisibility \(p^{e\sum j_i}\), proves the claim. Thus a full-column-rank matrix of the first differences of finitely many markers modulo \(p\) isolates the zero index. This is an elementary simultaneous congruence argument.

### L3. Symmetric second order by a finite Chebyshev identity

Assume in addition that the observed orbit is even:
\[
a(t):=f(H^tz),\qquad a(-t)=a(t)\quad(t\in\mathbb Z).
\tag{L6}
\]
A finite sufficient certificate is an involution \(I\) satisfying \(Iz=z\), \(fI=f\), and \(IHI=H^{-1}\), as identities on each finite residue system. Put
\[
\Delta_2=a(2)-2a(1)+a(0)=D^2f(z).
\]
If \(v_p(\Delta_2)=2e\), then
\[
\boxed{v_p(a(t)-a(0))=2e-v_p(2)+2v_p(t)\quad(t\ne0).}\tag{L7}
\]

Work at a fixed finite precision. Define \(W=U+U^{-1}-2=D^2U^{-1}\); it raises divisibility by \(2e\). For every integer \(t\),
\[
U^t+U^{-t}-2=\sum_{j=1}^{|t|}c_j(t)W^j,\tag{L8}
\]
where
\[
c_j(t)=\binom{|t|+j}{2j}+\binom{|t|+j-1}{2j}
=\frac{2t^2}{(2j)!}\prod_{h=1}^{j-1}(t^2-h^2).
\tag{L9}
\]
These are integers and \(c_1(t)=t^2\). To prove the identity, set \(P_t=U^t+U^{-t}\). Direct multiplication gives
\[
P_0=2,\quad P_1=W+2,\quad P_{t+1}=(W+2)P_t-P_{t-1}.
\]
Pascal's identity, twice, gives
\[
c_j(t+1)-2c_j(t)+c_j(t-1)=c_{j-1}(t),
\]
with \(c_0(t)=2\). The initial values are exactly those of \(P_0,P_1\). This proves (L8) by induction; (L9) follows by multiplying the consecutive factors in the binomial coefficient. Negative \(t\) is covered because both sides are even.

At precision \(p^N\), terms with \(2ej\ge N\) can already be discarded. Thus only finitely many differences determined by the requested precision need be formed, even when \(|t|\) is very large.

Here is the complete tail estimate, involving only factorial divisibility. Counting multiples of \(p,p^2,\ldots\) in \(n!\), and then writing \(n\) in base \(p\), gives
\[
v_p(n!)=\sum_{h\ge1}\lfloor n/p^h\rfloor
=\frac{n-\text{sum of its base-}p\text{ digits}}{p-1}
\le\frac{n-1}{p-1}.
\]
Every displayed sum is finite. Thus \(v_p((2j)!)\le j-1\) for odd \(p\), and \(v_2((2j)!)\le2j-1\). If \(a=v_p(t)\), (L9) gives
\[
v_p(c_j(t)W^jf(z))
\ge2a+v_p(2)-v_p((2j)!)+2ej
\ge2a+2e+1\quad(j\ge2).
\]
For odd \(p\) the spare amount is at least \(j-1\); for \(p=2,e\ge2\) it is at least \(2j-2\). Moreover
\[
W-D^2=-D^3U^{-1},
\]
so \(Wf(z)\equiv\Delta_2\pmod{p^{3e}}\).
Using evenness in (L8), at precision \(2e+2a+1\), proves the useful explicit congruence
\[
\boxed{2(a(t)-a(0))\equiv t^2\Delta_2
\pmod{p^{2e+2v_p(t)+1}}.}\tag{L10}
\]
Its right side has valuation \(2e+2a\). Dividing the valuation by the known factor \(2\) proves (L7), including its one-power shift at two.

For commuting maps and a common reversing involution, define finite second differences
\[
C_{ii}=D_i^2f(z)/p^{2e},\qquad
C_{ij}=D_iD_jf(z)/p^{2e}\quad(i<j),
\]
and the \(p\)-integral quadratic expression (a common denominator prime to \(p\) may be cleared)
\[
Q(v)=\sum_iC_{ii}v_i^2+2\sum_{i<j}C_{ij}v_iv_j.\tag{L11}
\]
For a primitive vector \(v\), apply the one-index argument to
\(\Gamma=H_1^{v_1}\cdots H_r^{v_r}\).
Finite binomial multiplication gives
\[
(U_\Gamma-I)^2f(z)\equiv p^{2e}Q(v)\pmod{p^{3e}}.
\]
If \(Q(v)\not\equiv0\pmod p\), then (L7) applied at \(p^a\) gives valuation \(2e-v_p(2)+2a\) on that ray. A finite family of \(Q\)'s with no common primitive zero modulo \(p\) therefore isolates the origin in every integer direction.

More generally, choose \(A\ge1\), and assume \(e\ge A\) as well as (L1). The same proof strengthens (L10) to modulus \(p^{2e+2v_p(t)+A}\): the discarded terms have at least \(e\ge A\) extra powers. If the quadratic forms have no common primitive zero modulo \(p^A\), then for each primitive \(v\), some form has \(q=v_p(Q(v))<A\); its marker has exact valuation
\[
2e-v_p(2)+2a+q.
\]
Checking primitive vectors modulo \(p^A\) is a finite certificate. This replaces the quadratic primitive-jet isolation statement without an analytic interpolation.

At \(p=2\), (L11), followed by the factor \(2\) in (L10), is the correct formulation. One must not write a coefficient \(1/2\) in \(\mathbb F_2\). Nor does the odd-prime card's depth-one drift matrix remain unchanged when the depth is raised to two.

### L3A. A finite polynomial replacement for one-variable Strassmann counting

The relevant integer-zero counting conclusion also has a finite proof. No infinite series is needed if finite polynomial approximants with a certified common coefficient reduction are supplied.

**Finite separation lemma.** Let \(P\in\mathbb Z_{(p)}[X]\) be nonzero, let \(\nu\) be the smallest valuation of its coefficients, and let \(d\) be the largest index at which that minimum occurs. Let \(n_0,\ldots,n_d\) be distinct integers. They cannot all satisfy
\[
v_p(P(n_i))\ge N,\qquad
N>\nu+\max_i\sum_{j\ne i}v_p(n_i-n_j).
\tag{L15}
\]
Here \(\mathbb Z_{(p)}\) denotes rational numbers whose denominators are prime to \(p\); no local-ring theorem is used.

**Proof.** Let \(R\) be the degree-at-most-\(d\) polynomial interpolating the values \(P(n_i)\):
\[
R(X)=\sum_{i=0}^d
\frac{P(n_i)}{\prod_{j\ne i}(n_i-n_j)}
\prod_{j\ne i}(X-n_j).
\]
Every coefficient of \(R\) has valuation strictly greater than \(\nu\), by (L15), since the numerator polynomials have integer coefficients. Consequently \(Q=P-R\) has the same minimum coefficient valuation \(\nu\) and the same largest minimal index \(d\). It vanishes at every specified \(n_i\). Divide \(Q\) by the monic integer polynomial
\[
D(X)=\prod_{i=0}^d(X-n_i).
\]
Ordinary monic division uses only addition and multiplication of \(p\)-integral coefficients. Its remainder has degree at most \(d\) and vanishes at \(d+1\) distinct rational points, hence is zero, by the elementary factor theorem. Thus \(Q=DS\) with \(S\in\mathbb Z_{(p)}[X]\).

If \(\eta\) is the minimum valuation of \(S\)'s coefficients, the reductions of \(D\) and \(p^{-\eta}S\) modulo \(p\) are nonzero. Their product is nonzero, so the minimum valuation of \(Q\) is \(\eta\); therefore \(\eta=\nu\). But that product has degree at least \(d+1\), whereas \(p^{-\nu}Q\) has reduction of degree \(d\). This contradiction proves the lemma. Only finite interpolation, monic division, and multiplication of polynomials over \(\mathbb F_p\) occurred.

**Finite-approximation consequence.** Suppose, for every sufficiently large integer \(N\), a finite polynomial \(P_N\) represents the same integer-index marker \(a(t)\) modulo \(p^N\), and every \(P_N\) has the same minimum coefficient valuation \(\nu\) and largest minimal index \(d\). Then \(a(t)=0\) has at most \(d\) distinct integer solutions. Otherwise choose \(d+1\) such integers and then choose \(N\) exceeding the explicit finite bound in (L15).

The same statement excludes \(d+1\) compatible candidate residue branches once their pairwise separating digits have been specified and \(N\) exceeds that bound. It does **not** assert that a single congruence modulo \(p^N\) has at most \(d\) residues: multiple residues can coalesce near one root.

For the systems of L1 there is an explicit finite certificate for these hypotheses. Set
\[
P_N(X)=\sum_{0\le j<\lceil N/e\rceil}\binom XjD^jf(z).
\tag{L16}
\]
Equation (L3) gives its values at every integer \(t\), positive or negative. Since \(j!\binom Xj\) has integer coefficients, every coefficient of its \(j\)-th summand has valuation at least
\[
ej-v_p(j!).
\]
Under (L1) this is at least \((j+1)/2\) for \(j\ge1\). Compute a finite polynomial
\[
P_*(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{J_0-1}\binom XjD^jf(z),
\]
its actual coefficient minimum \(\nu\), and its largest minimal index \(d\). If \(P_*\ne0\) and \(J_0\ge2\nu+2\), every later summand has all coefficient valuations greater than \(\nu\). Hence every \(P_N\) with \(N\ge eJ_0\) has precisely the same \(\nu,d\). This is a finite, directly checkable certificate for the zero budget.

There is also an explicit way to choose a sufficient truncation from any known nonzero orbit value. If \(v_p(a(t_0))=A<\infty\), take \(J_0=2A+2\). All omitted coefficients have valuation greater than \(A\), so finite congruence (L3) at \(t_0\) shows that the resulting \(P_*\) is nonzero and has \(\nu\le A\). Thus it satisfies the certificate test. If the marker is identically zero, no such nonzero-value certificate exists; a finite zero budget is then false.

**The tangential two-root case survives.** Let \(p\ge5\), \(e\ge1\), and suppose
\[
a(0)=a(-1)=0,\qquad v_p(a(1))=2e.
\]
Finite expansion at \(-1\), modulo \(p^{3e}\), gives \(D^2f(z)\equiv Df(z)=a(1)\pmod{p^{3e}}\). For \(j\ge3\),
\[
ej-v_p(j!)\ge ej-(j-1)/4>2e.
\]
Therefore the common normalized coefficient reduction of the sufficiently long finite polynomials is
\[
p^{-2e}P_N(X)\equiv
\frac{a(1)/p^{2e}}2 X(X+1)\pmod p.
\]
Its degree is two. The finite separation lemma proves that there are at most two integer zeros, and the two displayed zeros are already known. Thus the integer zeros are exactly \(0,-1\), without an analytic interpolation or an analytic factorization. This retains the integer exclusion implication used by the tangential-return lemma in P07.

This replacement addresses the local counting step. It does not manufacture genus-two coordinates, a finite residue cover, an exact arithmetic marker, or a substitute for a separately used analytic integral construction. Those remain separate proof obligations.

### L3B. Local saturation by finite congruence quotients

The assertion that a rank-one rational group is locally covered by a generator also has a purely finite formulation. It is useful when a candidate generator has an unknown integer index, but its index is known to be prime to \(p\).

Let \(A\) be a certified finitely generated abelian group, embedded in an ambient abelian group with subgroups
\[
U_1\supset U_2\supset\cdots,\qquad
pU_j\subset U_{j+1},\qquad A\cap\bigcap_jU_j=\{0\}.
\tag{L17}
\]
Suppose \(L=A\cap U_1\) is torsion-free of rank one. Thus \(L=\mathbb ZE\); this elementary cyclic-group statement follows by identifying the rank-one subgroup with integer multiples of a primitive rational direction and taking its least positive coefficient. Let \(D\in L\setminus U_2\). Writing \(D=kE\), one has \(p\nmid k\), because \(pL\subset U_2\).

For every \(P=mE\in L\) and every \(N\ge1\), solve the ordinary integer congruence
\[
ka_N\equiv m\pmod{p^{N-1}}.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{P-a_ND\in U_N.}\tag{L18}
\]
Indeed \(m-ka_N\) is a multiple of \(p^{N-1}\), and repeated use of (L17) gives \(p^{N-1}L\subset U_N\). The solutions can be chosen compatible as \(N\) increases, by the ordinary modular inverse of \(k\). At no stage is a noninteger multiple of \(D\) introduced.

The higher-rank version is just finite integer linear algebra. Suppose \(L\) is free of rank \(r\), with basis \(E_1,\ldots,E_r\), and \(D_1,\ldots,D_r\in L\) have independent images in \(U_1/U_2\) over \(\mathbb F_p\). Write their coefficient columns in the \(E\)-basis as the integer matrix \(M\). Then \(p\nmid\det M\). Otherwise some nonzero vector modulo \(p\) would give a combination of the \(D_i\) in \(pL\subset U_2\), contradicting that independence. The adjugate matrix and the modular inverse of \(\det M\) solve
\[
Ma_N\equiv m\pmod{p^{N-1}},
\]
and give
\[
P-\sum_i(a_N)_iD_i\in U_N.
\tag{L19}
\]
A separate finite torsion certificate is required before assuming \(L\) free; a residue computation is not a proof that torsion has disappeared.

The local hypotheses (L17) can be proved from finite addition formulas. Suppose an identity chart has rational coordinates \(x\), its identity is \(x=0\), and its addition and inverse have unit denominators and satisfy the finite identities
\[
F(x,y)-(x+y)\in (x,y)^2\mathcal R^k,\qquad
I(x)+x\in(x)^2\mathcal R^k.
\]
The notation on the right means an explicitly finite sum of monomials of degree at least two times rational functions with unit denominators; it does not mean an infinite expansion. Define \(U_j\) by coordinate divisibility by \(p^j\). The identities prove closure under addition and inversion. Adding \(x\) to itself \(p\) times gives
\[
[p]x\equiv px\pmod{p^{2j}}\qquad(x\in U_j),
\]
so \(pU_j\subset U_{j+1}\), since \(2j\ge j+1\). A nonzero rational point cannot have all its coordinates divisible by every power of \(p\): some nonzero rational coordinate has finite valuation. If the chart's zero coordinate represents only the identity, this proves the intersection assertion in (L17). All assertions about the chart, addition, and identity are finite formula obligations.

Here is the connection with L3A. Suppose a residue class is \(R+L\), and the exact arithmetic condition is represented by a marker that preserves congruences modulo \(U_N\). In rank one, a rational point satisfying that condition yields integers \(a_N\) for which the orbit marker at \(R+a_ND\) vanishes modulo \(p^N\). If \(d+1\) distinct rational points were present, (L17) and compatibility ensure that their parameter residues eventually have fixed, finite pairwise separating digits. Choosing \(N\) above the bound in (L15) contradicts the certified degree-\(d\) finite coefficient budget. Thus the local rational-point count follows from finite quotients and the finite polynomial lemma.

The translation return and marker must satisfy the hypotheses of L1–L3A. One may first split into finitely many index classes to reach the required depth; at two, for example, replace a depth-one return by its square and retain both parity classes.

If marker transport loses a fixed number of powers of \(p\), the same proof simply raises \(N\) by that certified amount. The higher-rank coverage (L19) can likewise feed the multivariable finite marker lemmas; it does not by itself supply a zero-count theorem for an underdetermined system.

This is a conditional local coverage theorem with a complete proof. It does not establish an equation's global group rank, torsion, identity chart, residue-class list, or marker incidence. In particular it does not by itself certify that every genus-two proof in the repository has had each such input rechecked under the clarified standard.

### L4. Polynomial norm markers also have a finite proof

Let \(C\) be an integer \(d\)-by-\(d\) matrix and
\(N(x)=\det(\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}x_iC^i)\). Determinants here are finite polynomial identities.

If \(C\) represents multiplication by \(X\) in \(\mathbb Z[X]/(g)\), for a monic \(g\) irreducible modulo \(p\), then \(N(x)\) is a unit modulo \(p\) whenever \(x\) is primitive modulo \(p\): multiplication by its nonzero residue is invertible in the finite field \(\mathbb F_p[X]/(g)\), by the polynomial Euclidean algorithm. Therefore
\[
v_p(N(p^ax))=da\qquad(x\text{ primitive}).
\]

For the ramified cyclic matrix \(Ce_i=e_{i+1}\), \(Ce_{d-1}=pe_0\), one has \(C^d=pI\) and, for every nonzero integer vector \(x\), with \(v_p(0)=+\infty\),
\[
v_p(N(x))=\min_{0\le i<d}(d\,v_p(x_i)+i).\tag{L12}
\]
The weights are distinct modulo \(d\). Modulo \(p\), the multiplication matrix is triangular with diagonal \(x_0\), so \(N(x)\equiv x_0^d\pmod p\). If the minimum weight is positive, \(p\mid x_0\); set
\(R(x)=(x_1,\ldots,x_{d-1},x_0/p)\).
The exact identity
\[
C\sum_iR(x)_iC^i=\sum_ix_iC^i
\]
gives \(N(x)=(-1)^{d-1}pN(R(x))\). The weights rotate and decrease by one. After exactly their minimum number of such finite steps, the new first coordinate is a unit, proving (L12).

Apply (L5) to the state coordinates themselves. If their drift matrix is injective modulo \(p\), a displacement at \(t=p^av\) is
\[
H^tz-z=p^{e+a}(Lv+p\,u)
\]
with a primitive parenthesis. A marker that is exactly the first norm above has valuation \(d(e+a)\). For the cyclic norm it has valuation \(d(e+a)+j\), where \(j\) is the first nonzero coordinate of \(Lv\) modulo \(p\). Thus one polynomial norm marker can isolate several integer indices by finite arithmetic. A claimed more general “norm-leading analytic jet” still needs an exact finite remainder estimate; naming its leading term alone is insufficient.

### L5. Producing the return and carrying the raw tau value

The return test can also be stated without differentiation. Suppose a finite rational map \(F\), with unit denominators on the residue class of \(z\), satisfies \(F(z)\equiv z\pmod p\). Substitute \(z+pw\), expand its finitely many monomials by the binomial theorem, and use the quotient identity from L1. This gives
\[
T(w)=\frac{F(z+pw)-z}{p}\equiv b+Lw\pmod p,
\]
where \(L\) is the matrix of coefficients of the linear terms. If \(L\) is invertible modulo \(p\), the affine map is a permutation of a finite set. Compute its order \(q\); then \(T^q\equiv I\pmod p\) coefficientwise. At two square once more:
\[
(I+2K)^2(w)=w+2K(w)+2K(w+2K(w))\equiv w\pmod4.
\]
All of this is finite coefficient arithmetic. Merely testing a nonlinear polynomial at all points modulo \(p\) does not certify coefficientwise divisibility.

For a homogeneous bilinear tau recurrence, the exact gauge identity is an integer-exponent identity:
\[
R^{Nt}=G_{A^tB^{N\binom t2},B^t}H^t,\qquad H=G_{A,B}^{-1}R^N.
\]
It follows by finite induction from \(RG_{a,b}=G_{ab,b}R\); negative indices follow by inversion. No analytic idea occurs in it.

The distinction between normalized and raw values remains essential. Suppose
\[
\tau_{c+Nn}=C(n)f(H^nz),\qquad
C(n)=A^nB^{N\binom n2},
\]
with \(A,B\) units modulo \(p\). Assume \(H\) satisfies L1. Choose a common order \(E_0\) of \(A,B\) in the finite unit group modulo \(p^{2e}\), and put \(L=2E_0\). For \(0\le b<L\),
\[
C(b+Lt)=C(b)D_b^tE^{\binom t2},\quad
D_b=A^LB^{N(Lb+\binom L2)},\quad E=B^{NL^2}.
\]
Both \(D_b,E\) are \(1\) modulo \(p^{2e}\). On variables \((x,s,v)\), use the polynomial/rational return
\[
\mathcal T_b(x,s,v)=
\left(H^Lx,\ D_b(1+p^ev)s,\
Ev+(E-1)/p^e\right).\tag{L13}
\]
It is coefficientwise the identity modulo \(p^e\). Starting at \((H^bz,1,0)\), its iterates satisfy
\[
1+p^ev_t=E^t,\qquad s_t=D_b^tE^{\binom t2}.
\]
Thus the **raw** marker is exactly
\[
C(b)s f(x)-w.\tag{L14}
\]
The first or second finite theorem applies only after its actual finite differences have been checked. Symmetry of \(f(H^tz)\) does not automatically give symmetry of (L14).

If the exact ledger instead identifies the raw difference with \(p^b\) times a unit times the marker difference, add \(b\) to the proved valuation. The unit may vary with the index; the certificate must prove that it remains a unit at every retained state.

The zero-aware polynomial suspension from P12 also survives. Its identities are finite polynomial identities, and its inverse scale is found digit by digit from \(S^h=u\), with \(h\) prime to \(p(p-1)\). The binomial identity
\[
(S+p^na)^h\equiv S^h+hS^{h-1}p^na\pmod{p^{n+1}}
\]
gives its unique next digit. Only finite rings are needed. Unit endpoints, cancelled block identities, and a complete phase itinerary remain required certificates.

### L6. The P12 Somos-4 test is retained, without its analytic proof

For the all-one Somos-4 sequence at two, P12's polynomial five-step lift \(\Phi\) has the finite values
\[
\Phi^2(1,1,1,1,1)\equiv(25,17,17,9,1)\pmod{32},
\]
\[
\Phi^4(1,1,1,1,1)\equiv(17,1,1,17,1)\pmod{32}.
\]
The matrix of coefficients of the linear terms of \(\Phi\), reduced modulo two on odd anchors, is reversal on the first four coordinates and identity on the scale. Its square is the identity. Binomial substitution therefore proves

T(w)=\frac{\Phi^2(1+4w)-1}{4}\equiv w\pmod2,\qquad H=T^2\equiv w\pmod4.

For \(f(w)=w_0-w_4\), the second displayed constant gives \(f(H0)\equiv4\pmod8\). The exact raw ledger is

\tau_{20t}-1=4f(H^t0)/S_t,\qquad S_t\text{ odd}.

\boxed{v_2(\tau_{20t}-1)=4+v_2(t)\quad(t\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}).}

## 4. Integer-height coverage and a new S-003 descent

### 4.1. The height compiler needs an integer descent, not an analytic height

**PROVED.** Let \(A\) be an abelian group with a positive integer height \(H\) having finite sublevel sets. Suppose a finite, certified set \(\mathcal R\) represents \(A/mA\), where \(m\ge2\), and suppose
\[
H(mQ)\ge H(Q)^d/B,\qquad
H(P-R)\le C_R H(P)^b,
\quad d>b\ge0,
\]
with explicit positive rational constants. Then \(A\) is generated by \(\mathcal R\) and the finite set of elements of height at most any integer \(K\) satisfying
\[
K^{d-b}>B\max_{R\in\mathcal R}C_R.
\]

Indeed, write \(P=R+mQ\). If \(H(P)>K\), then
\[
H(Q)^d\le BC_R H(P)^b<H(P)^d,
\]
so \(H(Q)<H(P)\). Repeatedly replace \(P\) by \(Q\). A positive integer decreases, so the process terminates. Reading the equations backward expresses the original element in the subgroup generated by the asserted finite set. This is a proof of finite generation for the actual group once the coset cover and height bounds have been certified.

Here is the finite algebra behind a typical height lower bound. Let \(k\) be a primitive integral coordinate vector, \(F_h(k)=0\) its equations, and \(\delta_j\) the degree-\(d\) multiplication polynomials. All polynomials in the following certificate have integer coefficients, and \(D\) is a nonzero integer. Suppose the certificate gives
\[
Dk_i^N=\sum_jR_{ij}(k)\delta_j(k)+\sum_hS_{ih}(k)F_h(k),
\quad D\ne0,
\]
where the degrees of \(R_{ij}\) are at most \(N-d\). If \(g\) is the gcd of the \(\delta_j(k)\), then \(g\mid Dk_i^N\) for every \(i\). Since the \(k_i\) are primitive, so are their \(N\)-th powers, and hence \(g\mid D\). Coefficient-sum bounds on the \(R_{ij}\), applied to a coordinate with \(|k_i|=H(k)\), bound \(\max_j|\delta_j(k)|\) below by a fixed positive rational multiple of \(H(k)^d\). Dividing by \(g\le|D|\) proves the normalized height inequality.

Every step is a finite polynomial identity, gcd argument, or inequality. A nonzero integer coefficient minor can supply the identities through its adjugate. It does not supply the separate halving/coset cover. This distinction is the relevant audit point for [coverage.height_compiler][height] and the genus-two applications.

### 4.2. A finite substitute for a small-element volume argument

**PROVED small-element lemma.** Let \(\mathcal A\) be an explicitly given order of degree \(d\), with basis \(e_1,\ldots,e_d\), and let \(I\subset\mathcal A\) be a full-rank integer sublattice of index \(h\). Let \(C\) be the sum of the absolute coefficients of the norm polynomial
\[
N(X_1e_1+\cdots+X_de_d).
\]
There is a nonzero \(x\in I\) satisfying
\[
0<|N(x)|\le Ch.
\]

Choose the unique integer \(r\ge1\) with \(r^d\le h<(r+1)^d\). The \((r+1)^d\) vectors with coordinates in \(\{0,\ldots,r\}\) occupy only \(h\) cosets modulo \(I\). Two have the same coset. Their nonzero difference has all coordinates of absolute value at most \(r\), lies in \(I\), and has norm of absolute value at most \(Cr^d\le Ch\). Its norm is nonzero because the ambient algebra is a field. This uses pigeonhole and a homogeneous polynomial bound.

This lemma is a concrete replacement for the **small-element part** of a volume argument. To turn it into a complete square-allocation representative bound, one must also prove the relevant inverse-lattice, multiplication-index and reconstruction identities. The lemma does not assert those identities for every stable lattice. The card's adjugate/odd-determinant allocation theorem remains elementary once its finite representatives and identities are supplied; a completeness claim cannot be obtained merely by calling an ideal a lattice.

For example, if a finite relation matrix has odd determinant \(D=2h+1\), its adjugate expresses the appropriate \(D\)-th powers through the listed representatives. For a class with a separately certified square relation, the identity
\[
I=I^D(I^2)^{-h}
\]
is the entire odd-order elimination step, interpreted in the equivalence group whose multiplication/scalar witnesses have been certified. For an \(m\)-th power relation use integers \(u,v\) with \(uD+vm=1\). These are finite identities; coverage of the representative list is still an input.

### 4.3. A general finite unit-move certificate

The next theorem is a different route to unit coverage. It uses a positive **integer** height and does not construct a logarithm lattice.

**PROVED.** Let \(\mathcal A\) be an explicit order in a totally real degree-\(d\) field, with real embeddings \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_d\). Supply finitely many norm-one units \(u_j\), their integral inverses, rational upper bounds
\[
R_{jk}\ge\sigma_k(u_j)^2-1,
\]
and a rational \(\varepsilon>0\). Suppose that for each \(i\) there are nonnegative rational weights \(\lambda_{ij}\) satisfying, componentwise,
\[
\sum_j\lambda_{ij}R_{jk}\le 1_{i=k}-\varepsilon
\quad(1\le k\le d).
\tag{H1}
\]
For every nonzero integer norm \(m\), the solutions \(N(v)=m\), \(v\in\mathcal A\), are covered by finitely many explicitly enumerable seeds and the group generated by the \(u_j\).

**Proof.** Put \(W_k=\sigma_k(v)^2>0\) and \(H=\operatorname{Tr}(v^2)=\sum_kW_k\), a positive integer. If no unit move decreases \(H\), then \(\sum_kR_{jk}W_k\ge0\) for every \(j\). Multiply by \(\lambda_{ij}\), add, and use (H1):
\[
0\le W_i-\varepsilon H.
\]
Thus every \(W_i\ge\varepsilon H\), whereas \(\prod_iW_i=m^2\). Consequently
\[
(\varepsilon H)^d\le m^2. \tag{H2}
\]
Choose an integer cutoff by direct comparison of powers, for example any \(H_0\) with \((\varepsilon H_0)^d\ge m^2\). Above that cutoff some move decreases the positive integer \(H\). Repeated moves terminate.

Let \(G\) be the trace Gram matrix of the given basis. It is positive definite. Cauchy–Schwarz gives, for every basis coefficient \(c_i\),
\[
c_i^2\le (G^{-1})_{ii}H_0.
\]
These finitely many rational inequalities give a finite coefficient box. Enumerate the norm-\(m\) elements in it. Undoing the recorded unit moves gives the claimed coverage. No assertion is made that suitable moves (H1) can be found for every order by a predetermined small search. ∎

The search for the weights is a finite linear-inequality search. The accepted certificate consists of the displayed rational weights and comparisons, so it does not rely on the optimizer used to find them. In the S-003 order, three rows and their conjugates suffice.

### 4.4. S-003: exact trace descent and complete unit coverage

**PROVED, elementary.** The proposed trace descent gives complete norm-one unit coverage in the actual S-003 order, with no logarithms, rounding in a real logarithm space, general unit theorem, infinite series, or analytic compactness argument. It does not by itself classify the intersection of those unit orbits with the Thue plane.

#### 2. Why a decreasing move exists

Let \(v\in\mathcal A\) have norm one, and put
\[
W_i=\sigma^i(v)^2>0,\qquad H=\sum_{i=0}^3W_i.
\]
Suppose none of the listed moves decreases \(H\). For \(j=1,2,3\),
\[
0\le H(g_jv)-H(v)
=\sum_i\bigl(\sigma^i(g_j)^2-1\bigr)W_i
\le\sum_iR_{ji}W_i.
\]
Multiply these three inequalities by the positive \(\lambda_j\), add, and use (U3):
\[
0\le\sum_i(\lambda^TR)_iW_i
\le W_0-H/150.
\]
Thus \(W_0\ge H/150\). Applying the identical argument to each common cyclic shift of the three rows, which is available because all conjugate moves were included, gives
\[
W_i\ge H/150\qquad(0\le i<4).
\]
But
\[
\prod_iW_i=N(v)^2=1.
\]
If \(H>150\), every \(W_i>1\), contradicting this product. Therefore
\[
H(v)>150\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{some listed unit move strictly decreases }H(v). \tag{U4}
\]

Since \(H(v)\) is a positive integer, repeatedly selecting a decreasing move terminates after finitely many steps at \(v'\) with \(H(v')\le150\). Every step remains in the order and preserves norm one. The moves may be found by evaluating the twelve resulting integer heights; the rational bounds (U1) are used only to prove that a successful move exists whenever needed.

Equivalently this follows by expanding a square in the positive quadratic form; no analytic theorem is used.

#### 4. The ghost-plane ledger limitation

The S-003 Thue elements are
\[
v=w-\beta u=(w-u)+3ue+2ut-2uet,\qquad
\beta=1-3e-2t+2et.
\]
Their marked conditions are
\[
c_t+c_{et}=0,\qquad 2c_e-3c_t=0,\qquad c_t\equiv0\pmod2.
\]
Unit multiplication preserves the ambient norm equation, but it does not preserve these conditions.

This is true even for an actual decreasing move from a known marked point. The point \((u,w)=(2,1)\) has
\[
v=(-1,6,4,-4),\qquad N(v)=1,\qquad H(v)=356.
\]
Multiplication by \(ts^{-1}e\) gives
\[
v'=(-1,2,1,-1),\qquad H(v')=34.
\]
The first marker is still zero, but
\[
2c_e-3c_t=1,\qquad c_t\equiv1\pmod2.
\]
Thus the decreasing step has left the Thue plane.

The exact transport rule is finite. If multiplication by the chosen move has integer matrix \(M\), write \(v'=Mv\). A row marker \(\ell v=0\) becomes
\[
(\ell M^{-1})v'=0,
\]
and the parity row is transported by the same integer inverse modulo two. The inverse is integral because the multiplier is an explicit norm-one unit. Hence the descent is a valid ghost move when the transported marker rows are carried in its ledger.

What is not proved is a finite classification of all possible transported ledgers, or a decreasing move that preserves the original mark. The finite seed box therefore completes **coverage**, but does not impose a finite bound on the exponents of original marked elements. It cannot by itself replace P14's excluded logarithmic separation step or turn the finite enumeration of the normalization box into an exhaustive Thue search. A plane-preserving descent, a finite marked-ledger reduction, or another allowed separator is still needed for that last step.

### 4.5. The coverage uses the actual quartic field, and gives independent indices

For clarity, the algebra in §4.4 is a field of degree four, not a formal four-dimensional algebra being assumed to be a field. Take
\[
e=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2},\qquad
t=e+\sqrt{e+2}=e+\frac12\sqrt{10+2\sqrt5}.
\]
The polynomial \(X^2-X-1\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\). The norm of \(e+2\) from \(\mathbb Q(e)\) to \(\mathbb Q\) is \(5\). If \(e+2\) were a square in \(\mathbb Q(e)\), its rational norm would be a rational square, contradicting the elementary irrationality of \(\sqrt5\). Thus adjoining \(t\) doubles the degree. Both \(e+2\) and its conjugate \(3-e\) are positive, so all four embeddings are real. The displayed automorphism identities exhibit all four of them. No claim that the order is maximal is needed for the Thue coverage.

The unit indices are also independent, by a proof with integer powers. Suppose
\[
t^a s^b e^c=\pm1.
\]
Multiply by the image under \(\sigma^2\). Since
\(\sigma^2t=-t^{-1}\), \(\sigma^2s=-s^{-1}\), and \(\sigma^2e=e\), this gives
\[
(-1)^{a+b}e^{2c}=1.
\]
As \(e>1\), this forces \(c=0\). Taking absolute values of the original relation and of its \(\sigma\)-image gives
\[
|t|^a|s|^b=1,\qquad |s|^a|t|^{-b}=1.
\]
Raise these respectively to \(a\) and \(-b\) and multiply. It follows that
\[
|t|^{a^2+b^2}=1.
\]
Since \(t>1\), \(a=b=0\). Thus §4.4 proves the actual norm-one orbit presentation
\[
\{v\in\mathcal A:N(v)=1\}=\{\pm t^a s^b e^c:(a,b,c)\in\mathbb Z^3\},
\]
with unique sign and exponent triple, using neither logarithms nor a unit theorem.

The exact norm identity is
\[
N(w-\beta u)=w^4-6w^3u-29w^2u^2+54wu^3-19u^4,
\quad\beta=1-3e-2t+2et.
\]
Consequently every integral solution of the S-003 norm-one Thue equation is covered. This assertion is independent of whether all the marked points have been isolated.

The parity entry in the ledger is useful for reconstructing \(u=c_t/2\). With the two row equations imposed exactly over the integers it is redundant: \(2c_e=3c_t\) already forces \(c_t\) even. It is not a third independent marker.

### 4.6. Why a literal finite ledger of transported planes cannot be assumed

Let \(P\) be the rational plane spanned by \(1\) and \(\beta\), with the two row equations in §4.4. Then
\[
P\cap\mathbb Q(e)=\mathbb Q.
\]
Indeed a vector in \(P\) has \(t,et\)-coefficients \(2u,-2u\); both vanish only when \(u=0\).

**PROVED.** The planes \(e^nP\), \(n\in\mathbb Z\), are all distinct. If \(e^mP=e^nP\), then \(e^{m-n}P=P\), so \(e^{m-n}\in P\cap\mathbb Q(e)=\mathbb Q\). Apply the quadratic conjugation \(e\mapsto-e^{-1}\) and take absolute values. This would give \(e^{2(m-n)}=1\), hence \(m=n\).

Thus an exact atlas that literally lists every transported plane and is closed under arbitrary unit moves is infinite. A successful finite compiler needs a proved quotient of those ledgers, a smaller set of admissible marked moves, or a reset/descent theorem. Congruence equality of two ledgers is not equality of their rational planes.

This identifies the next ghost obligation precisely: retain enough of the transported plane and parity ledger to reconstruct the original mark, while finding an additional integer rank that controls its infinitely many possible presentations. The trace rank alone has already done its job of normalizing the ambient unit.

## 5. Thomas: a finite recurrence separator and an arithmetic attack on a remaining fibre

This section uses the clarified P15 standard. They use rational arithmetic,
finite polynomial identities, the intermediate value theorem, ordinary real
differentiation justified by difference quotients, Rolle's theorem, integer
valuations, and a least-integer argument. They use no infinite power series,
integrals, logarithmic lower bounds, interpolation determinant theorem, or
complex analysis. Complex numbers below are only pairs of real numbers with
their usual algebra and Euclidean norm.

### A. PROVED: the large-parameter Thomas separator has a finite proof

**Theorem A.** For an integer $a\ge182$, the solutions of

\[
 |F_a(x,y)|=1,\qquad
 F_a(x,y)=x^3-(a-1)x^2y-(a+2)xy^2-y^3,
\]

are exactly the six signed pairs

\[
 \pm(1,0),\quad\pm(0,1),\quad\pm(1,-1).
\]

This retains the accepted threshold $182$. The new parts are a finite
Taylor argument identifying the root bracket and a discrete determinant
argument replacing the logarithmic irrationality-exponent calculation.

#### A1. Finite binomial identities

For a rational number $\alpha$ define the finite polynomials

\[
 P_j^{\alpha}(Z)=\sum_{i=0}^j
   {j+\alpha\choose i}{2j-i\choose j}Z^i,
 \qquad Q_j^{\alpha}=P_j^{-\alpha}.
\]

The convention is that a coefficient outside $0\le i\le j$ is zero.
We need the following three identities, all in polynomial rings:

\[
 Q_j^{\alpha}(Z)\sum_{k=0}^{2j}{\alpha\choose k}Z^k
       -P_j^{\alpha}(Z)\equiv0\pmod{Z^{2j+1}}. \tag{A1}
\]
\[
 P_j^{\alpha}(Z)=(1+Z)^j
       Q_j^{\alpha}\!\left(-\frac Z{1+Z}\right). \tag{A2}
\]
\[
 P_{j+1}^{\alpha}(Z)
 =\frac{2j+1}{j+1}(2+Z)P_j^{\alpha}(Z)
   -\frac{j^2-\alpha^2}{j(j+1)}Z^2P_{j-1}^{\alpha}(Z)
       \quad(j\ge1). \tag{A3}
\]

Here (A2) is polynomial because the denominator in each term divides

$(1+Z)^j$. None of these statements invokes a power series.

Here are all-index proofs. Set

\[
 \mathcal L_{j,\alpha}Y
  =Z(1+Z)Y''+[-2j+(1-2j-\alpha)Z]Y'+j(j+\alpha)Y.
\]

The coefficient of $Z^i$ is

\[
 (i+1)(i-2j)c_{i+1}+(i-j)(i-j-\alpha)c_i.
\]

Substitution of the factorial formula for the coefficients proves

$\mathcal L_{j,\alpha}P_j^{\alpha}=0$.
Let $B(Z)=\sum_{k=0}^{2j}{\alpha\choose k}Z^k$ and

$D=(1+Z)B'-\alpha B$. The adjacent binomial identity shows that

$D=(2j-\alpha){\alpha\choose2j}Z^{2j}$.
Direct polynomial differentiation gives

\[
 \mathcal L_{j,\alpha}(BQ)
  =B\mathcal L_{j,-\alpha}Q+2ZDQ'+(ZD'-2jD)Q.
\]

For $Q=Q_j^{\alpha}$, its right side is divisible by $Z^{2j+1}$.
The constants of $BQ$ and $P_j^{\alpha}$ are equal. The displayed
coefficient recurrence, for $i=0,\ldots,2j-1$, then forces their
coefficients through degree $2j$ to be equal. This proves (A1).

For (A2), its coefficient of $Z^k$, divided by

${2j-k\choose j}$, is

\[
 \sum_{i=0}^k(-1)^i{j-\alpha\choose i}{2j-i\choose k-i}
       ={j+\alpha\choose k}. \tag{A4}
\]

To prove the finite identity (A4), more generally replace $j-\alpha$
by $u$ and $2j$ by $v$. In the truncated polynomial ring

$\mathbb Q[Z]/(Z^{k+1})$, the coefficient in question is that of

$(1+Z)^v(1-Z/(1+Z))^u=(1+Z)^{v-u}$.
For nonnegative integers $u,v$ with $v\ge u$, the equality follows
from the ordinary finite binomial theorem, with only terms through
degree $k$ retained. Both sides of the coefficient identity are
polynomials of degree at most $k$ in each of $u,v$. It suffices to
use the finite grid $u=0,\ldots,k$ and $v=k,\ldots,2k$.
For each fixed $u$, the difference has $k+1$ distinct roots as a
polynomial in $v$ and is zero. Its coefficients, as polynomials in
$u$, then also have $k+1$ roots and are zero. This proves the identity
for all rational $u,v$.
This proves (A4) and (A2) without infinite expansions.

For (A3), write

\[
 c_{j,i}={j+\alpha\choose i}{2j-i\choose j}.
\]

For $0\le i\le j$, divide its coefficient identity by $c_{j,i}$
(first use indeterminate $\alpha$, so no division by a zero occurs).
The required identity becomes

\[
\begin{split}
\frac{(j+1+\alpha)(2j+2-i)(2j+1-i)}
 {(j+1+\alpha-i)(j+1)(j+1-i)}
={}&\frac{2j+1}{j+1}
 \left(2+\frac{i(2j-i+1)}{(j+\alpha-i+1)(j-i+1)}\right)\\
&-\frac{j^2-\alpha^2}{j(j+1)}
 \frac{i(i-1)j}{(j+\alpha)(j+\alpha-i+1)(j-i+1)}.
\end{split}
\]

Clearing denominators and expanding proves this identity. The coefficient
of degree $j+1$ is the identity

\[
 {j+1+\alpha\choose j+1}
 =\frac{2j+1}{j+1}{j+\alpha\choose j}
 -\frac{j^2-\alpha^2}{j(j+1)}{j-1+\alpha\choose j-1},
\]

which follows by canceling factorials. This proves (A3).

#### A2. Integer approximants and their recurrence

Set

\[
 \omega^2+\omega+1=0,\quad \Im\omega>0,\quad s=1+2\omega,
 \quad U=a+2+3\omega,\quad V=\overline U,
\]

\[
 L=2a+1=U+V,\qquad R=|U|=\sqrt{a^2+a+7},\qquad
 z=V/U-1,
 \qquad\delta=|z|=3\sqrt3/R.
\]

In particular $(V-U)^2=-27$ and $R^2\delta^2=27$.
Use $P_j=P_j^{1/3}$, $Q_j=P_j^{-1/3}$, and define

\[
 A_j=U^jP_j(z)=u_j+v_j\omega,
 \qquad p_j=v_j,\qquad q_j=u_j-v_j.
\]

These are integers. Indeed the $i$-th term is an integer factor

${2j-i\choose j}U^{j-i}$ times

\[
 \frac{\prod_{h=0}^{i-1}(3j+1-3h)}{i!}(-s)^i.
\]

For every ordinary prime $\ell\ne3$, each block of
$\ell^e$ consecutive factors in the numerator contains a multiple
of $\ell^e$. Choose an integer $E$ with $\ell^E>i$.
Its valuation is therefore at least

$\sum_{e=1}^{E}\lfloor i/\ell^e\rfloor=v_\ell(i!)$.
The uncanceled denominator is at worst

$3^{v_3(i!)}$. Since $s^2=-3$ and

$2v_3(i!)\le i$, it is canceled by $s^i$. For the last inequality,
take $E$ with $3^E>i$ and sum only the finite geometric progression:
$v_3(i!)=\sum_{e=1}^{E}\lfloor i/3^e\rfloor
\le i(1-3^{-E})/2<i/2$ when $i>0$.
This proves $A_j\in\mathbb Z[\omega]$.

Identity (A2) and $1+z=V/U$ imply

\[
 U^jQ_j(z)=\overline{A_j}. \tag{A5}
\]

Identity (A3) gives the exact recurrence, simultaneously for $p,q$,

\[
 Z_{j+1}=c_jLZ_j+d_jZ_{j-1},\quad
 c_j=\frac{2j+1}{j+1},\quad
 d_j=\frac{3(9j^2-1)}{j(j+1)}, \tag{A6}
\]

with

\[
 (p_0,q_0)=(0,1),\qquad(p_1,q_1)=(-2,L+1).
\]

Although the recurrence coefficients are rational, integrality was proved
directly, not assumed from this recurrence.

#### A3. A real-parameter cube root without a complex-analytic branch

Since $a\ge182$,

\[
 R>a,\qquad \delta<6/a<1/30. \tag{A7}
\]

For $0\le t\le1$, put $1+tz=x(t)+iy(t)$. Its real part is
positive. Define the positive real numbers

\[
 r(t)=\sqrt{x(t)^2+y(t)^2},\qquad u(t)=r(t)^{2/3}.
\]

Positive square and cube roots exist and are unique by IVT and monotonicity;
their continuity follows from the difference-of-powers identity. There
is a unique real $A(t)\in[\sqrt{3u(t)}/2,\sqrt{u(t)}]$ such that

\[
 4A(t)^3-3u(t)A(t)=x(t). \tag{A8}
\]

The cubic takes the values $0,r(t)$ at the two endpoints, while

$0<x(t)\le r(t)$. It is strictly increasing there because its
difference quotient is $4(A^2+AB+B^2)-3u\ge6u>0$.
Set

\[
 B(t)=\frac{y(t)}{4A(t)^2-u(t)},\qquad h(t)=A(t)+iB(t).
\]

The identity

\[
 u^3-A^2(4A^2-3u)^2=(u-A^2)(4A^2-u)^2
\]

shows $B^2=u-A^2$. Consequently $h(t)^3=1+tz$, $h(0)=1$,

$\Re h>0$, and $\Re(h^2)\ge u/2>0$. Hence

\[
 |h(t)-1|=\frac{|tz|}{|h(t)^2+h(t)+1|}\le|tz|\le\delta. \tag{A9}
\]

For completeness, continuity does not require an implicit-function
theorem. For nearby $t,t'$, let $u_{\min},u_{\max}$ be the smaller
and larger of the two $u$'s. They satisfy $u_{\max}<2u_{\min}$.
Subtracting the two equations (A8), with one common coefficient $u(t)$,
gives a coefficient of $A(t)-A(t')$ at least

$9u_{\min}-3u_{\max}>3u_{\min}$.
The other side is bounded by

$|x(t)-x(t')|+3|u(t)-u(t')|A(t')$, which tends to zero.
Thus $A$, then $B$ and $h$, are continuous.

The algebraic identity

\[
 \frac{h(t+v)-h(t)}v
   =\frac z{h(t+v)^2+h(t+v)h(t)+h(t)^2}
\]

now proves ordinary real differentiability, coordinate by coordinate,
and $h'=z/(3h^2)$. The same construction works on the real
neighborhood $(-1,2)$ of $[0,1]$, since $\Re(1+tz)>1-2\delta>0$
there. Repeated real differentiation gives

\[
 h^{(k)}(t)=k!{1/3\choose k}z^k
     \frac{h(t)}{(1+tz)^k}. \tag{A10}
\]

Also, since $V/U$ has absolute value $1$,
\[
 |1+tz|=|(1-t)+t(V/U)|\le(1-t)+t=1\quad(0\le t\le1),
\]
so $|h(t)|\le1$. For $k\ge1$, the elementary product definition gives

$|{1/3\choose k}|\le1$, so

\[
 |h^{(k)}(t)|/k!\le
       \bigl(\delta/(1-\delta)\bigr)^k. \tag{A11}
\]

This is a finite derivative identity. It makes no statement about an
infinite Taylor expansion.

#### A4. Finite Taylor remainders locate the correct root

The following finite form of Taylor's theorem follows from Rolle alone:
if a real function has $N+1$ continuous derivatives on an interval
containing $[0,1]$, then its value at $1$ differs from its degree

$N$ Taylor polynomial at $0$ by $f^{(N+1)}(\xi)/(N+1)!$
for some $0<\xi<1$. Subtract the Taylor polynomial and a multiple
of $t^{N+1}$ making the difference vanish at $1$; the difference
already vanishes to order $N+1$ at $0$. Apply Rolle $N+1$ times.
Apply this theorem separately to the two real coordinates of $h$,
using (A11), with $N=2j$. It gives

\[
 h(1)=\sum_{k=0}^{2j}{1/3\choose k}z^k+E_j,
 \quad |E_j|\le2\bigl(\delta/(1-\delta)\bigr)^{2j+1}. \tag{A12}
\]

The coefficients of $Q_j$ are nonnegative and satisfy

\[
 0\le[Z^i]Q_j\le {j\choose i}2^{2j-i},\qquad
 \sum_i[Z^i]Q_j\le6^j,\qquad
 |Q_j(z)|\le4^j(1+\delta)^j.
\]

Use (A1), (A12), and the finite geometric-sum inequality to obtain

\[
 |h(1)Q_j(z)-P_j(z)|\le\frac\delta{1-\delta}
 \left\{(6\delta^2)^j+
 2\left(\frac{4\delta^2(1+\delta)}{(1-\delta)^2}\right)^j\right\}.
 \tag{A13}
\]

For the first term, after (A1) removes degrees at most $2j$, fix
the coefficient index $i$ of $Q_j$. The remaining finite sum has
absolute value at most

$\delta^{2j+1}/(1-\delta)$; sum this over $i$, with its
coefficient bound $6^j$. The second term is the product of the
last bound for $Q_j$ and the remainder in (A12). This proves (A13).

The following rational inequalities are exact:

\[
 6R\delta^2=162/R<81/91<9/10,
\]

\[
 \frac{4R\delta^2(1+\delta)}{(1-\delta)^2}
 <\frac{54}{91}\frac{930}{841}
 =\frac{50220}{76531}<9/10.
\]

Also $3\delta/(1-\delta)<1$. Consequently (A13) implies

\[
 R^j|h(1)Q_j(z)-P_j(z)|<(9/10)^j. \tag{A14}
\]

Let $f_a(X)=F_a(X,1)$. Its positive root $\theta$ lies in

$(a,a+1)$: $f_a(a)=-2a-1<0$ and

$f_a(a+1)=a^2+a-1>0$. The exact substitution

$X\mapsto-1/(X+1)$ supplies the other two roots

\[
 \beta=-1/(\theta+1),\qquad
 \theta_2=-(\theta+1)/\theta.
\]

They lie in disjoint real intervals, so these are all three roots.
Direct expansion gives

\[
 U(X-\omega)^3-V(X-\omega^2)^3=3s f_a(X).
\]

Thus

\[
 h_\beta=\omega\frac{\beta-\omega}{\beta-\omega^2}
 \quad\hbox{satisfies}\quad h_\beta^3=V/U.
\]

Moreover $h_\beta-1=\beta(\omega-1)/(\beta-\omega^2)$,
and hence $|h_\beta-1|<2/(a+1)<1/3$, since

$|\beta-\omega^2|\ge\sqrt3/2$.
By (A9), $h(1)$ is within $1/30$ of $1$. The other two
cube roots, $\omega h(1),\omega^2h(1)$, have distance at least

$\sqrt3-1/30>1$ from $1$. The factorization of

$X^3-V/U$ proves these are the only three roots. Therefore

$h_\beta=h(1)$.

Finally, substitution of $A_j=u_j+v_j\omega$ gives the exact identity

\[
 q_j\beta-p_j=-\frac{(\omega^2-1)(\beta-\omega^2)}3
            (A_j-h_\beta\overline{A_j}).
\]

The prefactor has modulus less than $1$, because

$-1<\beta<0$. Equations (A5) and (A14) prove the weak but
fully finite approximation bound

\[
 |q_j\beta-p_j|<(9/10)^j. \tag{A15}
\]

#### A5. Positive recurrence gives sharp brackets, without a limit theorem

Put

\[
 \lambda=L+\sqrt{L^2+27},\qquad\lambda^2=2L\lambda+27.
\]

All $q_j$ are positive, and finite induction using (A6) proves

\[
 \lambda^j/(j+1)\le q_j\le\lambda^j. \tag{A16}
\]

For the upper bound use $c_j<2,d_j<27$. For the lower bound use

$q_1=L+1\ge\lambda/2$ and the positive differences

\[
 \frac{c_j}{j+1}-\frac2{j+2}
   =\frac j{(j+1)^2(j+2)},\qquad
 \frac{d_j}j-\frac{27}{j+2}
   =\frac{27j^2-3j-6}{j^2(j+1)(j+2)}.
\]

The adjacent determinants satisfy

\[
 \Delta_j=p_jq_{j+1}-p_{j+1}q_j,\quad
 \Delta_0=2,\quad \Delta_j=-d_j\Delta_{j-1},
\]

and therefore

\[
 0<|\Delta_j|=\frac{2\,27^j}{j+1}
     \prod_{h=1}^j\left(1-\frac1{9h^2}\right)
 \le\frac{2\,27^j}{j+1}. \tag{A17}
\]

The recurrence expresses $p_{j+2}/q_{j+2}$ as a strictly positive
weighted average of $p_j/q_j$ and $p_{j+1}/q_{j+1}$. Thus the
intervals $I_j$ with these two endpoints are nested. For any fixed

$j$, if $\beta$ lay outside $I_j$, its distance $d>0$
from $I_j$ would contradict (A15) at a sufficiently large *finite*

$N\ge j$. Such an $N$ exists because

$(10/9)^N\ge1+N/9$, by the finite binomial theorem; choose

$1+N/9>1/d$. Since $q_N\ge1$, (A15) then gives

$|\beta-p_N/q_N|<d$, although $p_N/q_N\in I_j$.
This is not an invocation of convergence of a series or a continued
fraction.

The rational-root test proves $\beta$ irrational: the only possible
rational roots of the monic cubic are $\pm1$, neither of which is
a root when $a\ge182$. Thus $\beta$ lies strictly inside every

$I_j$. Equations (A16)--(A17) now imply the sharp bound

\[
 |q_j\beta-p_j|<|\Delta_j|/q_{j+1}
       \le\frac4\lambda\left(\frac{27}{\lambda}\right)^j.
 \tag{A18}
\]

#### A6. The finite cone and the small-denominator bound

The integral map $R(x,y)=(y,-x-y)$ satisfies

$R^3=1$ and $F_a\circ R=F_a$. Its six signed powers put every
nonzero pair in

\[
 Y>0,\qquad-Y/2<X\le Y. \tag{A19}
\]

To see this explicitly, among $x,y,-x-y$ choose one with maximum
absolute value and make it the positive second coordinate $Y$, using
a signed cyclic permutation. The other two coordinates are nonpositive:
otherwise their sum being $-Y$ would make the remaining coordinate
have absolute value greater than $Y$. If the first coordinate is at
most $-Y/2$, the additional map $-R^2$ gives

$(X',Y')=(X+Y,-X)$ with $0\le X'\le Y'$.

In this cone, $X\ge0$ gives only $(0,1)$ when $|F_a|=1$.
Indeed $1\le X\le Y$ gives

$X^3-(a-1)X^2Y\le0$, and the remaining terms give $F_a<-1$.
If $X=-u<0$, then $1\le u<Y/2$. The polynomial

$F_a(-u,Y)$ is strictly increasing for $0\le u\le Y/2$, since
for $v<u$ its difference quotient is at least $9Y^2/4$.
For $3\le Y\le a+1$,

\[
 F_a(-1,Y)=-1+Y\{(Y-1)(a+1-Y)+2\}>1.
\]

Therefore every nontrivial solution has

\[
 Y\ge a+2. \tag{A20}
\]

On $[-1/2,0]$, the difference quotient of $f_a$ is at most

$-9/4$. Since

$f_a(-2/a)=1+4/a^2-8/a^3>1$, any solution has

$-2/a<X/Y<0$. The distances of this interval from the roots

$\theta>a$ and $\theta_2<-1$ have product greater than

$a(1-2/a)=a-2$. Factoring the cubic therefore proves

\[
 0<|\beta-X/Y|<\frac1{(a-2)Y^3}. \tag{A21}
\]

#### A7. A logarithm-free determinant contradiction

Let $E=\lambda/27>1$. Choose the least nonnegative integer $j$
such that

\[
 E^j\ge8Y/\lambda. \tag{A22}
\]

It exists by the finite binomial inequality

$(1+(E-1))^j\ge1+j(E-1)$. Since

$Y\ge a+2$ and $\lambda<4a+3$, we have $j\ge1$.
By (A18), both indices $j,j+1$ have raw errors less than $1/(2Y)$.
Their determinant is nonzero, so at least one of

$p_jY-q_jX$, $p_{j+1}Y-q_{j+1}X$ is a nonzero integer.
For that index $k$,

\[
 1\le|p_kY-q_kX|
 <\tfrac12+q_kY|\beta-X/Y|.
\]

Together with (A16) and (A21), this forces

\[
 (a-2)Y^2<2\lambda^{j+1}. \tag{A23}
\]

Minimality in (A22) gives

$Y>(\lambda/8)E^{j-1}$. Consequently

\[
 \frac{(a-2)Y^2}{2\lambda^{j+1}}
 >\frac{a-2}{128}
       \left(\frac\lambda{729}\right)^{j-1}>1,
\]

because $a-2\ge180>128$ and

$\lambda>4a+2\ge730>729$. This contradicts (A23) and proves
Theorem A.

**What this closes.** It closes the infinite-series dependency in the
accepted large-parameter Thomas evaluation. It also supplies a concrete
finite alternative to invoking an irrationality exponent expressed by
logarithms. It does not solve an arbitrary logarithmic tail or general
Thue equation.

#### A8. A reusable finite recurrence separator

The final argument has a useful abstract form. Suppose a real irrational
number $\beta$ has integer pairs $(p_j,q_j)$, $q_j>0$, with
nonzero adjacent determinants and

\[
 q_j\le Q_0 A^j,\qquad |q_j\beta-p_j|<C(D/A)^j,
 \qquad A>\max(1,D),\quad D,C,Q_0>0.
\]

If a candidate rational point $X/Y$, $Y\ge1$, and a constant
$K>0$ satisfy

\[
 0<|\beta-X/Y|<1/(K Y^3),
\]

choose the least $j\ge0$ with $(A/D)^j\ge2CY$. If $j\ge1$,
the same determinant argument gives a contradiction whenever

\[
 \frac K{8Q_0 C^2 A^2}
     \left(\frac A{D^2}\right)^{j-1}\ge1.
\]

Indeed minimality gives $Y>(2C)^{-1}(A/D)^{j-1}$, and

$KY^2/(2Q_0A^{j+1})>K(8Q_0C^2A^2)^{-1}(A/D^2)^{j-1}$.
Thus a strict score $A>D^2$ is enough to reduce candidates to
finitely many $j$, by ordinary integer power comparisons. No real
logarithm is needed to compute a cutoff. The small $j=0$ case has

$Y\le1/(2C)$ and is a finite box.

This criterion tells the certificate compiler exactly what to search
for: integer approximants, a positive recurrence or an explicit bracket,
and an effective base $D$ with $A>D^2$. Merely exhibiting a
covariant or a transport is not enough.

### B. PROVED: the parameter transport carries an exact denominator debt

For arbitrary integers $a,p,q$, define

\[
 m=F_a(p,q),\quad Q=p^2+pq+q^2,\quad P=pq(p+q),\quad
 D_a=a^2+a+7,\quad B=am+D_aP.
\]

Let

\[
 M_{p,q}=\begin{pmatrix}p&-q\\q&p+q\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Then the following polynomial identities hold:

\[
 \det M_{p,q}=Q.
\]
\[
 F_a(M_{p,q}(X,Y))
 =m(X^3+X^2Y-2XY^2-Y^3)-BXY(X+Y). \tag{B1}
\]
\[
 B^2+Bm+7m^2=D_a Q^3. \tag{B2}
\]

Identity (B1) follows by expansion. An informative factorized proof of
(B2) uses

\[

##### C2.1. A carry lemma for every prime power

Fix an ordinary prime \(p\ne3\) and \(q=p^e\). Let
\[
a=j\bmod q,\qquad c=3^{-1}\bmod q,\quad 1\le c<q,\qquad
b=(a+c)\bmod q.
\]
For \(t=i\bmod q\), the contribution at the level \(q\) to \(v_p(T_{j,i})\) is
\[
d_q(i)=1_{\{t>b\}}+
\left\lfloor\frac{2j-i}{q}\right\rfloor
-\left\lfloor\frac j q\right\rfloor
-\left\lfloor\frac{j-i}{q}\right\rfloor. \tag{G3}
\]
The second summand is a carry, so it is either zero or one.

**Proof.** Among the \(i\) numerator factors \(3j+1-3h\), the ones divisible by \(q\) have
\(h\equiv j+c\equiv b\pmod q\). Writing \(i=Aq+t\), their number is \(A+1_{\{t>b\}}\). Subtracting the factorial contribution \(\lfloor i/q\rfloor=A\) leaves the first term of (G3). The three other floors are exactly the binomial coefficient contribution. Summing (G3) over the finitely many \(e\) gives the complete \(p\)-valuation. ∎

The minimum of this single-level contribution over \(0\le i\le j\) is
\[
\boxed{\min_{0\le i\le j}d_q(i)
=\mu_q(j):=1_{\{a\ge\max(c,q-c)\}}.} \tag{G4}
\]

Here is the finite case check. If \(2a<q\), choose \(i=0\); both terms of (G3) are zero. If \(2a\ge q\), the carry is zero precisely on the residue interval
\[
2a-q<t\le a
\]
and is one outside that interval. All the residues in that interval occur among \(i=0,\ldots,j\), since its upper endpoint is \(a\le j\). The sum in (G3) is at least one on the entire interval exactly when \(b\le2a-q\). This is equivalent to \(a+c\ge q\) and \(c\le a\), namely \(a\ge\max(c,q-c)\). If the condition fails, the first residue \(t=2a-q+1\) lies at most \(b\) and gives zero. If it holds, every residue gives at least one, and \(i=0\) gives exactly one. This proves (G4), including the case \(q>j\).

The inverse of three is explicit:
\[
c=
\begin{cases}
(2q+1)/3,&q\equiv1\pmod3,\\
(q+1)/3,&q\equiv2\pmod3.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus
\[
\mu_q(j)=
\begin{cases}
1_{\{j\bmod q\ge(2q+1)/3\}},&q\equiv1\pmod3,\\
1_{\{j\bmod q\ge(2q-1)/3\}},&q\equiv2\pmod3.
\end{cases} \tag{G5}

\ge\sum_e\mu_{p^e}(j).

Taking the minimum over \(i\) proves (G7). This uses the valid inequality

The possible extra content beyond \(\mathcal D_j\) is confined to small primes. For \(j\ge3\), (G8), the divisibility \(g_j\mid\binom{2j}{j}\), and the carry formula for that central binomial coefficient give
\[
1\le\frac{g_j}{\mathcal D_j}
\le
\prod_{p\le\sqrt{3j+1}}
p^{k_p}
\le(2j)^{\lfloor\sqrt{3j+1}\rfloor}. \tag{G10}
\]
The exponent \(k_p\) is the number of powers \(p,p^2,\ldots\) not exceeding \(2j\); no logarithmic estimate is used. Each factorial carry contributes at most one at each of those levels. Formula (G10) is an exact finite inequality, not a claimed asymptotic formula.

##### C2.3. What remains in the prime-product route

A sufficient new target is the entirely explicit prime-product inequality
\[
\boxed{\mathcal D_j\ge\tfrac12(3/2)^j
\quad\text{for all sufficiently large }j,} \tag{G11}
\]
with an explicit threshold and finite verification below it. A weaker divisor bound with a slightly smaller base still works if it satisfies the strict rational score in §5.C1. An estimate for \(\mathcal D_j\) is sufficient, not necessary: extra small-prime content in \(g_j\) could improve the finite exceptional range.

The script [P15_content.py](../certificates/P15_content.py) verifies these carry, divisor and large-prime formulas through index 500 by default; `--extended` checks through index 1000. It also checks Target C over that specified finite range. No proof of (G11) is supplied here. It cannot be inferred from exact contents through \(j=1000\), or from the fact that the intervals in (G9) have positive lengths.

The naive use of separate Chebyshev lower and upper bounds is quantitatively inadequate. For example, even granting bounds with linear coefficients \(0.92\) and \(1.11\), the first interval gives only
\[
0.92\cdot\frac32-1.11=0.27
\]
as its coefficient of \(j\) in the logarithm of the guaranteed prime product, before endpoint errors. Every later individual interval gives no positive lower coefficient by that same subtraction: its endpoint ratio is at most \(6/5\), and \(0.92(6/5)<1.11\). Thus that calculation does not establish the required base \(3/2\). This observation is conditional arithmetic about those proposed bounds; it does not invoke an unproved prime-distribution statement.

The outstanding arithmetic task is therefore a sufficiently strong **explicit finite lower bound for the product over the union of intervals (G9)**, or another direct all-index divisor argument. The carry theorem removes cancellation ambiguity and determines exactly which large primes must occur. It does not replace the missing lower bound on their total product.

## 6. What can close the cancellation wall, and what cannot do so by itself

### 6.1. Assessment and the recommended attack

**BELIEF, with a concrete mathematical reason:** the best immediate experiment is the \(a=7\) common-content target in §5.C1. Its input sequence is explicit, its content has an exact factorial/divisibility formula, the required gain is modest, and the entire conditional finite continued-fraction exhaustion has already been performed. A proof of Target C is the one missing input to that particular route. This is a finite arithmetic problem about a gcd sequence, rather than a request to reconstruct a logarithmic lower-bound theory.

The S-003 direction also has useful new structure: its ambient unit coverage is now proved by an integer descent. To finish the Thue equation, one needs a new mechanism that also controls the transported mark, or a passing recurrence-based approximant separator. The trace descent specifies both a successful coverage rank and the precise information it leaves uncontrolled. A coupled Padé construction remains admissible if its integrality/content, determinant and error are proved by finite identities and inequalities. A representation by an integral is a discovery tool until that dependency has been replaced or explicitly approved as borderline.

There is no proof here that a suitable marked descent or approximant must exist for S-003. In particular, the norm-one tail constant is genuinely nonintegral: the script rechecks that its four conjugates have polynomial
\[
61X^4-772X^3+1934X^2-1028X+61
\]
and trace \(772/61\). The four conjugates are distinct, so the degree is four. An algebraic integer of degree four has integral trace, by its monic integer polynomial. Thus this constant cannot be absorbed into a product of algebraic-integer units. A different cover may change the presentation of this debt; it must prove the corresponding transport and integrality statements.

### 6.2. A fixed finite congruence sieve has an unavoidable periodic remainder

**PROVED finite-sieve obstruction.** Let \(r\ge1\) commuting invertible moves act on integer states, and suppose that modulo each of finitely many specified moduli they induce permutations of a finite state set. Any test depending only on these finitely many reduced states is periodic on the exponent lattice \(\mathbb Z^r\). If it accepts one exponent vector, it accepts infinitely many vectors.

**Proof.** Each reduced move is a permutation of a finite set and has a finite order. Let \(L\) be a common multiple of the orders of all the moves on all the specified state sets. Adding any vector in \(L\mathbb Z^r\) to an exponent vector changes none of its reduced states. Thus every accepted vector comes with its whole coset modulo \(L\mathbb Z^r\). Such a coset is infinite when \(r\ge1\). ∎

This is a statement about the scope of a **fixed finite list of congruences**. The finite all-precision valuation arguments of §3 are stronger: their induction distinguishes every nonzero index from a known index at a sufficiently high, explicitly determined precision. A successful adaptive multi-prime argument likewise needs a proved progress statement, a finite recursive cover, or a height/descent relation. Pigeonhole alone usually supplies a return; it does not turn the return into exclusion.

The obstruction is especially relevant after a long sieve finds only the known residue classes. Those classes still contain infinitely many integer indices. A local exact isolation certificate can remove the excess on a genuinely covered class, but its hypotheses and coverage must be present.

### 6.3. Why the quadratic Vieta model does not transfer automatically

For a monic quadratic with integer coefficients, knowing one integer root makes the other root integral by their integer sum. For a cubic, factoring out a known integer root leaves a quadratic whose discriminant need not be a square. Thus the familiar Vieta involution is not an automatic operation on a Thue solution set.

There is also a finite algebraic obstruction to obtaining a Pell-like infinite group from linear symmetries of a fixed form.

**PROVED.** Let \(F\) be an irreducible binary form over \(\mathbb Q\) of degree \(n\ge3\). The projective rational linear transformations preserving its zero set form a finite group of order at most \(n!\).

**Proof.** In characteristic zero, irreducibility implies that the roots are distinct: a repeated factor would divide both the one-variable polynomial and its nonzero formal derivative of smaller degree. A projective transformation preserving the zero set permutes its \(n\) roots. If a transformation fixes at least three distinct projective points, it is the identity. In an affine coordinate, a fixed point of \((ax+b)/(cx+d)\) satisfies
\[
cx^2+(d-a)x-b=0.
\]
Three distinct finite fixed points force all three coefficients to vanish; if one fixed point is infinity, the same conclusion follows from the resulting linear equation and the other two fixed points. Hence the permutation action has trivial kernel and embeds the group into the finite permutation group on the roots. ∎

For exact rational linear symmetries of \(|F|\), the additional scalar kernel is at most \(\{1,-1\}\). This does not prohibit a nonlinear descent or a change of equation. It explains why signed cyclic transformations of a Thomas form give a useful cone reduction but do not themselves supply the missing infinite descent. The parameter transport in §5.B is the appropriate sort of ambient move; its exact denominator \(Q\) identifies the extra debt that a further move must reduce.

### 6.4. Content and ledger invariants must be checked before proposing a descent

There are two different kinds of content in this answer.

The Padé content \(g_j\) in §5.C1 is removed from an integer approximant pair. This improves its two quantitative estimates simultaneously, and the nonzero adjacent determinant survives. The exact criterion is \(\lambda\gamma^3>D^2\) for a divisor of size at least \(\gamma^j/C_0\). It is a genuine saving when proved at the required indices.

By contrast, simply multiplying the S-003 plane ledger by an integral unit matrix does not create a decreasing determinantal content. Here is the relevant finite invariant. If \(B\) is an integer rank-\(k\) matrix and \(M\in\mathrm{GL}_d(\mathbb Z)\), the gcd of all \(k\)-rowed minors of \(BM\) equals that for \(B\). Finite Cauchy–Binet expresses every minor of \(BM\) as an integer linear combination of minors of \(B\), giving one divisibility. Apply the same argument to \(BM\) and \(M^{-1}\) for the reverse divisibility. Thus this gcd is an invariant, not a rank, for those moves.

Similarly, a cycle of unit multiplications that returns a nonzero ambient element \(v\) to itself has total multiplier \(1\), since \(uv=v\) in a field implies \(u=1\). The transported exact plane then also returns unchanged. A finite cycle graph on normalized units cannot secretly perform an additional reduction of the plane ledger.

These observations leave room for new ghost moves: a covariant cover, a change of norm equation, an allocation move with a verified inverse, or a nonlinear involution can change the relevant arithmetic situation. Such a proposal should print its forward identity, inverse integrality filters, mark transport and strictly decreasing nonnegative integer before a large search is treated as evidence of a descent theorem.
